Small wins: what 12,000 work diaries said about good days

Managers ranked “progress” last on a list of motivators. The diaries ranked it first by a mile.

Habits · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 1 September 2026

Ask a room of managers what motivates people at work and you’ll hear the usual answers: recognition, incentives, clear goals, support. Nobody says “getting a little bit of something done”. They should.

The diary study

Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer asked 238 people[1], in 26 creative teams at seven companies, to write a short diary at the end of every working day. They ended up with nearly 12,000 entries about what happened, how people felt and what they got done.

When they looked at people’s very best days, one thing stood out. In 76%[1] of those best days, people had made progress on work that mattered to them. Nothing else came close. Not praise, not pay, not a nice lunch.

The authors called it the progress principle. Even small steps forward lifted mood and motivation, and setbacks dragged them down more than you’d expect.

The bit managers got wrong

Here’s the punchline. When 669 leaders from around the world were asked to rank five motivators, they put progress last. Only 5% ranked it first. The thing that made the most difference was the thing almost nobody was looking for.

Why a tiny puzzle can help

We’re not going to claim a crossword fixes your job. The study was about meaningful work, and a puzzle is a puzzle. But there’s something in the shape of it. Some days the big project doesn’t budge, and a small, finished, correct thing still feels good. A green tick before breakfast is a reminder that things can get done today.

That’s why Ziggle is built around visible progress: a tick for each daily, a perfect-day banner when you finish all eight, a 12-week calendar that fills in, levels in the trainers, and coaching that shows how you improved.

Make your own progress visible

  1. End the day with a done list. Three lines: what moved forward today. It takes a minute.
  2. Shrink the next step until it’s obvious. “Write the report” has no progress in it. “Write the first heading” does.
  3. Count small things. One email sent, one page read, one Letter Lock cracked. They all count.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the diaries were

The Harvard Business School interview with Teresa Amabile describes the study’s design. The researchers collected confidential accounts from 238 white-collar employees at seven companies[2] in a variety of industries. Each person kept a daily diary for the length of a project, sometimes as long as nine months, answering open questions such as “Briefly describe one event from today that stands out in your mind.” The result was about 12,000 daily diaries; the Wikipedia article on Amabile describes the work as a 15-year research programme[3]. They were coded for what the authors call inner work life, which the book defines as the mix of perceptions, emotions and motivations a person has as they react to the events of the workday.

  • 238employees at seven companies
  • ~12,000daily diary entries
  • up to 9 monthslength of a single project diary

The pattern: of all the events that marked the best days, by far the most prominent was making progress; of all those that marked the worst, by far the most prominent was a setback, the feeling of losing ground. Money barely registered in the diaries. Salaries and bonuses, the interview says, hardly appeared.

Setbacks weigh more

The interview records an uncomfortable result: the effect of setbacks was two to three times stronger than the effect of progress. It is a common finding in psychology that bad events grip attention and feeling more than good ones. So the second half of the principle is the warning. Avoiding setbacks matters at least as much as cheering progress, and one lost afternoon can undo several small gains.

The 28% is the good news in the study. Big breakthroughs are rare, but more than a quarter of small events of all kinds had a major effect on how people felt, which means small steps are common enough to count on. Amabile’s conclusion: if the work is divided into manageable pieces, small wins are things people can experience fairly regularly.

What lets progress happen

The diaries were also coded for the things that helped or hindered progress. The authors call events that help a project move forward catalysts and interpersonal events that lift people’s spirits nourishers; their opposites are inhibitors and toxins. The book names seven catalysts and four nourishers.

Two items on the list are worth reading against puzzles. “Clear goals” that can be reached in a realistic time frame is how a daily puzzle is built: the goal is fixed, visible and finishable. And “learning from problems and successes” is Amabile’s way of turning a setback into progress, since someone who asks what went wrong and why has learned something, and learning is a step forward. Saying what happened and offering a way on is the job of the end-of-level popup when a level is lost.

What managers said, and what McKinsey found

The book excerpt in the interview says that when the authors asked dozens of managers, individually and in groups, for their main levers for motivating employees, progress was almost never mentioned; they listed recognition, tangible incentives and clear goals. They wondered if it was too obvious to say, so they ran the formal survey of 669 managers with progress included in the list. It came last. The excerpt adds that a 2009 McKinsey survey on motivating people at work told the same story: progress was completely absent from the results.

What the study cannot tell you

It is a study of people at work on projects, mostly in creative or knowledge jobs, reporting on their own days. A diary tells you what people say about their best and worst days, not what caused them, and the interview is written from the authors’ side. Nothing in it says a puzzle has the same effect. Our reading is narrower: the design principles that the study finds help work, a clear goal, a finishable step and a visible record, are the same ones a daily set is built from, and a tick on a calendar is a small, honest piece of progress. We do not claim more.

The twist

The managers who were surveyed were not unkind or careless: they were asked directly, with progress written in the list, and still put it last. The authors’ own explanation is that it is not obvious. Progress is what the diaries show lifting people most, and what the managers least expected to matter.
