What chess masters see that you don’t (yet)

Masters remembered real chess positions far better than novices. Scramble the pieces at random and the gap shrank. That’s chunking.

Show a chess master a game position for a few seconds, take it away, and ask them to rebuild it. They’ll put most of the pieces back exactly. Ask a beginner and you’ll get a few pieces and a shrug.

So masters must have amazing memories, right? That’s what it looks like. Then William Chase and Herbert Simon ran the obvious follow-up in 1973.

The random board

They showed players boards with the pieces placed at random, in positions that would never happen in a real game. Suddenly the masters’ advantage shrank considerably. They weren’t remembering every piece separately. They were remembering a handful of meaningful groups: a castled king, a pawn chain, a familiar attack. Take away the meaning and they were nearly as stuck as anyone.

Psychologists call those groups chunks. A beginner sees pieces. A master sees patterns, and a pattern only takes one slot in memory.

Seven, give or take

The idea goes back to a famous 1956 paper by George Miller, “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two”. Miller noticed that short-term memory held roughly seven items, and that the number stayed about the same whether the items were digits, letters or words. The trick is that a chunk can be big. “F-B-I-C-I-A” is six letters, but if you know them it’s two chunks. (Miller himself admitted nobody was quite sure what a chunk was. We find that very relatable.)

You’re building chunks right now

Every puzzle you play regularly is quietly teaching you its chunks.

  • Sudoku: after a few weeks, you stop checking cells one by one and start seeing “this box only has room for a 7 in the top row”.
  • Word search: you stop reading letters and start spotting shapes: “ING”, “TH”, double letters.
  • Arrow escape: you learn to see clusters of arrows that clear together, not single arrows.
  • Typing: fast typists type common pairs like “th” and “ing” as one movement. Level 8 of the trainer practises exactly that.

How to grow chunks faster

  1. Play the same kind of puzzle regularly. Chunks need repetition.
  2. Name the patterns. When you spot one, say it in your head (“that’s a pair”). Names make chunks easier to find again.
  3. Review your mistakes. The pattern you missed is the next chunk to learn.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What de Groot’s masters did

The experiment the post begins with is older than Chase and Simon. Adriaan de Groot, a Dutch chess master[6] who played for the Netherlands at the 1937 and 1939 Olympiads and wrote his thesis on chess thinking in 1946, showed players a position for a very short time, between 2 and 10 seconds, and asked them to rebuild it. Simon and Chase describe the results in[3] American Scientist. The positions came from real master games the players did not know, with about 25 pieces on the board.

The gradient is steep. Masters were nearly perfect, while a sharp drop separated masters from experts, and good amateurs (Class A) placed about half. Then Chase and Simon built random positions from the same pieces. Under the same conditions, all players, from master to novice, recalled only about three or four pieces, “significantly more poorly” than novices did with real positions. The post says the masters’ advantage shrank considerably. In the account Simon and Chase wrote for American Scientist, every player lands at the same three or four pieces, so the advantage is gone.

That matters for what chunking explains. The authors conclude that the masters’ superior performance cannot be put down to any general superiority in visual imagery. The skill is specific to chess.

Counting the chunks

The article also reports chunk counts for a master and a Class A player. The theory it was testing predicted that the number of chunks would be much the same for strong and weak players, and that only their size would differ by a factor of two or three. The data fitted only partly. Comparing a master with a Class A player, the master recalled about twice as many pieces, with chunks about 50% larger and about 50% more of them: the first chunk averaged 3.8 pieces against 2.6, and a position broke into 7.7 chunks against 5.7. Both counts are not far from Miller’s seven.

How many chunks, and how long?

The authors estimate that matching a master needs something of the order of 50,000 stored patterns, a vocabulary comparable to the 50,000 words that highly literate adults can read. They give two rough checks. No one had reached grandmaster level, including Bobby Fischer, with less than about a decade’s intense preoccupation with the game, and a master has probably spent 10,000 to 50,000 hours looking at chess positions, a Class A player 1,000 to 5,000. They also take from a general theory (EPAM) that a new chunk takes about 5 seconds to learn.

Our arithmetic, not the authors’: 50,000 chunks at 5 seconds each is 250,000 seconds, or about 69 hours. The difference between that and the thousands of hours is the time it takes to meet 50,000 different patterns often enough for them to be there. The slow part is the experience, not the filing.

What the eyes do

Simon and Chase describe a Russian experiment in which an expert chose a move while his eye movements were recorded for the first five seconds. The movements did not fit the idea that he was searching a tree of moves. De Groot’s own account of choosing a move has four stages, orientation, exploration, investigation and proof, and he found that much of what matters is settled in the first few seconds. The fovea, the sharp centre of vision, is only about one degree in radius, and the eye jumps from point to point in abrupt movements called saccades.

Miller’s seven, and what a chunk is

George Miller’s 1956 paper noticed two limits[4] that happened to sit close together. People can tell apart only about four to eight stimuli on one dimension, such as ten tones of different pitch, which he put at 2 to 3 bits. And a young adult’s memory span is about seven items, whether the items are binary digits (1 bit each), decimal digits (3.32 bits) or words (about 10 bits). Because the span did not shrink as items carried more information, Miller concluded it is counted in chunks, which are the largest meaningful units the person recognises, so what counts as one depends on what you know. A word is one chunk to a speaker and many to someone who does not know the language. Later work found the span differs with the material: about seven for digits, six for letters, five for words.

Wikipedia gives a phone-number example[5]: 9849523450 can be grouped as 98 495 234 50, so ten digits become four chunks. Wikipedia also describes a student known as SF, studied by Chase and Ericsson over two years, who started with an ordinary span of seven digits and grew it to 80 numbers, helped by turning strings of digits into race times, because he was a long-distance runner.

The twist

The same article that explains why chess masters remember so much also explains why it will not help with anything else. A master scrambles to three or four pieces on a random board, no better than the novice, and the authors stress the skill is chess-specific. Practice builds chunks for what you practise. Our reading: that is in step with the brain-training review described in the post on whether puzzles make you smarter.
