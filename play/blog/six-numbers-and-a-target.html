Six numbers and a target

A French TV show from 1965 gave the world its favourite arithmetic race. Sum Spark keeps the puzzle and drops the clock.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 20 February 2026

In 1965, French television launched Des chiffres et des lettres (Numbers and Letters), created by Armand Jammot. Its numbers round was simple to explain and hard to master: here are some numbers and a target; use them, once each, with + − × ÷, to get as close as you can.

When Channel 4 opened in Britain on 2 November 1982, the very first programme it broadcast was Countdown, the British version of the same show. Its numbers round gives contestants six numbers, a three-digit target and thirty seconds, with a ticking clock that became famous in its own right.

What makes it a good puzzle

There are usually many routes to the target, and the fun is in spotting one. Strong players tend to look for a big multiplication that lands near the target, then patch the gap with what is left.

What Sum Spark does differently

We liked the puzzle and wanted a calmer version of it.

  • No clock. Sum Spark is untimed. You can think for as long as you like.
  • Three rounds that build. The first round only allows adding, the second adding and taking away, and the third all four operations, so the day gets harder as you go.
  • Left to right. Your chain is worked out step by step as you build it, with a gauge showing how close you are.
  • Close still counts. Lock in a near miss for part of the points, and see one way there afterwards.

The server rebuilds each day’s rounds and replays every chain before a score joins the leaderboard, so a place on it means the sums really worked.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The French original

The Wikipedia article says Des chiffres et des lettres began in 1965 as Le mot le plus long (“the longest word”), with letters only, and reached its present form in 1972. It was created by Armand Jammot, ran for more than 12,000 episodes[2] and finished on 25 August 2024, after a 58-year run. In its numbers round, le compte est bon (“the count is right”), six numbers are chosen at random from 1 to 10 and 25, 50, 75 and 100, and the same number can come up more than once. A random three-digit target from 101 to 999 follows. The contestants have 40 seconds and a set of rules: only the four operations, only positive whole numbers at every step, no number used more often than it appears, and no need to use all six. The article’s own example: from 8, 4, 4, 6, 8 and 9, reach 594. One route is 8 + 8 = 16, 16 × 4 = 64, 6 − 4 = 2, 64 + 2 = 66, 66 × 9 = 594. A French computer program called Numbo was written in 1987 to model how people play the game.

The British version

Countdown was the first programme shown on Channel 4 when it began on 2 November 1982, and it is one of Britain’s longest-running game shows; the article counts 8,798 regular episodes as of 26 June 2026. Its host for 23 years was Richard Whiteley, until his death in 2005. The numbers round is a little different from the French one. The contestant chooses six tiles from 24 laid out face down: twenty small numbers (two each of 1 to 10) and four large ones[1] (25, 50, 75 and 100). They say how many large ones they want, from none to four, and the tiles are drawn at random. A machine called CECIL, short for Countdown’s Electronic Calculator In Leeds, produces a target from 100 to 999. There are 30 seconds. Division must be exact, and fractions and negative numbers are not allowed.

The scoring is also stricter. The contestant closer to the target scores: ten points for hitting it, seven for being within five, five for being within ten. Nothing is scored for being further away, or for calculations that are flawed or too slow. The article’s example: from 75, 50, 2, 3, 8 and 7 with a target of 812, one contestant reaches 813, which is worth seven points, while the assistant shows that 7 × 2 × (50 + 8) hits it exactly.

How many large numbers should you ask for?

The article says one large and five small is the most popular choice, that two large gives the best chance of an exact solution[1], and that none or four are generally considered the hardest. That is a claim we can test. The pattern matches the article’s: two large numbers is best (98.1%), but one is almost as good (97.8%), none is much the hardest at 83.3%, and four, at 90.3%, is the next hardest. Our reading: small numbers make many small totals but cannot reach the high end of the range, and four large numbers make big jumps but can miss the targets in between. Choosing one or two large numbers is not just habit, it is also what the arithmetic favours.

How many ways to draw the tiles?

Sum Spark keeps the arithmetic and drops the clock, with a different structure: three rounds, adding only, then adding and taking away, then all four operations. That builds the skill in steps.

The twist

The first programme ever shown on Channel 4 was a game of words and sums. And the French original’s numbers round was studied as a puzzle of the mind: in 1987 a computer program, Numbo, was written to copy how people play it.
