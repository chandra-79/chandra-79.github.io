Eight queens: four billion ways to try, 92 that work

A chess composer posed it in 1848. Counting the answers has been a favourite exercise ever since.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 23 July 2026

In this article 5 sections

Place eight queens on a chessboard so that none can capture another. Max Bezzel, a chess composer, published the puzzle in 1848, and Franz Nauck published the first solutions in 1850. Carl Friedrich Gauss is among the mathematicians who then took a turn.

Needles in a haystack

There are 4,426,165,368[1] ways to put eight queens on 64 squares. Only 92[1] of them are solutions, about one in 48 million. If you treat a solution and its rotations or reflections as the same, there are just 12 fundamental ones.

  • 4.4 billionways to place 8 queens
  • 92that work
  • 12that differ by more than a turn or a flip

Why the 6×6 board is the stingy one

Look at the row: 4×4 has 2 solutions, 5×5 has 10, and then the 6×6 board drops back to 4 before the counts climb again (40, 92, 352, …). Bigger is not smoothly easier; the geometry of diagonals makes some sizes meaner than their neighbours.

The twist

The puzzle’s fame rests on how little it asks of you and how much it asks of a program. It is the standard first exercise in “backtracking”: place a queen, and the moment you cannot place the next, step back one. The same step-back logic is how a Sudoku solver, or your own head, rules out a guess.

From four billion to 92 in three clever steps

Nobody solves eight queens by trying every placement, and the reduction from 4.4 billion to 92 is a lesson in itself. Each step removes a kind of obviously wasted work:

The last step takes more than brute force, but still only 40,320 candidates to check, which a computer does instantly. The trouble is how this grows: brute force is manageable at 8 and “intractable for n ≥ 20”[1], since 20! is about 2.4 × 1018. That is why counting solutions for bigger boards needs far cleverer methods.

Which boards have a solution?

All of them except two: solutions exist for every n apart from n = 2 and n = 3, which no arrangement can solve.

A star of computer science

In 1972 Edsger Dijkstra used the puzzle[1] to demonstrate what he called structured programming, the discipline of building a solution from small, checkable pieces. It is a classic exercise for teaching backtracking, the technique of trying a choice, noticing it cannot work, and stepping back. A chess puzzle from 1848 became a way of teaching how to think about code.
