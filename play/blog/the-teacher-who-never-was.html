The typing teacher who never existed

Millions learned to type with Mavis Beacon. There was no Mavis Beacon. What the most famous typing tutor got right anyway.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 10 April 2026

In 1987, the Software Toolworks released Mavis Beacon Teaches Typing. The box showed a poised, smiling instructor, and for many people she was the face of learning to type. Plenty assumed she was a real teacher who had written the course.

She wasn’t. Mavis Beacon was a character created for the product; the woman on the box was a model. The program went on to sell millions of copies over the following decades.

What it got right

Strip away the persona and the program did several sensible things: it introduced keys a few at a time, gave short lessons, tracked speed and accuracy, and wrapped drills in small games so practice felt less like a chore.

What Ziggle’s typing trainer does

We followed the same broad path, with our own choices about how:

  • Ten levels, home row first. Each level adds keys and sets a goal for both speed and accuracy. You pass on accuracy as well as speed, because fast and sloppy is a habit that is hard to unlearn.
  • Your weak keys come back. Drills mix in the keys you miss most, instead of hammering the ones you already know.
  • Fair numbers. Words per minute is measured the standard way (five characters to a word), and errors are counted honestly.
  • Short and optional games. Word Rain and a shared daily phrase are there for variety, not as the main event.

And no invented teacher. The trainer is written by people who type too much, and it says so.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The program, and the people behind it

The Software Toolworks, the company behind Mavis Beacon Teaches Typing, was founded by Les Crane, Walt Bilofsky and Mike Duffy, and was best known for the chess simulator Chessmaster 2000. The Wikipedia article credits Norm Worthington, Walt Bilofsky and Mike Duffy as the program’s original authors, and says Crane, a former talk-show host and an early partner in the company, played an integral part in creating the Mavis Beacon persona. The name was chosen on purpose: Mavis came from Mavis Staples, the lead singer of The Staple Singers, and Beacon for its sense of guidance and light.

The face on the box belonged to the Haitian-born model Renee L’Esperance. The article reports that the 2024 documentary Seeking Mavis Beacon, which premiered at the Sundance Film Festival, found she had been paid $500 for the marketing photograph[1] and had no part in building or selling the software and did not share in what it earned. The article adds that L’Esperance herself neither appears in the film nor is directly quoted in it. Whatever the program taught, the arrangement behind its teacher is worth knowing about.

What it did, in detail

The article describes lessons and tests that could earn certificates, drills that gave you passages to type and a summary of your progress, and a racing game in which you drove a car by typing the words on screen accurately. It ran on a long list of machines: MS-DOS, Apple II, Commodore 64, Apple IIGS, Atari ST, Mac, Windows, Palm OS and Amiga. Early versions offered both the usual QWERTY layout and the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard, and a 1989 review in Compute! supported Dvorak. Reviewers at the time praised it: a 1987 New York Times technology column noted its potential for improving typing, Amiga Format praised the user-friendly design, and CU Amiga said it was more interesting than the usual educational packages.

The programme’s mini-games are also credited, the article says, with being among the ancestors of the typing-game genre: inventive, low-budget indie games that use typing as a control method, not only as a subject. Typing trainers and typing games have been close relatives ever since, and Ziggle keeps a small game, Word Rain, beside the lessons too.

Where the trainer follows, and where it differs

Two of those rows are worth saying more about. The layouts matter because the keys you learn are the keys you will use: someone on a French keyboard or a Dvorak keyboard should not have to learn on a layout they do not own. And the missing teacher is a choice. A persona gives a program a friendly voice, but the voice implies somebody behind it. We chose not to invent one: the trainer talks in the plain voice of a product and says what it is.

The twist

The name was built from two borrowed ideas: a singer, Mavis Staples, and a word for guidance and light. The persona worked well enough that a 1998 New York Times column listed in the article wrote about it under the headline “Next They’ll Say Betty Crocker Isn’t Real, Either”, and a 2024 documentary went looking for the person behind it.
