Pelmanism, shell games and the sneaky shuffle

Memory Mischief borrows from two old tricks: turning over pairs of cards, and keeping your eye on the moving cup.

Two very old games sit behind Memory Mischief.

Concentration, or Pelmanism

Lay a deck of cards face down, turn over two, and keep them if they match. The game goes by many names: Concentration, Pairs, Memory, and in Britain, Pelmanism. That last name comes from the Pelman Institute, a London company that sold memory-training courses by post in the early twentieth century. The game borrowed the name; it is fair to say the courses made bigger promises than a card game can keep.

The shell game

The other ancestor is the shell game: a pea under one of three cups, a quick shuffle, and a guess. On a street corner it is usually a con, because the pea isn’t where you think. As a puzzle, the honest version is a test of tracking: can you follow where things went?

Putting them together

Memory Mischief combines the two. Six friends light up in a sequence you need to remember; then they swap places, and you find them in their new spots. Rounds get longer, and in the harder modes the friends shuffle faster and add the occasional plot twist.

We kept the game friendly on purpose. There are no lives to lose, the help explains what changed when you miss, and the modes (Cozy, Curious and Daring) describe what is different rather than how clever you are.

And unlike the Pelman Institute, we make no promises about what it does for your memory in general. It is a good puzzle. That is reason enough to play it.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

One game, many names

Concentration, the article says, is also known as Memory, Matching Pairs, Match Match, Match Up, Pelmanism, Pexeso or simply Pairs. The standard rules use a 52-card deck laid face down in four rows of 13. Turn over two cards; if they match in rank and colour you keep them and go again, otherwise they are turned back and the turn passes. With the two jokers you can make six rows of nine. Nothing requires a grid: some versions strew the cards around a room, and the one called Spaghetti scatters them on the floor.

Pexeso is played with 64 square cards in an 8 × 8 grid, and its content is just 32 identical pairs, with no ranks or suits at all. Memory Mischief shares that idea: what matters is only that two things belong together.

The mathematics of a good flip

Suppose you remember every card you have seen. The best strategy is not the obvious one. Before each turn, t cards are in play and n of them have been seen. The article says to flip an unknown card first. If it matches a card you have seen, take the pair. If it does not match, flip one of the n known cards as your second card, so you give your opponents the least information. Flipping a second unknown card has a 1 in (t − 1 − n) chance of a lucky match, but also an n in (t − 1 − n) chance of showing your opponent a pair they can take.

Playing alone with perfect memory and the best strategy, the expected number of turns to clear a table of n cards approaches about 0.8 n; for a standard 52-card deck the figure the article gives is about 41.6[1]. Note what that says: 26 pairs could in theory go in 26 moves, but even perfect play needs about 41.6, because most moves are spent learning where things are.

The twist

The article’s advice for human players runs against intuition: when you are sure where a pair is, it is better to turn over a card you are less sure of first. If you are wrong about it, you will know not to waste the one you are sure about, and you will not have shown your opponent where it is.

The honest shell game

The game of three shells and a pea has other names, such as thimblerig (first recorded in 1826, from the thimbles it could be played with) and “the old army game”. The article says it goes back at least to Ancient Greece and appears in several paintings of the Middle Ages. It is related to the conjuring routine called cups and balls, the same idea with no money involved, which has been practised since Egyptian times and is shown in Hieronymus Bosch’s The Conjurer. Played honestly, the operator wins only if the shuffle cannot be followed.

In practice it is “almost always” a confidence trick, a “short-con” in the trade’s slang, because sleight of hand moves or hides the pea during play, and shills let you win a few times first. William Powell Frith painted a thimblerig team at the Derby in 1858, and in his memoir he tells how he was about to back his guess when the artist Augustus Egg stopped him and pointed out that the clergyman encouraging him was a confederate. One of the nineteenth century’s most notorious confidence men, Soapy Smith, ran gangs of shell men across the American Midwest and Alaska. Today it is still played for money in cities with many tourists, from La Rambla in Barcelona to Westminster Bridge in London.

The twist

The Price Is Right has a pricing game with a ball hidden under one of four shells made to look like walnut shells. Because federal game-show rules are meant to keep every game one that can legally be won, the shuffling is allowed only before the contestant starts. The honest shell game is possible on television, but only with the con taken out.

What Memory Mischief takes from each

Both ancestors are really tracking games. Concentration asks where a thing is once it is hidden; the shell game asks where it went while it was moving. Memory Mischief asks both, in an honest form: the friends light up, they swap places in plain view, and you find them in their new spots. Nothing slips out of sight, and nothing is for sale.
