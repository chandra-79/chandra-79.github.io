The 2,500-year-old memory trick that still works

A collapsed banquet hall, 40 days of practice, and why memory champions say they were born with ordinary memories.

The story goes like this. Around 2,500 years ago, the Greek poet Simonides of Ceos was performing at a banquet. He stepped outside, and while he was gone the roof of the hall fell in, killing everyone inside. The bodies couldn’t be recognised. But Simonides found he could remember exactly where each guest had been sitting, and so the families could be told.

From that grim evening, the legend says, came the method of loci, better known as the memory palace. Picture a place you know well, walk through it in your mind, and leave a vivid image of each thing you want to remember at a spot along the way. To recall the list, you take the walk again.

Roman writers took it very seriously. An anonymous guide called the Rhetorica ad Herennium explained the technique in detail around 90 BCE, and Cicero told the Simonides story in 55 BCE. Orators used it to remember their speeches. (They also had no slides, which we think is an underrated part of their success.)

Does it work? A 2017 brain study

In 2017, a team led by Martin Dresler published a study in Neuron that tested it properly. First they scanned the brains of 23 of the world’s top memory athletes[1], along with 23 matched people with ordinary memories. The surprise: there was no difference in brain structure. The champions weren’t built differently. What differed were patterns of connection between brain areas.

Then came the fun part. They took 51 people with no memory training at all. One group practised the memory palace for 30 minutes a day, for 40 days. At the start, people could recall about 26 words from a list of 72. After training, the memory palace group averaged about 62. Four months later, they were still remembering more than 22 words more than when they started. And their brain connection patterns had shifted toward those of the champions.

The athletes themselves said they had “trained for months and years”. None of them started out special. That’s our favourite line in the whole study.

The honest bit

What the memory palace gives you is a technique: a reliable way to remember lists, speeches, names or decks of cards when you use it. It doesn’t quietly upgrade the rest of your memory in the background. You still have to use the palace to get the benefit, the same way owning a bicycle only helps when you get on it. We think that’s a great deal anyway.

Where Memory Mischief fits

Memory Mischief is a different kind of memory: keeping track of where things are while they move. You watch the friends swap places, then retrace the trail. You’ll get better at it the more you play, partly because you’ll start inventing your own tricks: naming the friends, telling yourself a tiny story about where they went. That instinct is the seed of every memory technique there is.

The Ziggle memory palace trainer

Ziggle’s memory palace trainer goes from your first ten-room palace to the formats used at the World Memory Championships: random words, names and faces, numbers, dates and playing cards. It uses two of the best-tested ideas in learning research. You test yourself instead of rereading, and reviews are spaced out over days and weeks. In a study of more than 1,350 people[2], the best gap before a review was about 20–40% of the time you wanted to remember something for when that was a week, falling to about 5–10% when it was a year. To remember for a week, review after a day or two. To remember for a year, review after a few weeks.

Try it tonight

  1. Picture your front door, your hallway, your kitchen, your sofa and your bed.
  2. Take five things you need to buy, and leave a ridiculous image of each one at a stop. A giant egg wedged in the front door. Milk pouring down the hallway stairs.
  3. Tomorrow morning, walk the route in your head and see what’s there. Then tell us we didn’t warn you about the egg.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The legend, and the man who doubted it

Both Cicero and Quintilian tell the story, and the Wikipedia article on Simonides gives the longer version. Simonides had been commissioned to write a victory ode for a boxer, hosted by Scopas, a Thessalian nobleman. He filled the poem with references to the twin gods Castor and Pollux, and Scopas told him to collect half his fee from them. During the banquet, Simonides was told that two young men were asking for him. He went outside, found nobody, and the hall collapsed behind him. The two visitors, in the story, were the twins, paying their bill. Afterwards he could name the dead by recalling where each had sat.

There is a twist the usual retelling leaves out. Quintilian himself dismissed the story as a fiction[5], on the ground that Simonides never mentions the incident in his poems although it would have brought him such glory. So the technique has a famous origin story, and one of the people who passed it on did not believe it. That does not matter for the method, which stands or falls on whether it works.

How the method is used

The article defines it as a technique that uses visual imagination[4] and spatial memory: you link pieces of information to places in a familiar space, imagine each item there, and recall by walking the route again. It also goes by the memory journey, journey method or mind palace. Its example is a shopping list: bread on the front step, spilled orange juice in the entryway, apples hanging from the living-room light. The images are usually vivid or odd, because odd images stand out. The article says competitors in memory events use it for shuffled decks of cards, long strings of digits and word lists, and students use it for dates and vocabulary. Dominic O’Brien, a World Memory Champion eight times, built the Dominic System on it, and Joshua Foer, the 2006 US champion, combines it with the Major System and the Person-Action-Object system. The chess grandmaster Timur Gareyev says he adapted the method to keep track of 48 boards in a blindfold exhibition.

The 2017 study, in more detail

The Cell Press release describes it[3]. Dresler and colleagues examined the brains of 23 world-class memory athletes and 23 people matched for age, health and intelligence with typical memory skills. They used functional MRI to measure how strongly brain regions communicate and structural MRI to measure sizes. They expected anatomical differences, as one might expect big muscles in a bodybuilder, and found none. What differed was connectivity, across about 2,500 connections, of which a set of 25 best separated the athletes from the rest. Two regions were hubs for those connections: the medial prefrontal cortex, which is active when new knowledge is related to existing knowledge, and the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, which is involved in learning strategically.

Then came the training. Fifty-one people with typical memory skills and no memory training were split into three groups: strategic memory training with the method of loci, short-term memory training (practising remembering sequences, a bit like the game Concentration), and no training. The training was 30 minutes a day for 40 days, that is 20 hours in all (computed).

  • 26 → 62words recalled from a list of 72
  • 35 / 11 / 7extra words: loci, short-term, none
  • 25 of 2,500connections that best told athletes apart

In proportions (computed): 26 of 72 words is 36% and 62 of 72 is 86%, so the group went from remembering just over a third of the list to nearly nine in ten. Our reading: the extra seven words for people who did nothing come from simply doing the test again, so the 35 against 7 is a fair picture of what the method added. The authors’ comment is that once people are familiar with the strategies and know how to apply them, they can keep their performance high without much further training. After training, the strategic group’s brain scans looked more like the champions’.

What the athletes themselves said

The release quotes the lead author: the athletes, without a single exception, had trained for months and years using mnemonic strategies. One, Boris Konrad, a top-ten memory athlete and co-author, became one to improve his academic performance, and was not born with exceptional memory skills. The release adds that top competitors can memorise about five hundred digits or a hundred words in five minutes. The Wikipedia article on memory sport says the first world championship was held in 1991[6], and that in 2016, after a dispute, most of the organisations except those in China and Arabia left the World Memory Sports Council and launched the International Association of Memory, so since 2017 there have been two world championships.

What this does and does not show

All 51 trainees followed one method for 40 days, and the test was recalling lists of words. The study says nothing about whether the skill carries over to names at a party or facts for an exam, nor whether the brain-connectivity change helps anywhere but the memory task. The Wikipedia article’s description of everyday uses is a description, not a measurement. What the study supports is what the post already says: a method is a skill, it is learnable by ordinary people, and a good share of it lasts.

The twist

In Dresler’s study the group who practised plain short-term memory, a bit like the game Concentration, gained only 11 words, and four months later they no longer showed substantial gains. The group who learned a method kept theirs. It is the method that lasts, not the number of repetitions.
