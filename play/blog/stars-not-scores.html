Stars, not scores: why trainers have a pass mark

In 1968 Benjamin Bloom argued that most learners can master a topic if they move on only once they’ve got it. Ziggle’s trainers work that way.

Training design · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 17 July 2026

In this article 7 sections
  1. How it shapes the Ziggle trainers
  2. Why stars rather than high scores

Traditional teaching often moves a whole class on to the next topic on a fixed date, whether or not everyone has understood the last one. Gaps pile up quietly.

In 1968 the educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom proposed mastery learning: break a subject into units, check understanding at the end of each, and only move on once a learner meets a set standard. Those who don’t yet get more practice and different explanations, not a lower grade and a push forward.

How it shapes the Ziggle trainers

  • Ten levels each, from first steps to advanced, in typing, emoji and flags, the memory palace and the cube.
  • A star for passing, with a clear bar: for example eight out of ten on an emoji round, or a speed and accuracy goal in typing.
  • The next level opens when you pass, and you can always go back. There’s no penalty for repeating a level; it is the point.
  • A placement test in typing lets experienced typists skip ahead, so nobody is stuck on lessons they don’t need.

Why stars rather than high scores

A high score invites you to grind one level for a better number. A star says “you’ve got this, move on”. Both have their place, which is why the daily games have scores and the trainers have stars.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

An idea older than the name

Bloom’s 1968 paper was not the first attempt. The article traces efforts in the 1920s, such as the Winnetka Plan of Carleton Washburne and Henry Morrison’s methods at the University of Chicago Laboratory School, which put pupils’ proficiency before finishing the course on schedule. They faded, the article says, mainly for lack of technology to sustain them. The idea came back in the late 1950s and 1960s, partly through B. F. Skinner’s programmed instruction, which taught complex behaviour by breaking it into small steps, each learned in order.

John B. Carroll’s model of school learning supplied the central claim. Having studied language learning, he found that a student’s aptitude predicted not only how far they got in a given time but also how long they needed to reach a given level. Read that way, aptitude is mostly a measure of time required. If each student gets the time they need, a majority should reach the standard. Bloom built his method on that, observing that teachers varied little in how they taught while pupils’ results varied widely.

  1. 1920sThe Winnetka Plan and the Chicago Laboratory School experiments put individual mastery before the calendar.
  2. 1960sCarroll’s model of school learning: aptitude as the time a learner needs.
  3. 1967Fred Keller’s Personalized System of Instruction: self-paced, with unit-by-unit mastery and proctors.
  4. 1968Bloom formally proposes “learning for mastery”.
  5. 1980sInterest falls, despite mostly positive results.

What the numbers say

Bloom thought that more than 90 percent of students could reach a worthwhile level under mastery learning. He also noted that only a small share, about 1 to 5 percent, have a special talent for a subject, and about 5 percent a special difficulty. For the other 90 percent, he argued, aptitude is simply a measure of how fast they learn. The article quotes a twelve-country study in which, leaving out the top 5 percent of students, the slower learners of a subject such as mathematics needed about six times as long[1] as the faster ones, and in which time spent on homework had no relation, or a slightly negative one, to final grades. Time on task, in other words, is not a measure of mastery.

  • 6 : 1time needed by slower and faster learners of maths in a twelve-country study
  • 0.59average effect size reported for mastery learning
  • 90%Keller’s students who said they learned more, had more fun and felt more accomplished

The article reports an average effect size of 0.59[1], which it describes as moderate to substantial, and that higher mastery thresholds went with greater improvement in exam performance. Two large meta-analyses were positive. Guskey and Pigott (1988), covering 46 studies of group-based mastery classrooms, found positive effects on achievement, retention of what was learned and involvement. Kulik and colleagues (1990), covering 108 studies, also found positive effects, and students reported liking the approach. In Keller’s version, 90% of the students tested said they learned more, had more fun and felt a greater sense of accomplishment, although they worked harder.

The caveats

The article is plain about the criticisms, which makes them worth listing:

  • Time and equality. If everyone must reach the same level, some will need more time. Critics say the theory does not settle that. Bloom assumed slower learners would speed up with practice, but a four-year study by Arlin in 1984 found students who needed extra help in year one[1] still needed about the same extra help in year four.
  • Weak comparisons. Control groups were sometimes ordinary existing classes against courses built to follow the mastery model.
  • Home-made tests. The largest effects came when researchers used their own tests, and they essentially disappeared on standardised tests.
  • Short studies. Many lasted only three or four weeks.
  • Depth. Programmes of unit tests may suit surface learning better than deep learning.

Those caveats suggest keeping the claim modest. A star says you met a bar on the day you played, not that you have mastered typing for life, which is why the trainers also keep a Review list to bring old keys and cards back.

Why a star and not a grade

Bloom’s view of the scoring was specific. He wanted the results of progress tests expressed in a non-grade format, because grades on repeated progress tests prepare students to accept a level of learning below mastery. He also argued for absolute standards rather than competitive ones, because the first encourages cooperation. And the article describes mastery classrooms as using criterion-referenced tests, in which students are not racing each other but trying for a personal best. A star is exactly that: a yes or no against a fixed bar, with the same bar for everybody.

Ziggle’s pass mark has two parts. Across the ten typing levels the accuracy gate rises from 95% to 98% while the speed goal rises from 15 to 70 words a minute (computed from the level list), so a level is not passed by being fast and sloppy.

The twist

In one study the article cites, applying mastery learning in a class raised the share of students with a grade of A from 20 percent to 80 percent, about two standard deviations. Using the formative test records as quality control then lifted it to 90 percent the next year. The article links this to Bloom’s “2 sigma problem”: the observation that the average student tutored one-to-one with mastery-learning techniques did two standard deviations better than students taught in the conventional way.
