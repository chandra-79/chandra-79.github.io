The 1880 puzzle craze, and the arrows that followed

A sliding-tile toy once gripped America. Its descendants, from Rush Hour to Arrow escape, all ask one question: what has to move first?

Origins · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 20 March 2026

In early 1880, a small box of fifteen numbered tiles became a sensation in the United States. The 15 puzzle had one empty space; you slid tiles into it to put the numbers in order. Newspapers wrote about people playing it at work and in the street.

The puzzle is usually credited to Noyes Palmer Chapman, a postmaster in New York State. The famous puzzle-maker Sam Loyd later claimed to have invented it and offered a prize for an arrangement that, mathematicians soon showed, could never be solved. Exactly half of all starting positions cannot reach the goal, for a reason called parity.

Rush Hour

About a century later, the Japanese puzzle designer Nob Yoshigahara created a traffic-jam puzzle in the 1970s that went on sale in the United States in 1996 as Rush Hour (it is now made by ThinkFun). Cars and trucks can only slide along their own lane, and you must clear a path for one red car to escape.

What they share

These are ordering puzzles. The hard part is not any single move; it is working out what must happen before what. The blocker in front of you is itself blocked by something else.

Arrow escape

Ziggle’s arrow puzzle takes that ordering idea and strips it down. Every arrow can only leave the board the way it points. Tap an arrow whose path is clear and it flies off; tap one that is blocked and it bumps and costs a heart. Untangling a big board is a chain of small “this one first” decisions.

Unlike the 15 puzzle, every Arrow escape board is checked to be solvable, and Arrow Twists adds new rules (two-headed arrows, one-way tiles, colour gates) one at a time.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

How a postmaster’s puzzle reached the shops

The 15 puzzle has a messier birth than most toys, with a patent that was turned down, a dentist with a prize and a famous puzzle-maker who claimed the credit twelve years too late. This is the trail as the Wikipedia article reads it:

  1. 1874Noyes Palmer Chapman, a postmaster in Canastota, New York, is said to have shown friends a forerunner: 16 numbered blocks to be put in rows of four that each add up to 34 (a magic square).
  2. 1878Ernest U. Kinsey is granted a US patent for “Puzzle-Blocks” on 20 August.
  3. 1879Johnson and Story publish the parity argument that shows half of all starting positions can never be solved. By December, students at the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut, are making and selling the puzzle, and a Boston woodworker, Matthias Rice, starts making it as the “Gem Puzzle”.
  4. Jan 1880Charles Pevey, a dentist in Worcester, Massachusetts, offers a cash reward for a solution, and the puzzle draws attention.
  5. Feb 1880Chapman applies for a patent on his “Block Solitaire Puzzle” on 21 February. It is rejected, likely because it was too close to Kinsey’s 1878 patent.
  6. 1886 and 1891Sam Loyd writes his first article about the puzzle in 1886[1]. In 1891 he first claims to have invented it, and keeps saying so until his death in 1911. He had no connection to the invention or its first popularity.

The craze itself, in the article’s plain words, happened in 1880. It had other names along the way: Gem Puzzle, Boss Puzzle, Game of Fifteen and Mystic Square, and the “16 puzzle” if you count the gap.

Why exactly half the positions are impossible

Take the 16 squares of the frame, 15 tiles and the gap, and every way of arranging them. Every legal slide changes two things at once: the parity of the arrangement (whether it takes an odd or an even number of swaps to reach from the solved one) and the parity of the gap’s distance from the bottom-right corner. Because both flip on every move, a combination of the two stays fixed. That fixed quantity splits all arrangements into two camps that can never reach each other, and the two camps are the same size. Johnson and Story also showed that on any board at least two tiles wide and two tall, every even arrangement can be reached, so the parity rule is the only obstacle.

That explains Loyd’s trap. His $1,000 prize (about $35,833 in 2025 money, according to the article) was for swapping just the 14 and the 15, which flips the parity once. It was impossible, as Johnson and Story had shown more than a decade earlier, and the prize was never at risk.

How far from solved can a position be?

  • 80single-tile moves for the hardest 15-puzzle positions (17 of them)
  • 43moves if a run of slides in one direction counts as one
  • 31single-tile moves, at most, for any solvable 8 puzzle

Every reachable 15-puzzle position can be solved in at most 80 single-tile moves[1]; there are 17 starting positions that need all 80. Counting several tiles slid in a row as one move, the longest is 43. The little 8 puzzle never needs more than 31 single-tile moves, or 24 multi-tile ones. For bigger boards, finding a solution is easy, but finding the shortest one is NP-hard, which is why the n puzzle became a standard test for search algorithms. Two cheap rules of thumb for guessing how far a position is from solved, counting misplaced tiles and adding up each tile’s distance in rows and columns from home, never overestimate, and that is exactly what algorithms like A* need.

The twist

Bobby Fischer, the chess world champion, was an expert at the 15 puzzle: he was timed solving it in 17 seconds, and showed it on The Tonight Show on 8 November 1972.

Rush Hour: the same ordering puzzle, with cars

Nob Yoshigahara’s traffic jam is built in a 6 × 6 grid with 16 vehicles (12 cars and 4 trucks), each one square wide: cars two long, trucks three. Nothing may rotate, so a vehicle slides along its own line and nowhere else, and the only goal is to get the red car to the exit. The regular edition comes with 40 cards in four levels from Beginner to Expert, with more in the Deluxe and Ultimate editions and in spin-offs such as Rush Hour Jr. and Safari Rush Hour. The game has sold more than a million units.

It is harder than it looks. The most difficult starting position that can be set up takes 93 steps to solve[2], or 51 moves if each slide of a vehicle counts as one. On boards of any size, deciding whether a Rush Hour position has a solution is PSPACE-complete, a class of problems no one expects to solve quickly, and in 2005 Tromp and Cilibrasi showed this remains true even when every vehicle has length two.

That is the thread to Arrow escape. In the 15 puzzle the whole difficulty is hidden in the parity. In Rush Hour it is in the chain of blockers. Arrow escape keeps the chain and drops the trap: every board is checked to be solvable before you see it.
