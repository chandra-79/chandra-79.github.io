The best gap between reviews: what 1,350 people taught us

Reviewing sooner or later changes how much you keep. One big experiment found the sweet spot, and that it shrinks the longer you want to remember.

Memory · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 23 May 2026

“Space it out” is the oldest advice in learning, and for a century it stayed vague. Space it out by how much? In 2006 Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues tried to settle what was already known: they found 839 assessments of distributed practice, in 317 experiments, in 184 articles[1], and combined them. The result: the best gap depends on how long you need to remember.

Two years later the same group ran the experiment the literature was missing. More than 1,350 people[2] learned a set of facts, waited anything up to 3.5 months, reviewed them, and were tested up to a year after that.

The ridge

For any one test date, a longer gap at first helped, then gradually hurt, like walking up a ridge and down the other side. The top of the ridge moved with the test date. Measured against how long people had to remember, the best gap was about 20–40% of the delay for a one-week test[2], and only about 5–10% for a one-year test.

Why a shorter share is still a longer wait

The surprise is in the table: the share falls as the delay grows, while the gap itself rises from a day or two to a month or so. Learn something for a year and you can leave it far longer before the first review, yet you are reviewing sooner relative to the whole stretch. That is why a flashcard system starts with tiny gaps and then widens them.

The twist

The authors’ own conclusion is that many educational practices are “highly inefficient”[2]. Cramming everything into one sitting is the zero-gap end of the experiment: there is no wait before the review at all.

What this means for a daily puzzle

A once-a-day game spaces itself: you meet the same kinds of challenge, with a day between. The trainers (memory palace, typing, emoji) widen the gaps as you get things right. Ziggle does not claim to make your memory better. It simply avoids the cramming trap.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

A hundred and forty years of “space it out”

The idea is older than the experiments in this post. Hermann Ebbinghaus first identified the spacing effect, and his detailed study of it was published in his 1885 book Über das Gedächtnis. Learning is more effective when the sessions are spaced out, and the finding has held for free recall, recognition, cued recall and more. The timeline of the advice itself is short but patchy:

  1. 1880sEbbinghaus describes the forgetting curve and proposes that reviewing at several intervals can curb it.
  2. 1932C. Mace, in Psychology of Study, suggests reviewing at gradually increasing gaps, roughly one day, two days, four days, eight days, and so on[4].
  3. 1939H. Spitzer tests a form of spaced review on more than 3,600 sixth-graders[4] learning science facts in Iowa, and finds it works.
  4. Late 1960sAfter decades of quiet, cognitive psychologists, including Melton and Landauer and Bjork, return to the timing of repetitions; Landauer and Bjork’s 1978 name-and-face experiment follows.
  5. 1973Sebastian Leitner devises the Leitner system, a flashcard box that spaces reviews by how well you know each card.

What “one, two, four, eight” adds up to

Mace’s doubling rule is easy to say and surprisingly powerful. If each gap is twice the last, after n reviews you are 2n − 1 days from your first session. That is 31 days after five reviews, 255 after eight (about eight and a half months), and 1,023 after ten reviews, almost three years (computed). Ten short reviews, each a few seconds, can carry a fact for years, which is exactly the economy that cramming does not have.

How strong is the effect?

In the 2006 review the main post cites, spaced practice beat massed practice in 259 of 271 cases[3], which is 95.6% (computed). It is a rare finding in psychology that is so consistent. It does have limits, though. Shaughnessy (1977) found that for items shown only twice and tested after a 24-hour delay the effect was not robust, while for items shown four or six times it was present. His reading was that no single mechanism can explain all the forms the spacing effect takes.

The twist

The effect has been tested well beyond lists of words: in the 1980s it was extended to people with amnesia and other memory disorders, who could learn name-and-face pairs with expanding gaps. Mace’s 1932 sentence, written before any of the large experiments, already contains the practical shape (short gaps first, longer later) that the 2008 experiment measured as a ridge.
