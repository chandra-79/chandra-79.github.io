The five-minute break, by the numbers

A 2022 review of 22 studies found short breaks leave people feeling livelier and less tired. Performance is a more complicated story.

Habits · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 8 September 2026

In this article 6 sections

  2. How to take a good one
  3. Where Ziggle fits

You know the moment. The words on the screen have stopped meaning anything, and you’ve read the same email three times. Is a quick break a good idea, or just procrastination with better branding?

What 22 studies[1] found

In 2022, Albulescu and colleagues published a meta-analysis in PLOS ONE that pooled 22 studies on micro-breaks: pauses of 10 minutes or less. The breaks varied: stretching, relaxing, or something more engaging like watching a video.

  • Micro-breaks were linked to higher vigour and lower fatigue. People felt livelier and less worn out.
  • Overall, they didn’t clearly improve performance on the task.
  • Longer breaks were linked to better performance, especially on creative or clerical work, and less so on very demanding thinking tasks.

We like this study because it’s honest. A break is good for how you feel. It isn’t magic for your output, and for hard thinking you may need a longer one.

How to take a good one

  1. Actually stop. Switching from your report to your inbox isn’t a break; it’s a different kind of work.
  2. Move if you can. Stand up, refill your water, look out of a window.
  3. Pick something with an ending. A puzzle, a song, a cup of tea. Endless feeds don’t end, which is the problem.
  4. Come back on purpose. Before you take the break, note the next thing you’ll do when you return.

Where Ziggle fits

A daily puzzle is a decent break because it finishes. Letter Lock is six guesses at most. A typing speed test is 15 to 60 seconds. When you’re done, you’re done: no autoplay, no “just one more” nudge, no feed. We think a break should hand you back to your day, not keep you.

And if you’ve been at a screen all morning, the best break might not involve a screen at all. We won’t be offended. We’ll be here at lunch.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Reading the numbers properly

A quick correction to the count above: the search found 19 research reports[1], which gave 22 independent study samples and 2,335 people[1] in all. Several of the studies were run on students. Different outcomes had different numbers of samples behind them:

Cohen’s d of about 0.2 counts as small and 0.5 as medium, so breaks gave a small but reliable lift in vigour and drop in fatigue, and a smaller, uncertain one in performance. The authors did not stop at the overall figure. They split the performance results by the kind of task and found significant effects only for tasks with lower cognitive demand[1]. And a meta-regression showed that the longer the break, the larger the boost to performance[1].

What the authors recommend

Their reading: the data support micro-breaks for well-being, while “for performance, recovering from highly depleting tasks may need more than 10-minute breaks”, and they call for more research on that. In other words, the paper does not say that a ten-minute pause fixes a hard afternoon; it says a short pause helps how you feel and a longer one may be needed to help how you perform.

The twist

A “non-significant” result is not a zero. The performance effect (d = 0.16) is small and its confidence interval includes no effect, but it points the same way as the others, and with only 15 samples the estimate is imprecise. The honest summary is “probably a small help, not proven”, which is a better thing to know than either “breaks work” or “breaks do nothing”.
