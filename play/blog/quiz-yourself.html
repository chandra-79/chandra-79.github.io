Quiz yourself: the study method that beat the fancy diagram

Students were sure concept maps would win. A week later, plain old recall had won by a mile.

Science of practice · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 26 May 2026

If you’ve ever highlighted a textbook until it glowed, this one’s for you.

In 2011, Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt ran a study that was published in Science, which is not where most study tips end up. Around 200 students[1] read science texts and then prepared in one of two ways.

  • One group made concept maps: diagrams with boxes and arrows linking the ideas. It’s active, it’s visual, and it feels like proper studying.
  • The other group read the text, put it away, and simply tried to recall what they could. Then they checked.

A week later, everyone was tested. The recall group showed about a 50% improvement in long-term retention[1] over the concept mappers.

Here is the part that rarely makes the headlines. In the second experiment, 101 of the 120 students (84%)[1] did better after practising retrieval than after concept mapping. Yet 90 of the same 120 (75%) had said they expected concept mapping to work just as well, or better. Retrieval felt less productive and worked better, which is why a hard quiz is such an easy thing to skip.

And here’s the twist we love. Before the test, most students predicted that concept mapping would work best. They weren’t silly; it just felt more productive. Recall feels clumsy. You sit there, you can’t quite remember, you feel bad. Then the feeling turns out to be lying.

Why the clumsy way wins

Every time you pull something out of memory, you rebuild the path to it. Rereading gives you the comfortable feeling of recognition (“yes, I know this”) without doing that rebuilding. Psychologists sometimes call that comfortable feeling an illusion of fluency. It’s the same reason you can recognise a song instantly and still not be able to sing the second verse.

It lines up with an older finding too. In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke found students who practised recalling a passage remembered 61% of it a week later[2], while those who reread remembered 40%.

How to use it without a lab

  1. Close the book first. Before you review anything, write down what you remember on a blank page. Then open the book and fill the gaps.
  2. Turn notes into questions. “Ebbinghaus published in 1885” becomes “When did Ebbinghaus publish?”.
  3. Accept the struggle. The moment where you can’t quite remember is the moment doing the work.
  4. Space your quizzes. Quiz again tomorrow, then in a few days.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

An old observation with a long paper trail

The Wikipedia article on the testing effect says[4] people noticed it long before anyone measured it. Francis Bacon, in 1620, wrote that you will learn a text by heart more easily by reading it ten times and trying to recite it from time to time than by reading it twenty times. John Locke, in 1689, observed that ideas refreshed most often fix themselves best in memory. William James, in 1890, wrote that when you almost know a piece, it pays better to wait and recollect it by an effort from within than to look at the book again. The first documented experiments were published in 1909 by Edwina Abbott, and in 1932 C. A. Mace wrote that active repetition is far more effective than passive repetition, at least when forgetting has not yet begun.

  1. 1620Bacon: reading with attempts to recite beats reading alone.
  2. 1689Locke: ideas “oftenest refreshed” stay clearest.
  3. 1890James: recollect by effort, rather than look again.
  4. 1909Abbott: the first documented experiments.
  5. 1917Gates’s study, which Roediger and Karpicke cite as an early example.
  6. 2006Roediger and Karpicke test it with prose passages.
  7. 2011Karpicke and Blunt compare it with concept mapping, in Science.

The 2006 experiments, in detail

The study the post quotes was run with ordinary prose[3]: two TOEFL reading passages, one on the Sun (256 words) and one on sea otters (275 words), each divided into 30 idea units for scoring. Two raters scored the first 40 recall tests and agreed closely (a correlation of .95). In Experiment 1, 120 university students studied each passage once, and then either studied one again or took a free-recall test on it, with no feedback. A final test came after 5 minutes, 2 days or a week.

The result flips with time. Straight after studying, extra reading wins. After two days and a week, the tested material wins by 14 points both times. The authors noted something striking: the tested group’s recall after a week (56%) was slightly higher than the restudy group’s after only two days (54%), so one test seemed to buy five extra days of memory. In forgetting terms (computed from the table), from 5 minutes to a week the restudied passage lost 48% of what had been recalled and the tested passage lost 25%.

Experiment 2 used 180 students, 30 in each of six conditions. They had four 5-minute periods with one passage: read it four times (SSSS), read it three times and take one test (SSST), or read it once and take three tests (STTT). Nobody got feedback on the tests, and the final test came after 5 minutes or one week.

Read the table sideways. The students who studied hardest, with 14 readings, were the most confident, and were right about the first five minutes (83%). A week later they remembered the least: 40%, against 61% for students who read the passage a quarter as often and took three tests. Confidence went the wrong way, and the authors point out that the students who studied more also found the passage more boring. The Karpicke and Blunt study the post opens with tells the same story with a different rival, concept maps, and the deeper article linked below goes into its numbers.

What the testing effect needs

  • Effort, within reach. The “retrieval effort” idea is that difficult but successful retrievals are better for memory than easy ones. The article reports that learning a language was better with unfamiliar words than familiar ones. Even an unsuccessful attempt can help, particularly if the answer is shown afterwards.
  • Feedback. Testing with feedback, at a suitable difficulty, beats studying or testing without feedback. The experiments above gave none, and testing still won.
  • Short answers over multiple choice. The article says short-answer and essay tests produce bigger gains than multiple choice, though the format matters less than the act of retrieving. Cued recall, with a hint, makes retrieval easier and can help in the short term while weakening the long-term gain.
  • Spacing. Using spaced repetition raises the effect, with a delay between tests having a bigger impact. The article’s advice on timing: material studied in the day should be tested in the evening, while material studied in the evening should be tested at once, because of what sleep does to memory.
  • Beginning with a test. The pre-testing effect: trying questions before you have learned the material helps later learning, provided you are shown the answers afterwards, and it seems to suit concrete facts and concepts more than abstract material.

Where it can mislead

Two cautions from the same article. Flashcards work, but dropping a card from the pile too early, after one correct answer, leads to worse long-term retention, and people often misjudge their own results on a card. And the studies are mostly about material you can say or write, facts, passages, vocabulary: the article says transfer is strongest for applying theory to practice and for inference questions, but gives mixed evidence on whether it carries to other topics. A quiz is a good way to learn a fact. It is not a general power-up.

The twist

The students who restudied won the first test. Five minutes after reading, repeated reading beat testing, 81% to 75%. If the experiment had stopped there, the textbook advice to reread would look right. It is the delay, which is how real exams and real life work, that reverses it.
