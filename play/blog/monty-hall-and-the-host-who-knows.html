Monty Hall: the answer changes if the host doesn’t know

Switching wins two times in three, when the host knows where the car is. When he doesn’t, it makes no difference at all.

Logic games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 17 June 2026

You are on a game show. Behind one of three doors is a car; behind the others, goats. You pick door 1. The host, who knows what is behind every door, opens another door to show a goat, and offers you a switch. Do you take it?

The problem was posed in a 1975 letter by the statistician Steve Selvin and made famous in 1990, when Marilyn vos Savant answered it in her Parade magazine column: switch. About 10,000[2] readers wrote in, nearly 1,000 of them with PhDs[2], and many insisted she was wrong.

Let 100,000 games decide

She was right. We ran the game 100,000 times with a seeded random generator. Staying with the first pick won 33,324 times (33.3%) and switching won 66,676 (66.7%).

Take away the host’s knowledge

Now change one thing. Suppose the host does not know where the car is and simply opens one of the other two doors at random, and we only count the games where he happens to reveal a goat. Then the advantage disappears: in 199,842 such games, staying won 50.1% and switching won 49.9%.

The twist

The maths did not change, the information did. When the host knows, his choice of door tells you something: he had to avoid the car. When he picks blindly, a goat is just luck and tells you nothing. The “paradox” is really a question about what the host’s actions reveal, which is why so many careful people got it wrong, and why the puzzle needs its wording spelled out.

Where it pops up in puzzle games

Any game that gives you a clue after your guess is doing a gentler version of this: the feedback is only useful because of how it was chosen. A Letter Lock colour pattern is informative because the game always tells the truth about your word, not because it tells you something at random.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Make it a million doors

Marilyn vos Savant suggested a way to make the answer feel right: imagine 1,000,000 doors[2]. You pick one. The host, who knows where the car is and always avoids it, opens 999,998 of the others, leaving yours and one more. Would you switch? Of course: your door was a one-in-a-million shot, so the one the host carefully left alone almost certainly hides the car. Switching wins 999,999 times in 1,000,000.

Why most people stay

Even after hearing the argument, many people prefer not to switch. The psychological literature offers three explanations: the endowment effect[2] (we overvalue the door we already “own”), the status quo bias (we prefer to keep a choice we have made), and a preference for errors of omission over commission: if all else is equal, it feels better to lose by doing nothing than to lose by acting.

The host’s habits change the answer

The Parade version does not say how the host chooses.[3] The standard assumptions are that he always opens a goat door and, when he has a choice, picks at random. Suppose instead that when you have picked the car he prefers door 3 with probability q. If you pick door 1, the car is behind door 1, 2 or 3 with probability 1/3 each. Then door 3 is opened with probability q/3 when the car is behind door 1 and with probability 1/3 when it is behind door 2, and never when it is behind door 3.

Whatever his habit, switching is never worse than 1/2 once you see which door he opens, and it is exactly 2/3 for a host who picks at random. Wikipedia’s article notes that neither source suggests the player knows q, and that the standard 2/3 is the average over the two doors he might open.

Hosts with other rules

The history, with dates

  1. 1959Martin Gardner describes the three prisoners problem in Scientific American, mathematically equivalent to Monty Hall.
  2. 1975Steve Selvin poses the problem in a letter to The American Statistician, and names it in a later letter.
  3. 1990Marilyn vos Savant answers a reader’s question, from Craig Whitaker, in Parade: switch.
  4. 1990–91Three more Parade columns follow, after thousands of letters, most disagreeing with her.

Wikipedia adds that Paul Erdős, one of the most prolific mathematicians in history, remained unconvinced until he was shown a computer simulation. It also notes that vos Savant later offered a shell game with a pea under one of three shells to clarify her answer, and that it classifies the puzzle as a veridical paradox, one whose true answer seems absurd.

The twist

Whether switching is “rational” also depends on whether you know how the host behaves. If you cannot be sure he always avoids the car, the 2-in-3 argument is not guaranteed to apply, which is why the problem needs its rules stated so carefully, and why our simulation of the “random host” version came out at 50-50.
