The open dictionary inside Word Builder

It has 120,630 meanings, built by volunteers and given away free. Here is how a dictionary of meanings differs from a dictionary of spellings.

Word Builder shows a word, what it means, an example and a few neighbours. All of that comes from Open English WordNet, a free dictionary released under a Creative Commons attribution licence (CC BY 4.0) and maintained by volunteers. We chose it on purpose: it is free to use commercially if we credit it, which is the deal we are glad to keep.

A dictionary of meanings

An ordinary dictionary is a list of spellings with meanings underneath. WordNet turns it inside out: it is a list of meanings, called synsets, each holding the words that can express it. The 2024 edition has 161,705 words[1] sharing 120,630 synsets[1], joined by 419,168 relations[1] (is-a, part-of, opposite and so on).

Why meanings make better puzzles

Because every sense is its own node, we can ask “which words mean the opposite?” or “which of these three is the odd one out?” and get an answer from the structure, not from guesswork. That is where Word Builder’s quiz options and the Lexicon Atlas’s neighbours come from. WordNet began in 1985 at Princeton, led by the psychologist George Miller, the same Miller who was persecuted by seven (see the magical number four).

Two words with one meaning share a node; one word with two meanings sits in two nodes, which is how a dictionary can tell the bank you fish from the bank you save in.

The licence is part of the design

Ziggle’s vocabulary content comes from sources that allow commercial use with credit, and we credit them in the app, in a NOTICE file and on the page. Word Builder’s 242 words are about 0.15% of the 161,705 in the dictionary: plenty of room to grow.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the project is, in its own words

The Open English WordNet README describes it[3] as a lexical network of the English language that groups words into synsets and links them by relations such as hypernymy (is a kind of), antonymy and meronymy (is a part of). It calls itself a fork of Princeton WordNet developed under an open-source methodology, released under CC BY 4.0, and says it cannot accept automatically generated changes that have not been manually checked. Since the 2025 release there are three versions: Open English WordNet (common nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs), a Plus edition with a selection of validated proper nouns, and Open English Namenet, with a very large number of names derived from Wikidata.

The size of each release

Two things stand out in the computed columns. Words per synset sit between 1.34 and 1.36 in every release, an average of about one and a third words for each meaning. And relations per synset jump in 2023, from 3.22 to 3.46, because relations rose by 29,468 (computed, 386,437 to 415,905) while synsets barely moved. Against Princeton 3.1 the 2024 edition has 2,690 more words (+1.7%), 2,839 more synsets (+2.4%) and 40,965 more relations (+10.8%), so, our reading, the volunteers’ recent work has gone more into connecting meanings than into adding them.

One correction to the post’s table. The line it labels “Open English WordNet 2025” is the README’s “2025+” row, the Plus edition with the added proper nouns. The README gives the plain 2025 edition as 135,969 words in 107,519 synsets with 355,064 relations, so the Plus edition has 25,906 more words (16% of its total).

How the meanings are arranged

Wikipedia’s article on WordNet explains the structure[4]. Nouns and verbs sit in hierarchies of is-a links: one sense of dog goes up through canine, carnivore, placental mammal, mammal, vertebrate, chordate and animal. At the top the nouns are organised into 25 beginner trees and the verbs into 15, all linked to a single beginner synset, “entity”. Adjectives are not in trees. Two central antonyms such as hot and cold form the two poles of a dumbbell, and satellites such as steaming and chilly attach to the pole they resemble. Verbs have their own relations: lisp is a troponym of talk (a manner of doing it), and snore entails sleep.

Words, as well as meanings, can be linked. The article’s example is that one sense of the noun director is tied to a sense of the verb direct through a morphosemantic link, which is how a dictionary can follow a word across its parts of speech.

Where the idea came from

The project started in 1985 at Princeton’s Cognitive Science Laboratory under the psychologist George Miller, later directed by Christiane Fellbaum, who shared the 2006 Antonio Zampolli Prize with him. The initial aim, the article says, was a database consistent with theories of human semantic memory from the late 1960s. The experiments behind them found that people could confirm that a canary can sing faster than that it can fly, and that it has skin more slowly still, as if they climbed a hierarchy from canary to songbird to bird to animal. The article adds that these theories have been criticised, though some of WordNet’s organisation fits the evidence: anomia can knock out a whole category of words, and antonyms really do appear together far more often than chance.

The article also records a change: as of 2026 the Princeton project is no longer being developed and its online version is no longer available. Community projects, the Open English WordNet among them, carry the dictionary on.

What is in Word Builder

The word list is a trimmed selection from the dictionary, so the shares of synonyms and opposites reflect what we chose to show, not the dictionary as a whole. Each entry in the data carries the word, its part of speech, a short definition, an example sentence, synonyms, an opposite and a tier.

The twist

WordNet’s founder set out to model human memory and ended up building a dictionary that machines use. The same graph that lets a computer know a canary is a bird lets Word Builder ask for an opposite or an odd one out, and between Princeton 3.1 and the 2024 edition the links grew by 10.8% while the meanings grew by 2.4%.
