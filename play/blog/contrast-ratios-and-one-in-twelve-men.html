One in twelve men and the colours of a puzzle

Colour-blind players are not a niche. We measured the contrast of our own palette to see where it stands.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 12 July 2026

About 1 in 12 men[1] have some form of colour vision deficiency, according to the US National Eye Institute. Anyone building a word game on green and yellow tiles has to take that seriously. There is a second measure that matters just as much: whether text can be read against its background at all.

A number for readability

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines define a contrast ratio between two colours, from 1:1 (identical) to 21:1 (black on white). Normal text should reach at least 4.5:1[2] to pass level AA, large text 3:1, and the strictest level (AAA) asks for 7:1.

Everything in the table clears 4.5, and the main text clears 7. A test in our code base checks every colour look, light and dark, so a new look that dips under 4.5 for text fails the build.

The colour we do not use for text

Our hint buttons are gold. Gold text on the cream page would score only 1.6:1, hopeless for reading, so the gold sits behind dark text (7.4:1). Colour as decoration is fine; colour carrying a message needs a number.

The twist

Letter Lock never relies on colour alone. A correct letter shows a tick, a misplaced one a small dot, as well as green or yellow, so the game reads the same to someone who sees those two colours as the same brownish shade. For one in twelve men, that is the difference between a puzzle and a guess.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

How a contrast ratio is worked out

The W3C page on Success Criterion 1.4.3 gives the formula. First, each colour’s relative luminance: convert each 8-bit channel to a fraction of 255, apply a curve that undoes the screen’s gamma (divide by 12.92 for very dark values, otherwise raise ((value + 0.055) ÷ 1.055) to the power 2.4), and combine them as L = 0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B[4]. Green counts most because the eye is most sensitive to it. Then the ratio is (L1 + 0.05) ÷ (L2 + 0.05), where L1 is the lighter colour. The 0.05 stops the ratio from running away for near-black colours. Pure white against pure black is (1 + 0.05) ÷ (0 + 0.05) = 21, which is why the scale runs from 1 to 21.

A worked example with Ziggle’s own colours (computed): Day Spark’s text colour #231f1a has a relative luminance of 0.0141 and its page colour #f4ede0 has 0.8518. The ratio is (0.8518 + 0.05) ÷ (0.0141 + 0.05) = 14.1, which clears even the strictest 7 : 1.

The page records a detail: before May 2021 the specification used the number 0.03928 as the switch point of the curve; it is now 0.04045. For colours given as ordinary 8-bit values the two give identical results, because no 8-bit value divided by 255 falls between them: 10 ÷ 255 is 0.0392 and 11 ÷ 255 is 0.0431 (computed).

Where the numbers come from

The W3C explains the thresholds. The page says 3 : 1 is the minimum that two standards recommend for standard text and normal vision. The 4.5 : 1 level allows for a typical loss of contrast sensitivity of about 1.5 times in people with 20/40 vision (3 × 1.5 = 4.5), and the same logic gives about 7 : 1 for 20/80 vision. Large text needs only 3 : 1 because larger, bolder letters are easier to read; “large” means 18 point or 14 point bold, which the page says is about 24 and 18.5 CSS pixels. Text in logos and purely decorative text are exempt, inactive controls too, and the criterion applies to placeholder text and to text that appears on hover or focus.

Why contrast, and not colour, carries the weight

The W3C page makes a point that surprises people. For readers with a colour vision deficiency, it says, hue and saturation have minimal or no effect on how readable text is, and not being able to tell certain shades apart does not harm the perception of light and dark. So the guideline measures only light-dark contrast and treats hue as unimportant. The page also says that colour deficiencies are so varied that no general list of safe colour pairs could be given from the data. That explains why a rule about contrast cannot, on its own, make a game with green and yellow tiles safe: the tiles’ meaning rests on hue, which is just what some people cannot see.

How common is it?

The National Eye Institute says about 1 in 12 men have colour vision deficiency, that most are born with it, and that men are at much higher risk than women. The most common kind makes red and green hard to tell apart, another makes blue and yellow look alike, and in rare cases people see no colour at all. The NEI also says symptoms are often so mild that people do not notice them, and that people adjust, so many people with colour vision deficiency do not know they have it. That matters for design: you cannot count on a player telling you.

Wikipedia gives the figure for people of Northern European descent as 8% of males and 0.5% of females, which is about 1 in 200 for women (computed). Its table lists protanopia and deuteranopia at roughly 1.3% and 1.2% of males, and deuteranomaly, the most common mild form, at about 5%; it flags the table as needing clarification and a citation, so treat the split as approximate. The 8% figure is old: John Dalton’s 1798 paper noted that out of 25 pupils he taught[5], two agreed with his own colour confusions. Two in 25 is 8% (computed).

Our Letter Lock tiles, measured

The post says Letter Lock never relies on colour alone: a correct letter shows a tick and a misplaced letter a dot. Here is what the colours themselves do, measured with the WCAG formula from the colours in our style sheet (computed):

All three text pairs clear the AA level. The last row is a fair and slightly uncomfortable finding. In Day Spark, the dark green and the gold differ in lightness by 3.76 : 1, enough to tell apart if you look. In Night Fizz the green and gold tiles are almost the same lightness, 1.25 : 1. For a player who cannot separate the two hues, nothing about the colour of the tile tells them apart in the dark look, and the tick and the dot are the entire signal. They are doing the work they were added for.

The twist

John Dalton himself had red-green dichromacy, according to the article, and red-green colour blindness is sometimes called daltonism after him; in some languages the word is still the normal one. The first number anyone put on it, about 8%, has hardly moved in more than two hundred years.
