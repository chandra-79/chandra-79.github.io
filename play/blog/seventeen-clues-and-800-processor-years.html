Seventeen clues, 800 processor-years: the hunt for the smallest Sudoku

How few numbers can a Sudoku start with and still have only one solution? Proving the answer took one of the biggest puzzle searches ever run.

Logic games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 28 August 2026

A standard Sudoku starts with a handful of numbers filled in, and has exactly one solution. The obvious question is how few clues you can get away with. For years, hobbyists hunted for puzzles with fewer and fewer. They found many with 17 clues, but nobody ever found one with 16, and nobody could say whether one existed.

The proof, by brute force

In 2012 Gary McGuire, Bastian Tugemann and Gilles Civario settled it: no 16-clue Sudoku has a unique solution, so 17 is the minimum. They did it by reframing the problem as a “hitting set” search over all the essentially different solved grids and then running it on a supercomputer. The computation took about 800 processor-years[1]: one processor running for eight centuries.

A list, not a census

The authors note that a collection of 49,151 known 17-clue puzzles is “almost all” that exist, in the sense that their search kept finding puzzles already on the list. “Close to 50,000” of them is the best way to put it. Nobody has proved the list is complete, so the truthful answer to “how many 17-clue puzzles are there?” is “about that many”.

The twist

The proof leaves a strange fact behind: the hardest question in Sudoku was not about difficulty or technique. It was about existence, and the answer is a negative: something that is not there. Proving absence took far more computing than finding presence ever did, which is true of many puzzles, from chess endgames to the knight’s tour counts.

What it means for the puzzle on your phone

Like every proper Sudoku, each of ours is built (and tested) to have exactly one solution, which is the very property the search depended on. Every time you finish a grid, you are working in a space whose smallest members took the equivalent of eight centuries of computing to map.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Why you cannot go lower than eight digits

Before any supercomputer, there is a short argument that a proper Sudoku must show at least eight of the nine digits[3]. Suppose two digits, say 3 and 7, appeared nowhere among the clues. Then swap every 3 for a 7 and every 7 for a 3 throughout a finished solution. Every row, column and box still holds each digit once, and no clue is disturbed, so the puzzle would have a second solution. The same reasoning for the general n² × n² grid gives at least n² − 1 different digits. It is a floor of 8 for the rule that has to be satisfied before the hard question even starts, and it makes the actual answer of 17 look large.

How many Sudoku grids are there?

The number of filled grids is 6,670,903,752,021,072,936,960[3], about 6.671 × 1021. That is a pleasing round figure to say: more than six sextillion. Most of them are the same grid in disguise. There is a group of moves that keep a grid valid: shuffling rows within a band, columns within a stack, swapping the bands or stacks, reflecting the grid, and relabelling the digits. Counting the grids that cannot be told apart this way gives 5,472,730,538 essentially different solutions[3], a result first computed by Russell and Jarvis in 2005. That is the size of the world the search was organised around: about 5.5 billion grids that are genuinely different, not 6.7 sextillion. (The search was announced in 2012, and the paper appeared in 2014.)

Almost no grid is symmetric

Some grids map onto themselves under some of these moves. There are 26 kinds of such symmetry in all, but they occur in only about 0.005% of filled grids[3]. Symmetric puzzles are also constrained: the fewest clues in a Sudoku with 180° rotational symmetry is believed to be 18, one more than the unconstrained minimum of 17, though that is a belief and not a proof.

The twist

Sudoku has a deeper identity as a famous problem. It can be written as colouring the 81 cells of a graph with nine colours, or as an exact-cover problem with 324 conditions to satisfy (every cell filled, and every digit placed once in each row, column and box). Setting a good puzzle is closely related to a problem called ANOTHER SOLUTION, which asks whether a second solution exists, and that problem is complete in its class. The puzzle you solve in five minutes belongs to a family that mathematicians study in earnest.
