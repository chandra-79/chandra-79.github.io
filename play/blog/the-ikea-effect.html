Why the answer you found tastes better than the one you’re given

People paid 63% more for a storage box they’d built themselves. Then researchers tried making them wreck it.

Everyone has a crooked bookshelf they refuse to throw away. It’s not a good bookshelf. But you built it, with your own hands, a tiny Allen key and some strong words. Psychologists have a name for that attachment, and yes, it’s named after the shop.

The experiments

Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely ran a set of studies on what they called the IKEA effect, published in 2011–2012.

  • Furniture: people who assembled a simple IKEA storage box were willing to pay 63% more[1] for it than people who were handed a ready-built one.
  • Origami: people who folded their own origami valued it about five times as much as onlookers did. Onlookers valued origami made by experts about as highly as the builders valued their own wobbly creations. The builders thought other people would pay good money too. They were mistaken.
  • Finishing matters: the effect only appeared when people completed the task. When they built something and then took it apart, or didn’t finish, the extra love disappeared.

What it means for puzzles

A solved puzzle is a little piece of furniture you built in your head. The answer you dug out yourself feels better than the same answer handed to you, which is a big part of why puzzles are satisfying at all.

It’s also why our hints cost something. In Word Cloud, every extra clue or letter costs sparks. In the crossword, you get three hints, not thirty. We’re not being stingy. We’re protecting the good feeling at the end. If the game did all the work, the answer would be IKEA’s box, delivered fully assembled, and you’d feel nothing about it.

But please don’t suffer

The study’s last finding cuts the other way: the effect needs you to finish. Failing and giving up removes the glow. So if you’re genuinely stuck, take a hint. A finished puzzle with one hint beats an abandoned one with none. That’s also why Word Cloud has a “Show the word” button, and why using it never breaks your streak.

Bonus life tip

If you want people to love a plan, let them help build it. If you want to love your own work, finish it, even imperfectly. The crooked bookshelf is proof.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Where the idea came from

The Wikipedia article says Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon and Dan Ariely named and published the effect in 2011[2]. They did not start from nothing. They cited earlier work on “effort justification”, the finding that the more effort someone puts into something, the more they value it, which Leon Festinger observed in 1957. A well-known 1959 study by Aronson and Mills had women go through no, mild or severe initiation before joining a discussion group; their later rating of the group rose with the effort demanded. Product designers had known the effect long before it had a name. The authors point to Build-a-Bear, where customers pay a high price to make their own teddy bear, and to “haycations”, in which city people pay to do farm work.

They also gave an account of why. They cited a basic need for effectance, the ability to produce desired outcomes in one’s environment, and Albert Bandura’s 1977 finding that completing tasks is a main way people feel competent and in control. Building something is a way of meeting that need, so we like what we built.

The three experiments

  • 63%more willing to pay for a box they built
  • about 5×builders’ value of their origami, against non-builders’
  • 3kinds of task: IKEA boxes, origami and Lego

The origami result has a sting in it. Builders thought other people would value their folds as highly as they did, and the authors wrote that participants saw their amateurish creations as similar in value to the experts’. The last finding is the one the post relies on: when builders made something and then destroyed it, or failed to finish it, the effect went away. In the authors’ words, labour leads to love only when it ends in a successfully completed task.

Why it happens, and when it fails

The article lists three mechanisms from the original paper. Competence, or effectance, has two parts: actual control over the outcome, and merely perceived control, and the experiments, where people followed preset instructions, show both. Social utility matters for things people want to show off, such as Lego and origami, and less for plain storage boxes. And enjoyment: the more fun the building, the higher the valuation. A study of customised products by Ling and colleagues found that the effect is stronger among consumers who like creativity and originality and among those with high self-esteem.

There are limits. Dahl and Moreau (2007), the article says, found that customers are happier when there is a limit to how much creativity they have to supply. Gibbs and Drolet (2008) found that energised consumers choose more effortful experiences, but companies are warned not to set tasks so hard that people cannot finish and end up dissatisfied. In 2016 Sarstedt and colleagues replicated the effect with loom bands[2]: people who made a band, with instructions, were willing to pay more and felt more ownership than people who only chose a ready-made one. But telling them that a company would consider their design did not add much more.

What it means for Ziggle’s hints

The post argues that a hint costs something so that a solved puzzle feels like yours. The research supports the logic and also tells us where the limit is: the glow needs a finished task. A puzzle abandoned in frustration gives no reward, which is why a hint that gets you across the line is a good trade. It also suggests that the effort is not only the solving. Our reading: choosing a theme or a level is a small act of customisation, and the article’s warning applies there too, since too many choices are not always happier.

The twist

The original authors’ own summary is the one to remember: even assembling a standard bureau, a hard and solitary job, can lead people to overvalue what is often a poorly built result.
