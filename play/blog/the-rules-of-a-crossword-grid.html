The rules of a crossword grid: who decided 15 by 15?

A daily crossword is a small square of black and white with a surprising amount of law behind it, much of it written by one woman in the 1940s.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 5 November 2026

The crossword in The New York Times is 15 squares by 15[1] on weekdays and 21 by 21 on Sundays. A themed weekday puzzle may hold at most 78 words, a Sunday puzzle 140, and a Friday or Saturday puzzle with no theme 72. Where did these rules come from?

Margaret Farrar’s rules

The Times began its crossword in 1942, and its first editor, Margaret Farrar, edited it until 1969. She wrote many of the rules that crossword makers still follow: the grid itself, a limit on black squares, a minimum word length of three letters[1], grids with 180° rotational symmetry and an odd number of squares on each side, and no unchecked squares, so every letter belongs to both an across and a down answer.

The Sunday grid has nearly twice the squares of a weekday (441 against 225) but less than twice the words (140 against 78), so on average each Sunday answer has a little more room. Take away the theme on a Friday and the word cap drops by six, which leaves the construction more open: fewer, longer answers.

The twist

The rotational symmetry rule is purely aesthetic. Turn a grid upside down and it looks the same, which makes it feel orderly, but it does nothing for the solver. Constructors keep it because the grid is a small piece of design, and solvers have come to expect it, which is how many standards are born: by habit, then by habit-breaking being noticed.

And Ziggle’s?

Ours are much smaller, built fresh from themed lists, so one fits in a coffee break. We keep the principle that every letter is checked, so a stuck clue is always helped by its neighbours. That is the part of Farrar’s rulebook we would not give up.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The rest of the rulebook

The size limits are the part everybody notices. The same Times conventions govern the content too:

  • Themes. Monday to Thursday puzzles, and usually Sunday’s, have a theme: a set of long answers sharing a trick.
  • No answer in its own clue. The answer word may not appear in the clue itself.
  • Same degree. A clue containing a comparative or superlative must have an answer of the same kind: “Most difficult” for TOUGHEST.
  • What can be an answer. Popular proper nouns, brand names, abbreviations and even symbols are allowed. Profanity and disturbing subjects are not, although the odd slang word has slipped through.
  • Capital letters. All proper nouns are capitalised in clues wherever they fall, which can be used to mislead: a clue such as “John, for one” could be a name or a bathroom.

Notice how much of this is about fairness to the solver. The answer is never given away by the clue, a clue and its answer match in form, and the only legitimate ambiguity is a deliberate one.

The records, and how small the rooms are

  • 119[2]Sunday puzzles by Jack Luzzatto, the most of any constructor
  • 13the age of the youngest constructor, Harrison Walden, at his first solo puzzle
  • 62the fewest words in any debut puzzle (Ari Richter, a Saturday in 2019)
  • 67 × 41the grid of the largest Times crossword, in 2022

That 2022 grid has 2,747 squares (computed), more than six times the 441 of a Sunday puzzle. At the other end, the debut with 62 words sits ten under the 72-word cap for an unthemed Friday or Saturday. The rules fix the edges of the form, and constructors play at the limits.

A rule that changed in our lifetimes

Symmetry sounds like one of the oldest of the rules, and it has bent. Starting in January 2020, diagonal symmetry began to appear in Friday and Saturday puzzles. It is a small change, but it shows that a convention that feels as fixed as the shape of the grid is still a choice that editors can revisit.

The twist

The first daily puzzle was published without an author line, and as of 2001 nobody knew who wrote the first weekday Times crossword. A form that now credits every constructor, and keeps records of the youngest, the busiest and the sparsest, began its working life anonymously.
