QWERTY: the keyboard layout nobody chose on purpose

A newspaper editor, five years of fiddling, a manufacturing deal, and the myth that it was designed to slow you down.

Typing · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 13 October 2026

Look down at your keyboard. Q, W, E, R, T, Y. Nobody would design it that way from scratch. So who did, and why are we all still using it?

A newspaper editor’s tinkering

The layout comes from Christopher Latham Sholes, a newspaper editor and printer in Kenosha, Wisconsin, who was working on early typewriters. Over about five years of trial and error he moved the keys around, starting from a plain alphabetical arrangement.

In 1873, his backer James Densmore sold the manufacturing rights to E. Remington and Sons. Remington’s mechanics finished the layout within a few months. One of their changes was moving R to where the full stop used to be.

Three myths, gently retired

  • “It was designed to stop the keys jamming.” This is the famous story, and it may be invented or simply wrong. Other machines of the time avoided clashing typebars altogether, so there was no strict technical need for it.
  • “It was designed to slow typists down.” Not so. Splitting common letter pairs across the keyboard encourages your hands to take turns, which may actually help speed.
  • “Salesmen could type TYPE WRITER from the top row.” They can, and it’s a great story, but there’s no solid evidence it was the reason for the layout.

So is Dvorak better?

Later, August Dvorak designed a rival layout with the most common letters on the home row, and his studies said it was faster. Those studies are now widely seen as flawed, or worse. The economists Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis looked at the evidence and concluded that the rigorous studies are inconclusive.

The biggest typing study we know of points the same way. Across 168,000 people, the fastest typists weren’t the ones who’d taken a formal course. They were the ones who made fewer mistakes.

Our view

Use whatever layout you like. If you’re curious about Dvorak or Colemak, Ziggle’s typing trainer supports them, along with QWERTY, QWERTZ and AZERTY. The first three levels follow your layout’s own home row. But if you just want to type faster, switching layouts is a slow way to do it. Accuracy, not looking at your fingers, and a few minutes of practice a day will take you further, and you won’t have to relearn where the Q went.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The five-year shuffle, step by step

Sholes, with Carlos Glidden and Samuel Soulé, filed his first patent application in October 1867[2]. The machine he built had a piano-like keyboard with two rows of keys in alphabetical order. Over the next five years he moved the letters around by trial and error:

  1. Oct 1867Sholes, Glidden and Soulé file the patent for an early writing machine. Its two rows of keys run A to M and N to Z.
  2. Nov 1868Sholes reverses the second half of the alphabet, N to Z, to run right to left.
  3. Apr 1870A four-row keyboard close to today’s, with the vowels A, E, I, O, U and Y moved to the upper row.
  4. 1873James Densmore sells the manufacturing rights to E. Remington and Sons, whose mechanics finish the layout within months. One of their changes puts R where the full stop had been.
  5. 1874The Sholes and Glidden typewriter goes on sale through Remington.
  6. 1878The Remington No. 2 is the first with a Shift key for upper and lower case, and QWERTY spreads with its success.

Even the finished layout has fossils. The home row sequence DFGHJKL is what is left of the alphabet, and early machines had no 0 or 1 key: they were “redundant”, and typists used the capital I (or small l) for one and the capital O for zero. Some typewriters were still missing the 1 into the 1970s. The slanted columns of keys are a fossil too: they exist so that the levers behind each key do not collide, and every keyboard since has kept them, though some, such as the Kinesis and TypeMatrix, straighten them into vertical columns.

Why those letters? Two guesses compete. One credits a study of letter-pair frequency by the educator Amos Densmore, brother of the backer. The other says the groupings came from American Morse telegraph operators, who noted that E and R, a very common pair, belonged side by side. The article treats the first as a conjecture that some consider unfounded.

Why jamming cannot have been the whole story

Typewriters of the 1870s and 80s did not need QWERTY to avoid clashes. Edison’s 1872 print-wheel device, Crandall’s 1883 typewriter with its cylinder sleeve, the Hammond of 1885 with its shuttle and the Blickensderfer of 1893 with its type wheel had no colliding typebars at all. The Blickensderfer’s home row spelled DHIATENSOR, ten letters said to make up 70% of English words. So the article concludes that the layout had no particular technological requirement, and the jamming story is at best a partial explanation.

What QWERTY gets right, on our own word list

The layout is not the best that could be designed, but a few properties are good ones. It alternates hands often, which lets one hand prepare while the other types. Thousands of English words can be typed with the left hand alone, against only a couple of hundred with the right. And the top row holds all ten letters of TYPEWRITER (computed: T, Y, P, E, W, R, I, T, E, R), which is the origin of the salesman story, though the article says it cannot be substantiated.

We counted the letters of Ziggle’s 809 five-letter Letter Lock answers (computed, with the usual finger split: Q to T, A to G and Z to B on the left hand, the rest on the right):

On our list, 51.4% of the letters are on the top row, only 33.4% on the home row and 15.2% on the bottom. The home row, where your fingers rest, holds only A as a vowel, so most words send your hands upwards. About 50.2% of consecutive letter pairs are typed by the same hand, so roughly half the time the other hand is free to get ready. And 57 of our 809 answers (7.0%) can be typed with the left hand alone.

Other layouts, and why they did not win

The two best-known alternatives are Dvorak and Colemak, the second based partly on QWERTY and claimed to be easier for a QWERTY typist to learn. In Europe the QWERTZ (German) and AZERTY (French) variants dominate. German moved Z between T and U to make the common pairs TZ and ZU easy. As the post says, the practical point is to practise the layout you have.

The twist

On “half QWERTY” phones such as the BlackBerry Pearl (2006) and the Nokia E55 (2009), two letters share a key: press once for Q, twice for W. The layout nobody chose has even been squeezed onto phone keypads.
