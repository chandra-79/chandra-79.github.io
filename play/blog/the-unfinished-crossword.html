Why the unfinished crossword nags you

A waiter, a famous 1927 study, and a 2025 meta-analysis that politely took half of it apart.

Science of practice · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 25 August 2026

The story starts in a restaurant. The psychologist Kurt Lewin noticed that a waiter could remember every detail of the orders that hadn’t been paid yet. Once a table settled up, the details seemed to vanish from his head.

Lewin’s student Bluma Zeigarnik turned the observation into experiments, and in 1927 she published a report that made her name a household word, at least in households with a lot of psychology books. The Zeigarnik effect is the idea that unfinished tasks stick in memory better than finished ones, because an open task leaves a kind of mental tension behind.

The 2025 plot twist

Famous findings deserve re-checking, and in 2025 the researchers Ghibellini and Meier did exactly that. They pooled studies of the Zeigarnik effect and its lesser-known sibling, the Ovsiankina effect: the tendency to go back and resume something that was interrupted.

The result split neatly in two:

  • They found no reliable memory advantage for unfinished tasks. The Zeigarnik effect, in its classic form, “lacks universal validity”.
  • But they did find a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks. The Ovsiankina effect held up.

So the itch is real, even if the memory part isn’t. You might not remember your half-done crossword any better. You are, however, more likely to want to go back and finish it.

Using the itch on purpose

  • Stop mid-puzzle when you need to. Ziggle saves every game as you go: the letters you’ve placed, the clues you’ve revealed, the arrows you’ve cleared. The pull to resume does the rest.
  • Leave a hard thing slightly unfinished. Some writers swear by this: stop mid-sentence, and starting again tomorrow is easy. It works for practice sessions too.
  • Close the loops you don’t want. If an open task is nagging you at bedtime, write the next step on paper. You’ve resumed it, a little, and you can rest.

And if your unfinished Sudoku is still bothering you tomorrow, that’s not a character flaw. It’s Ovsiankina, and you can tell her we said hello.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the 2025 meta-analysis did

The paper is open access in[2] Humanities and Social Sciences Communications. Its authors searched two psychology databases on 14 September 2023 and found 1,455 publications, 1,349 after duplicates. Title and abstract screening left 124, and full-text screening left 59: 38 on the Zeigarnik effect, 20 on the Ovsiankina effect and one on both. They kept only studies in the original format, in which several tasks are presented, half are interrupted and half completed, and then recall or resumption is measured. A second rater checked 60 of the screened studies and agreed on every one (κ = 1.00).

Three ways to measure memory, and why they differ

The authors say the measure Zeigarnik used is heavily influenced by extreme values and overstates the effect. They recommend a different one, introduced by Marrow in 1938: the average proportion of recalled tasks that were interrupted, because it also takes account of the finished tasks. By that measure, interrupted tasks made up 49.2% of what people recalled, a hair under half.

The atmosphere of the experiment mattered

Achievement motivation did not rescue the effect: high 1.00, moderate 0.92, low 0.96 (seven studies). The authors suggest the effect depends on situations and people, such as the experimenter’s authority and how involved participants are, and that those conditions were more common in early experiments than in later ones.

The effect that held up

  • 67%of interrupted tasks resumed (21 publications)
  • 50%the chance level
  • 20publications without Ovsiankina’s own data

The resumption rate was 67.0% with Ovsiankina’s 1928 data and 66.8% without it, well above the 50% expected by chance. The authors add a caution: the Ovsiankina studies were mostly analysed descriptively, as percentages, so the effect size is less well pinned down than the Zeigarnik effect’s.

The best result for the memory effect is not what it seems

The study in the meta-analysis with the biggest memory advantage was Baddeley’s of 1963, with anagrams. Participants had 60 seconds, and if they failed the solution was shown. Then they recalled the solutions. The authors argue that this is not the classic setting: the unsolved anagrams were interrupted but the intention to solve them ended when the answer appeared, and Baddeley himself called the result “Zeigarnik-like”.

How the story started

  1. 1926Lewin proposes that an intention builds a tension that lasts until it is fulfilled.
  2. 1927Zeigarnik reports better recall of interrupted tasks.
  3. 1928Ovsiankina reports that people resume interrupted tasks unprompted.
  4. 1968Van Bergen publishes an in-depth replication of the original study.
  5. 2025Ghibellini and Meier publish the meta-analysis.

Wikipedia’s article on the Zeigarnik effect gives the restaurant story the post tells (Lewin noticing that a waiter remembered unpaid orders and forgot paid ones) and notes that several replications in other countries, Van Bergen’s among them, failed to find a difference. Our reading of the whole record: the itch to go back is the sturdy part, and the memory boost is real only in particular conditions.

The twist

The tendency that survived is the one nobody had heard of. A paper that set out to test the famous effect found, at the end, a quiet result that was there all along: in these lab studies about two people in three picked an interrupted task back up when they were given the chance.
