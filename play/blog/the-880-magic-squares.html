There are exactly 880 four-by-four magic squares

A French civil servant counted them by hand in the seventeenth century. We checked it in thirty seconds, and found 7,040.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 25 September 2026

A magic square is a grid of different numbers whose rows, columns and both diagonals add to the same total. For the numbers 1 to 9 in a 3×3 grid, that total is 15, and there is only one square, apart from turning it over or round: the ancient Chinese Lo Shu. For 1 to 16 in a 4×4 grid, the total is 34, and the question “how many are there?” was answered in 1693.

880, counted by hand

Bernard Frénicle de Bessy showed there are exactly 880 distinct 4×4 magic squares[1]. His work was published posthumously, twenty years after his death. We re-counted by computer: trying every arrangement of 1 to 16 with pruning takes about half a minute, and finds 8 arrangements of the 3×3 and 7,040 of the 4×4. The first is Lo Shu in its 8 turns and reflections; the second is 880 × 8, which is exactly Frénicle’s count with every rotation and reflection counted separately.

A square in a famous engraving

The most famous 4×4 square is in the corner of Albrecht Dürer’s engraving Melencolia I (1514)[1]. Its magic total is 34, and the two middle cells of the bottom row read 15 and 14, the year of the print.

The twist

The jump from 880 to 275 million is the real lesson. The order-four count was done by hand in the seventeenth century; the order-five count needed computers. Puzzle difficulty and counting difficulty are different things, and a puzzle can have millions of answers while still feeling impossible to start.

What this has to do with Sudoku

A Sudoku grid is a close relative: a square of numbers with a sum-like rule on rows, columns and boxes. The same trick of pruning, abandoning a partial grid the moment a rule is broken, is how both the magic-square count and a Sudoku solver stay fast.

A long road from China to Dürer

Magic squares are among the oldest puzzles in the world, and they were found independently in several places. A timeline from the Wikipedia history:

  1. 190 BCE (at least)The third-order square, the Lo Shu, was known to Chinese mathematicians; the article flags the exact date as needing a citation, and says it was explicitly given by the first century of the common era.
  2. 550 CEThe first dateable fourth-order magic square, in India.
  3. around 983Squares of order 3 to 9 appear in an encyclopedia written in Baghdad, the Encyclopedia of the Brethren of Purity.
  4. 1356In India, Narayana enumerates all the fourth-order pandiagonal magic squares.
  5. 1514Albrecht Dürer includes an order-4 square, with magic sum 34, in Melencolia I.
  6. 1593Cheng Dawei’s Suanfa tongzong prints a 9 × 9 magic square.
  7. 1683Seki Takakazu devotes one of his Seven Books to magic squares and circles; the same year Yoshizane Tanaka builds a fourth-order magic cube.
  8. 1693Frénicle’s count of 880 fourth-order squares is published, twenty years after his death.

More than one meaning of “magic”

The squares in this post are normal (each number from 1 to n² used once) and magic along both diagonals. Other families relax or add rules. A semimagic square needs only the rows and columns to add up, which is the property of one celebrated knight’s tour; a pandiagonal square also adds up along every broken diagonal, the kind Narayana enumerated. In the Wikipedia article’s own words, magic squares have a history “dating back to at least 190 BCE in China”[1].

The twist

The same arithmetic curiosity was used for very different purposes in different places: poetry and puzzles in Japan, charms alongside magic letters in medieval Islam, and, in one Indian text, a recipe for making perfumes from four of sixteen ingredients. Whenever people found a grid that adds up in every direction, they assumed it meant something.
