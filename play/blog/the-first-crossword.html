The first crossword was a diamond

In December 1913 a Liverpool-born editor in New York filled a gap on a puzzle page. A typo gave it its name.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 16 December 2025

On 21 December 1913, the New York World’s Sunday “Fun” supplement printed something new. Arthur Wynne, the editor of that page, had drawn a diamond-shaped grid of white squares, numbered the starts of each run, and written a clue for each one. He called it a “Word-Cross”.

Within a few weeks a typesetter had swapped the words round, and “Cross-Word” stuck. The hyphen took longer to fall off.

From novelty to craze

For about a decade the crossword was mostly a newspaper filler. Then, in 1924, a young publishing firm called Simon & Schuster released a book of them, with a pencil attached. It sold so well that crosswords became a national craze in the United States, and spread quickly to Britain, where setters went on to develop the famously sly cryptic style.

What we kept, and what we changed

Wynne’s puzzle already had the parts that matter: a grid where answers cross, so solving one word hands you letters for another. That “crossing help” is the heart of Ziggle’s crossword too. You are never stuck on one clue for long, because its neighbours keep chipping in letters.

What we changed is size and tone. A classic newspaper grid can take an hour. Ours are small, built fresh from themed word lists, with friendly clues, so a puzzle fits in a coffee break. On easy, a wrong letter shows the moment you type it; on hard you only find out when you press Check, the way a paper puzzle works.

Over a century on, the basic trick is the same: a grid, some numbers, and the small click when two answers agree on a shared letter.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The man behind the diamond

Arthur Wynne was born in Liverpool on 22 June 1871. His father edited the Liverpool Mercury. At 19 he emigrated to the United States, on 6 June 1891, and settled for a time in Pittsburgh, where he worked on the Pittsburgh Press and played the violin in the Pittsburgh Symphony Orchestra. He later moved to New York and the New York World, living in Cedar Grove, New Jersey. He became a naturalised American in the 1920s and died in Clearwater, Florida, on 14 January 1945.

What was new in 1913

The puzzle that ran in the World on 21 December 1913 was a diamond with a hollow centre and the letters F-U-N already filled in. Wynne called it a “Word-Cross Puzzle”. It rested on an earlier form, the word diamond, but he added a handful of ideas: drawing horizontal and vertical lines to make boxes for the letters, and, soon after, using black squares in a symmetrical pattern to split the words. Apart from the numbering scheme, the article says, the form of Wynne’s puzzles is the one modern crosswords use. A few weeks after the first one, a typesetting error turned “Word-Cross” into “Cross-Word”[1], and the name stayed.

  1. 21 Dec 1913The first “Word-Cross” appears in the New York World’s Sunday “Fun” section.
  2. 1920sThe first crosswords with strictly cryptic clues appear in Britain, pioneered by Edward Powys Mathers, who starts a series of 670 cryptics for The Observer that lasts until his death in 1939 under the pen name Torquemada.
  3. 1944Allied security officers are alarmed to find the secret code names of Operation Overlord, the D-Day landings, among the answers in The Daily Telegraph’s crosswords. Some Bletchley Park recruits had been picked after a crossword-solving competition.
  4. 1958Harold T. Bers, an advertising executive, invents the themed crossword.
  5. 1968Stephen Sondheim brings the cryptic crossword to the US in New York magazine, where it never becomes widespread.
  6. 20 Dec 2013A Google Doodle marks the 100th anniversary of the first puzzle, with a crossword by Merl Reagle.

How a modern American grid is built

Newspaper puzzles come in a few standard sizes: a weekday grid is usually 15 × 15 (225 squares), and the bigger weekend ones 21 × 21 (441 squares), 23 × 23 or 25 × 25. A constructor picks a theme, places the theme answers in a grid that is normally rotationally symmetric, fills the rest, and writes the clues; software now does much of the filling. The New York Times set another habit: puzzles get harder through the week, from Monday (easiest) to Saturday, with the larger Sunday puzzle about as hard as a Thursday. Solvers use the weekday as shorthand, so an easy puzzle is “a Monday”.

  • 50words, the fewest ever in a 15 × 15 weekday puzzle (June 2013)
  • 17black squares, the fewest in a 15 × 15 US grid, leaving 208 white
  • 66,666crosswords by Roger Squires, the most prolific compiler (Guinness, 2007)

Those records are all from the article: Joe Krozel’s New York Times puzzle of 29 June 2013 used just 50 words, and his of 27 July 2012 had only 17 black squares. Roger Squires of Shropshire published his 66,666th crossword on 14 May 2007, about two million clues in all, and holds the record for the longest word in a published grid: the 58-letter Welsh town name Llanfairpwllgwyngyll…, clued as an anagram. A person who builds or solves crosswords is a “cruciverbalist”, a word that appears to have been coined in the 1970s from Latin roots for “cross” and “word”.

The grammar of a clue

The conventions are simple to learn and are why a clue’s answer can be checked by the crossing words. A clue and its answer must agree in tense, number and degree, so “Traveled on horseback” is RODE but not RIDE, and “Family members” is AUNTS but not UNCLE. A question mark usually signals a pun (“Grateful?” for ASHES, since a grate might be full of them). Fill-in-the-blank clues are the easiest, which is why they are a good place to start. And a short clue like “PC key” could be ESC, ALT, TAB, DEL or INS; only the crossing letters tell you which. That last point is the same “crossing help” the post describes.

The twist

The names of the great British cryptic setters follow a theme: Torquemada, after the first Grand Inquisitor of Spain, then Ximenes (the Anglicised surname of Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros, a Grand Inquisitor of Castile), then Azed, which is Deza, another Grand Inquisitor, spelled backwards. Each pen name promises a puzzle that will put you to the question.
