Ten study tricks, ranked: the highlighter came last

A 55-page review judged ten popular ways to study. Two came out on top, and neither is the one most students reach for.

Science of practice · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 2 November 2026

Every September the stationery aisle fills with highlighters. In 2013 a team of five psychologists asked a blunt question about that aisle: which study techniques actually work? Their answer filled 55 pages[1] of Psychological Science in the Public Interest, and it graded ten techniques as high, moderate or low utility.

The verdict, in one table

The part that stings

The reviewers say some techniques were included precisely because students lean on them: highlighting and rereading are named in the abstract as ones students “report relying heavily on”. In other words, the most popular techniques were put under the microscope because they were popular, and they did not come out well. The summary from the journal’s publisher is blunt: these techniques “often resulted in inconsistent gains”[2].

The twist

The two winners are the two that feel worst while you are doing them. Rereading feels smooth, which makes it feel like learning; trying to recall something and failing feels like the opposite. Karpicke and Blunt’s students show it in miniature: most expected the easier method to work as well, and it did not.

Where Ziggle sits on the list

A puzzle is practice testing in disguise: the answer is not on the page, so you have to pull it out of your head. Dailies arrive once a day, which spaces your practice for free, and the trainers schedule reviews on a widening gap. Mixing categories covers the interleaving. None of that makes you cleverer in general, which is a different claim and a far weaker one; it just means the games lean on the techniques with the best evidence.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Testing yourself is an old idea

The review ranked practice testing near the top, but it did not invent the idea. Francis Bacon, in The New Organon[3], compared two ways of getting words back: if we recover them one way “we shall probably know them the next time”, and if the other, “we shall very likely need the book once more”. The first, in his account, is the way of trying to recall. The first documented experiments on what is now called the testing effect were published by Edwina Abbott in 1909[3]. In 1932 C. Mace wrote that when every reading is followed by an attempt to recall the items, the efficiency of learning and retention is enormously enhanced.

The history of the neighbouring idea, spaced repetition, has the same shape: an early result (Mace in 1932, Spitzer in 1939) that “went unnoticed” for decades. It took until Dunlosky and colleagues in 2013 for the evidence on ten techniques to be weighed side by side.

Why flashcards work, in one idea

Flashcards are the classic application of the testing effect: the card makes you pull the answer out of memory instead of looking at it. Combine them with spaced review and you have the idea behind the Leitner system and flashcard apps. The same logic is why a puzzle is a better way to hold a fact than a summary of it.

How the grades were decided

A teaching guide from the University of New Brunswick[4], which summarises the review with page references, says the techniques were rated on whether they improve performance across many kinds of materials, learning conditions, student characteristics and criterion tasks. That is why a technique can work in a study and still be rated low: the rating asks how far the benefit travels, not whether it ever appears.

Our reading: the interleaving figure matches the 63% against 20% in the volume-formula experiment of Rohrer and Taylor (2007) discussed in the mixing post, a 43-point gap, which would make it the same study. The review’s own count of the pages is easy to check: pages 4 to 58 make 55 (computed), the number in the post.

The two the review says to use more

For students the practical result is plain: replace rereading with testing yourself, and replace one long session with several short ones. The summary notes that practice tests of any format help, and that tests asking you to produce the answer do better than tests asking you to recognise it. The Association for Psychological Science’s account adds that the high-utility pair help students of many ages and abilities and across many subjects, while underlining, rereading and mnemonic devices gave inconsistent gains.

The twist

The technique the review put at the top was being measured in a laboratory in 1909, more than a century before the review. Nobody was waiting for a new discovery: students were waiting for a good reason to stop highlighting.
