No ads over the board

The rules Ziggle follows about advertising: where a promotion can sit, and what it never does.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 30 January 2026

In this article 7 sections

Anyone who has played a free puzzle game on a phone knows the moment: you finish a level, reach for the next one, and a full-screen video starts, with a tiny close button that appears after a few seconds.

The online ad industry itself has rules about this. The Coalition for Better Ads publishes “Better Ads Standards” listing ad formats that people find most annoying, including pop-ups and ads that take over the screen with a countdown. Browsers such as Chrome have used those standards to filter ads on sites that break them.

Ziggle’s rules

These rules are written into the code and checked by tests:

  • Never over a board. Ad spaces sit beside or below the game, at standard sizes, and never cover anything you are playing.
  • Never in kids mode. Switch it on and every ad space disappears.
  • No tracking. Nothing is loaded from another site, and nothing about you is used to choose a card. A card that links elsewhere says “Ad” and opens that site only when you tap it.
  • Never sold for progress. Lives and credits are earned by playing; they are not for sale, and an ad never buys one.

Why say this out loud?

Rules that are written down, and tested, are harder to bend by accident.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the Coalition measured

The Coalition for Better Ads is an industry group, and its Better Ads Standards are a list of ad experiences that fall below a threshold of consumer acceptability and are the most likely to drive people to install ad blockers. According to its page, more than 150,000 consumers took part in the research[1] behind the list. The Coalition says it deliberately focused on the least-preferred experiences, which leaves room for new formats to be tried. So the standards are a floor, not a recommendation: they say what to avoid, not what to build.

The page defines an “ad” for this purpose as promotional content displayed on the web as the result of a commercial transaction with a third party. It then lists the formats in four groups. We counted them (computed from the page):

Look at what the formats have in common. Most of them take something from you: your time (a countdown before the page, a pre-roll you cannot skip), your screen (a full-screen scrollover, a large sticky ad), your attention (flashing, animated, autoplaying with sound) or your place (a pop-up on top of what you were doing). A puzzle board is something you are holding in your head. The cost of an ad over a board is not a nuisance; it is a lost position.

How Ziggle’s rules map onto that

The rules in the post are written into the code and checked by tests, and the code is plain enough to read:

  • Static cards, nothing else. The ad-slot code only places cards in the page. It contains no video, no pop-up and no countdown; its few timers only redo the layout when the window changes.
  • Gone while you play. The style sheet hides every ad slot whenever a game is active, so nothing sits on or beside a board in play. They show on the home page, the blog and around a game before and after you play, and they are hidden in kids mode.
  • Standard shapes. A banner 728 by 90 pixels on wide screens, 320 by 50 on phones, and a 160 by 600 rail beside a game on wide screens. The banner under a game can grow into the room left above the footer, up to three rows of two cards, but only when the page would not otherwise scroll.
  • Labelled, and only a link. A card that leads to another site says “Ad”, and the link is loaded and followed only if you tap it. The sponsor’s card carries a campaign tag in its address and never anything about the player.
  • Never sold for progress. Lives and credits are earned by playing. An ad never buys one, and money never does.

The one card in a popup

There is one place where a card shows up in something that looks like an interruption, and we should be plain about it: the finished-level popup. After a win, the popup can carry one labelled card. The rule in the code is that there is none on the first win of a visit and then one win in three. The popup itself is the shared one every level ends with, saying what happened and offering a way on, and the card is added after it has opened, with focus already on the main button, so it never stands between you and Next. It is the nearest Ziggle comes to the Coalition’s interstitials, and it is the reason the rule says “never over a board” and not “never anywhere”.

Who the cards are for

Under the Coalition’s definition an ad needs a commercial transaction with a third party. Ziggle’s cards are mostly its own: posts and games from the site. A labelled “Ad” card appears about every other slot-day, chosen by the date, for house sites that Ziggle’s makers promote. We label them anyway, because a reader should know when a card is promotion. Nothing is loaded from other sites, the strict security rules for the site would block it, and a test checks the pages.

The twist

The Coalition’s standards are built so that new formats can still be invented, since they only mark the worst. Ziggle’s rule goes the other way: a few things are ruled out in advance, such as anything over a board, not because they have been measured as annoying but because a puzzle is one of the few places where being left alone is the product.
