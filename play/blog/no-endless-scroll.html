Why Ziggle has an ending

The inventor of infinite scroll has said he regrets it. Every Ziggle page, and every day, is built to finish.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 27 February 2026

Infinite scroll, the feed that loads more as you reach the bottom, has a disputed parentage. A patent filed in February 2006[1] by Microsoft’s Windows Live Image Search team describes it, and the first product to use it shipped in March 2006. The designer Aza Raskin has also said he invented it, though Wikipedia notes there is no evidence for that claim. What nobody disputes is the effect: a page with no bottom gives you no natural moment to stop.

Built-in stopping points

Ziggle is designed the other way round. There are natural ends everywhere:

  • The daily set ends. Eight dailies, then a “perfect day”. Nothing new appears until tomorrow.
  • Every level ends with a pause. A popup says what happened and offers a way on, so continuing is a choice, not a slide.
  • Pages end. The blog is split into pages with numbers, not an endless river of posts. Lists stop, and the footer sits where a footer should.

But isn’t more play good for you?

We would rather you came back tomorrow because you enjoyed today than stayed an hour because nothing told you to stop. Practice modes are there for anyone who wants more, and the streak counts a day the moment you finish any one daily.

A puzzle is satisfying partly because it finishes. We wanted the whole site to feel like that.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Where it came from

The Wikipedia article gives the Microsoft story in some detail. A US patent application from the Windows Live Image Search team (now Bing) was filed in February 2006, and it contains the first recorded mention of an “infinite-scroll” component. The first product with infinite scroll was released on 8 March 2006. Hugh Williams, then the engineering leader for image search, has said that he, Julie Farago and Nick Craswell were the inventors. The article says Aza Raskin claimed in 2019 to have invented it, but that there is no evidence he created earlier work before Microsoft’s filing. (The article also says the feature was built and tested through 2005 alongside a thumbnail-size slider and a scratchpad for saving images, but marks that as needing a source, so treat it as unverified.)

  1. Feb 2006Microsoft’s Windows Live Image Search team files the patent that names an “infinite-scroll” component.
  2. 8 Mar 2006The first product to ship with infinite scroll: Image Search.
  3. 2019Aza Raskin claims to have invented it. The article finds no evidence for earlier work.
  4. 2019A smartphone study names infinite scrolling, which lacks stopping cues, as one way apps are designed to prolong use.

Three ways to build a list

Infinite scroll is not one design but a family. The article lists a few variants. One adds a “load more” button, which gives the reader more control over what loads, and the article names Google Search as an example. Another adds pagination to the scroll, so the reader can see how far down they are, which the article illustrates with the mobile site of Google Shopping. Pure infinite scroll has neither the button nor the count. The difference is in the stopping cues: a button asks for a decision, a page number says where you are, and a bare scroll gives you no moment to decide anything.

What the research does and does not show

The article says usability research suggests infinite scrolling can be an accessibility problem, though it marks that passage as needing an example. It also says the lack of stopping cues has been described as a pathway to smartphone addiction and social media addiction, and cites two papers for it. We read one of them. It is a 2019 study in Computers in Human Behavior that logged every tap, scroll and keystroke on the phones of 64 people over eight weeks[2]. It found that smartphone addiction scores were linked to heavier use of lifestyle apps and social apps, and especially to interaction with Snapchat, and it suggested that the design of that app could be a particularly strong pathway. The paper itself names infinite scrolling, “which lacks any stopping cues”, as one of the ways apps are built to prolong use. But it was a correlation study, and its measurements counted taps, scrolls and keystrokes in general, so it did not isolate infinite scroll. The honest summary is that missing stopping cues are a plausible part of the story, not a proven cause.

How Ziggle puts the ending back

Seen from that angle, the endings in Ziggle are deliberate stopping cues, one of each of the designs above:

  • Numbered pages. Twelve posts to a page, with a pager that shows where you are. The footer sits where a footer should.
  • A decision at the end of every level. The shared popup says what happened and offers a way on. As with the “load more” button, continuing is a choice you make, not something that happens to you.
  • A finite daily set. Eight dailies and a perfect day, and nothing new until tomorrow.

None of this stops anyone playing more; the archive and the trainers are there for that. It only means that when you do keep going, it was your decision.

The twist

The article’s “See also” list puts infinite scroll beside doomscrolling, problematic smartphone use and the “story” feature of social apps: a family of designs with no natural end. The oldest stopping cues are far older than any of them: chapters and page numbers.
