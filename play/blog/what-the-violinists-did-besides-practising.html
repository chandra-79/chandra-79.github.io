Level 3 A deeper dive into The 10,000-hour rule started with thirty violinists

THE ZIGGLE BLOG

What the best violinists did besides practising: naps, 80-minute sessions and sleep

The 1993 study behind the 10,000-hour rule also logged a week of the violinists’ lives. The diaries say more about rest than about hours.

The famous number came from asking the thirty violinists what they had practised over the years. The same study also gave them a diary week: they recorded what they did in every 15-minute interval of the day, then rated how relevant, effortful and enjoyable each activity was for improving their playing. Those diaries are the neglected half of the paper.

How much, how long, how often

The two best groups practised alone for 3.5 hours a day[1], every day of the week including weekends. The future music teachers managed 1.3 hours. But the sessions were not marathon sittings: the average practice session lasted 80 minutes[1], and it did not differ between the groups. The best violinists simply fitted in more of them. They also preferred to practise before lunch.

Practice is effort; the violinists said so

Each player rated 22 activities. Taking lessons (8.60), practising alone (8.00) and solo performance (9.80) were rated high for effort. Playing for fun alone, which an observer would hardly tell from practice, scored only 3.27 for effort and 5.67 for relevance to improvement. Listening to music was rated 4.38 for effort.

The surprise in the table: sleep

Of ten everyday activities, only one was rated reliably more relevant to improving violin playing than the average: sleep (8.17). It took the least effort (0.47). And of the eight activities the players thought were highly relevant, only two filled more than five hours a week: practice alone, at 19.3 hours a week, and sleep, at 58.2[1].

The diaries backed the opinions. The two best groups napped for an average of 2.8 hours a week[1], against 0.9 for the music teachers, and they did not nap to make up for sleeping less at night.

Why this changes the story

The rule says “put in 10,000 hours”. The data say something closer to “put in focused hours in sessions you can sustain, around 80 minutes, and protect your sleep”. The authors also gathered evidence from other fields that useful daily practice is limited: in training studies, gains stopped after about four hours a day and shrank beyond two. The headline number was a by-product of a study that also measured how people spend their days.

What we can and cannot say

These are ten players per group, relying on estimates and a diary week, and the results show how top violinists differ from slightly less successful ones, not what would make a beginner better. The sleep result is a rating plus a diary, not an experiment. It is good evidence for what these players believed and did, and a reason to treat recovery as part of practice.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The forty players

The paper is the source of the 10,000-hour number[2], and its sample is smaller than the legend suggests. Professors at the Music Academy of West Berlin nominated fourteen violin students with the potential for international solo careers. Three were not fluent in German and one was pregnant, which left ten, “the best violinists”. The professors also nominated good violinists, from whom ten were matched to the first group by sex and age, and ten students from the music-education department, “the music teachers”, were matched in the same way. The authors also interviewed ten middle-aged violinists from the Berlin Philharmonic and the Radio Symphony Orchestra, making forty in all. The young players averaged 23.1 years old and the orchestra players 50.5. They all began at about 7.9 years old, with lessons from about 8.0, and by the group’s mean age of 23 every one of the forty had practised for at least ten years.

The paper treats the match between the orchestra players and the best students as support for its retrospective estimates, on the presumption that the orchestras had picked players who, in early adulthood, were at the level the best students are at now. The paper also reports that the groups differed in outside results. Successful competition entries averaged 2.9 for the best violinists against 0.6 for the good ones, and the best and good together averaged 1.8 against 0.2 for the future teachers.

How the diary was kept

Each violinist recalled one full day on a sheet that divided the 24 hours into ninety-six 15-minute intervals, then kept the same kind of diary for seven days, posting each page back in a pre-addressed envelope. Afterwards they coded every activity into a taxonomy of 30 categories. The authors chose a diary written at the end of the day, looking back over everything done, over a selective diary that would track one behaviour. Their reason is that people who keep a diary of one habit, such as drinking or smoking, tend to change that habit, which biases the estimate.

A week in hours

The sessions are the surprise. All three groups practised for about 80 minutes at a time, so the best players did not sit longer. They sat down more often, nearly three times a day against once. The paper states it plainly: the difference in the amount of practice reflected the difference in the number of sessions. The best groups also preferred to practise before lunch, in the late morning in particular.

Rest was part of the schedule

The paper counted a nap as sleep taken between 9 a.m. and 9 p.m. at least an hour away from night sleep. The best groups napped about three times as long as the teachers (2.8 hours a week against 0.9, computed ratio 3.1), and they did not nap to make up for short nights. Napping rose at the start of the working week and fell at the weekend, when the authors suggest other activities, leisure in particular, could provide the rest. The best violinists took less leisure than the good violinists (3.5 hours a day against 4.7), and the best and good groups together averaged 4.1 hours, the same as the teachers’ 4.0. The paper puts the typical leisure of other adults aged 18 to 29 at around 5.2 hours a day.

Why the sessions stop at about 80 minutes

The authors link the figure to older work on how long concentrated practice lasts. They cite training studies that compared one to eight hours a day and found essentially no benefit beyond four hours and reduced benefit beyond two. For typing and other perceptual-motor skills the effective length looked closer to one hour a day. Famous novelists tended to write for about four hours in the morning and rest for the remainder. Distance runners trained in sessions of 45 to 90 minutes, for a daily total of two to three hours of deliberate practice. Chess masters said, in informal interviews, that they spent about four hours a day analysing published games. Eighty minutes sits in that range.

What people said against what the diary showed

People gave a weekly sleep that was a whole number of hours a night multiplied out, undercounted their leisure and overcounted their practice. That is the case for diaries over recollection. Our reading: it is also a caution about the 10,000-hour figure itself, which came from retrospective estimates.

The pianists

The same paper reports a second study that compared expert and amateur pianists. By age 18 the experts had accumulated an estimated 7,606 hours of practice and the amateurs 1,606. The experts’ weekly practice rose in a straight line over the years, while the amateurs’ barely changed after their mean starting age of 9.9 years.

The twist

The paper that gave the world “10,000 hours” also gave it a reason to doubt a flat number of hours. The best violinists did not practise in longer sittings, they practised in more of them, they napped more than the music teachers, and they took less leisure than the good violinists. Our reading: the diaries describe a rhythm of a focused hour and a bit, then rest, repeated about three times a day.
