Twelve pieces, 2,339 ways: the pentomino puzzle

There are exactly twelve shapes you can make from five squares. Packing them into a rectangle has a precise answer, and a famous method for finding it.

Logic games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 17 October 2026

A domino is two squares side by side. A pentomino is five. If you do not count turned-over or turned-around copies as different, there are exactly twelve pentominoes[1]. The mathematician Solomon Golomb named them in the 1950s after the letters they resemble: F, I, L, N, P, T, U, V, W, X, Y and Z (the name “FILiPiNo” plus the end of the alphabet). Twelve pieces of five squares make exactly 60 squares, enough for a rectangle.

How many rectangles are there?

We re-counted the 6×10 case with a short program. Without removing rotations and reflections of the board it finds 9,356 fillings, which is exactly four times 2,339: a rectangle can be turned or flipped in four ways that leave its shape unchanged. It took about four minutes on a laptop.

The trick that makes it fast

Pentomino packing is an exact cover problem: choose placements so that every square is covered once and every piece is used once. Donald Knuth’s “Dancing Links” method, from 2000[2], solves exact cover problems by removing and restoring options in a linked structure, so a computer can try a choice and take it back cheaply. Sudoku is an exact cover problem too, which is why the same idea underlies fast Sudoku solvers.

The twist

The narrower the box, the fewer the ways: 2,339, then 1,010, then 368, and finally just 2 for the 3 × 20 rectangle. A box only three squares high leaves the pieces almost no freedom, so the easiest-looking shape to describe is the hardest to fill.

A square with a hole

The rectangles are not the only way to pose the problem. One of the earliest solved is the 8 × 8 square with a 2 × 2 hole in the middle, solved by Dana Scott as far back as 1958. It has 65 solutions[1]. And the arrival of efficient algorithms such as Knuth’s means that on modern hardware these puzzles are solved “in mere seconds”.

Polyominoes by size, counted

The ratio between the columns tells the story.[3] For five squares the fixed count, 63, is 5.25 times the free count of 12, and for eight squares the ratio is 7.4 (computed). It tends to 8, because large shapes mostly have no symmetry and so appear in eight orientations.

What the computer actually searches

The 63 fixed pentominoes can sit in a 6 × 10 rectangle in 2,056 ways (computed, counting every position of every orientation). The search chooses from those placements so that each of the 60 squares is covered once and each of the 12 pieces is used once, which is the exact cover matrix of 72 conditions the dancing-links method works on.

Who solved which

  1. 1953Golomb begins to define pentominoes formally.
  2. 1958Dana Scott solves the 8 × 8 board with a 2 × 2 hole in the centre, 65 solutions, in one of the first backtracking computer programs.
  3. 1960Colin and Jenifer Haselgrove solve the 6 × 10 rectangle.
  4. October 1965Martin Gardner introduces them in Scientific American, and Golomb’s book appears the same year.

Wikipedia adds two details. Golomb’s letter names use the mnemonic FILiPiNo plus the end of the alphabet (T to Z), and John Conway proposed another set of letters, using O for I, Q for L, R for F and so on. A Japanese craftsman, Saburo Oguro, popularised wooden sets based on the Chinese zodiac, which the article credits with spreading polyomino puzzles widely.

The twist

The man who named them, Solomon Golomb, formally defined pentominoes starting in 1953, and Martin Gardner introduced them to the public in his October 1965 Scientific American column. A recreational idea from one mathematician’s work became a favourite test for exact-cover algorithms such as Knuth’s.
