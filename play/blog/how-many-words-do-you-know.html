How many words do you know? About 42,000, and it depends what you count

A large online experiment put a number on the average adult’s vocabulary. The interesting part is the three different numbers hiding inside it.

Word games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 10 November 2026

In this article 6 sections

  4. From 1,500 words to 42,000

Ask a room how many words they know and the guesses run from a few thousand to a million. In 2016 Marc Brysbaert and colleagues did the sums properly, combining the literature with a very large crowdsourcing experiment. For a 20-year-old native speaker of American English they estimate 42,000 lemmas[1].

Three numbers, one vocabulary

A lemma is a dictionary headword: “run”, but not “running” or “ran”. The same person also knows about 4,200 multiword expressions[1] whose meaning you cannot guess from the parts (“hot dog”), and the whole lot comes from about 11,100 word families[1], where “happy”, “unhappy” and “happiness” count as one. Count families and the number is small; count every inflected form and proper noun and it is enormous. Both are “how many words you know”.

The slow drip

The study also found people keep learning: between the ages of 20 and 60 the average person adds about 6,000 lemmas, roughly one new word every two days[1]. That is the spirit of Word Builder: not a big push but a drip, a few words a day. Our trainer holds 242 words from Open English WordNet, about 0.6% of an average adult’s lemmas, which is a reminder that a small daily set goes a long way over forty years, and that nobody runs out.

The twist

Knowing a word can be shallow. The authors say that “the knowledge of the words can be as shallow as knowing that the word exists”[1]. A 42,000-word vocabulary is mostly recognition, not fluent use, which is why a word you half-know feels on the tip of the tongue and why a good game keeps showing you words you almost have.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

From 1,500 words to 42,000

A child who speaks English will have learned about 1,500 words[2] by the age of five or six. By 20 an average native speaker knows 42,000 lemmas. That is 28 times as many (computed), which averages out to roughly 2893 new lemmas a year across the fourteen or so years in between, or about 8 a day. (That is our arithmetic on two round figures, not a measured rate; most words come from reading rather than being taught one by one.)

The range around the average

The average hides a big spread. In the 2016 study, 20-year-old native speakers recognised from 27,100 lemmas for the lowest 5%[2] to 51,700 for the highest 5%: the top of the range is 1.9 times the bottom (computed). Earlier studies that counted differently gave estimates from 10,000 to 17,000 word families, or 17,000 to 42,000 dictionary words, for young adult native speakers. The unit is what changes the answer.

The small lists that carry a language

Because a few words do most of the work (see why “the” is everywhere), word lists have been built for rapid proficiency: Basic English has 850 words, Special English 1,500 and the General Service List 2,000. Learner’s dictionaries use a limited defining vocabulary so that their definitions can be understood by a learner. It echoes Zipf’s law: the first couple of thousand words are the ones that you meet constantly.

The twist

Vocabulary tests are often part of intelligence and neuropsychological assessments, and vocabulary size has been directly linked to reading comprehension. Yet the number depends so heavily on what you decide a “word” is that two honest counts can differ several-fold. The interesting question is not how many words you know but which ones, and how well.
