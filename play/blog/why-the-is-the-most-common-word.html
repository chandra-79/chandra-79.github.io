Why “the” is everywhere: Zipf’s law in a million words

In one famous million-word collection the most common word is nearly 7% of everything, and the second is almost exactly half as common.

Word games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 8 August 2026

In the 1960s a team at Brown University gathered a million-word sample of American English, from news stories to fiction to science writing, and counted every word. That collection, the Brown Corpus (1,014,312 words[1]), became one of the most studied in linguistics. The linguist George Zipf had said decades earlier that such counts follow a pattern, and here it is.

The pattern

“The” accounts for nearly 7%[1] of all words (69,971 of a little over a million). The second word, “of”, comes in at 36,411[1], just over half of that, and the third, “and”, at 28,852. Zipf’s law says the n-th word occurs about 1/n as often as the first, and these three are close. It is a rough fit, not a law of nature: “and” beats its prediction by about a quarter.

A few words do most of the work

Zipf’s curve has a long tail. In the Brown Corpus, half of all the word volume is made up of repeats of only 135 different words[1]. Everything else, all the rare, interesting, crossword-clue words that make a vocabulary feel large, shares the other half.

The twist

Researchers still argue about why language looks like this. Zipf’s own explanation was a principle of least effort, speakers and listeners both wanting to spend as little as possible. A later critical review (Piantadosi, 2014)[2] goes through the evidence and the competing explanations, and reminds us that several quite different mechanisms can produce the same curve. A neat curve is not an explanation.

What this means for a word game

If you hide a word in a grid, it is nearly always a rare one, because the common ones are tiny and dull. That is why a word search with the themed categories feels like a different game from a pile of everyday words, and why our easy lists use common words in a small grid while hard lists draw on much longer, rarer ones.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Discovered before Zipf, and a bad fit for most texts

The law carries Zipf’s name, but he said himself that he did not originate it. He reported the pattern in 1932, and he acknowledged that the French stenographer Jean-Baptiste Estoup had found the same relationship in 1916[3]. A stenographer, someone whose job was to write down other people’s words as fast as they were said, was the first to notice which words come back again and again.

The curve is also less universal than its fame suggests. In an analysis of a corpus of 30,000 English texts, only about 15% fitted Zipf’s law well[3]. Loosening the definition slightly raised that to nearly half. The first ten million words of English Wikipedia are the sort of case where a single straight line does not do, and the data is better described by separate curves for different kinds of word. A useful reading: Zipf’s law is a very good first sketch of language, and every careful study finds it needs adjusting.

What an exact 1/n curve would look like

Suppose a vocabulary of N words followed the law exactly, so the word of rank n had a share proportional to 1/n. The shares must add up to one, so the top word’s share is 1 divided by the sum 1 + ½ + ⅓ + … + 1/N (the harmonic number). We can compute what that predicts:

Set that beside the real Brown Corpus and you see a rough fit rather than a match. Its top word is nearly 7%, a little under what an ideal curve gives for any of these vocabularies, while half of its volume is covered by 135 words, fewer than the ideal curve needs for a vocabulary of 50,000 (168). The shape of the arithmetic is the lesson: a twentyfold bigger vocabulary (10,000 to 200,000) takes the top word’s share down only from 10.2% to 7.8%, and the number of words that cover half of all text grows from 75 to 335, nowhere near twentyfold. However large the vocabulary, a few hundred words carry half of what is said.

A curve that must stop, or it would add up to infinity

The generalised law lets the exponent be any s, not just 1: the word of rank n has a share proportional to 1/ns. With s = 1 the shares sum to a total that grows without limit as the vocabulary grows, so a Zipf language with a literally endless vocabulary cannot exist: the shares would not add to one. Only when s is above 1 do the shares add up to a finite total, and then the sum is the Riemann zeta function. This is a quiet constraint on real language. It is also a good question for any model of language: how can there be a long tail of ever-rarer words without the sums running away?

The twist

The same shape turns up far from words. A growth process where the rich get richer, the Yule–Simon distribution, has been shown to fit both word frequency against rank and city population against rank[3], and in each case better than Zipf’s law itself. The pattern that makes “the” so common may have less to do with grammar than with a general rule: things that are already popular get chosen more often.
