52 cards, 80 unvigintillion orders, and why sort() is not a shuffle

A well-shuffled deck is almost certainly in an order that has never existed before. A lazy shuffle in code can be wildly unfair, and we measured it.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 6-minute read · 9 July 2026

A deck of 52 cards can be arranged in 52 × 51 × 50 × … × 1 ways, written 52!. The number is 80,658,175,170,943,878,571,660,636,856,403,766,975,289,505,440,883,277,824,000,000,000,000, about 8.07 × 1067. If every one of the eight billion people alive dealt a hand each second since the universe began, they would have covered a vanishingly small corner of that number. Shuffle a deck properly and you are almost certainly holding an order nobody has ever held.

How many shuffles is enough?

In 1992 Dave Bayer and Persi Diaconis analysed the ordinary riffle shuffle, where you cut the deck in two and interleave it. For 52 cards, five riffles still leave the deck far from random, while seven bring it close[3] (a measure called total variation distance falls to 0.334). That is where “seven shuffles” in the folklore comes from.

The shuffle you should not use

A correct computer shuffle is the Fisher–Yates method (1938), in the efficient form published by Richard Durstenfeld in 1964: walk down the list and swap each item with a random one from the rest. A tempting shortcut is to sort the list with a random comparator, sort(() => random() − 0.5). It looks fair. We tested both on just three items, 60,000 times each, with the same random generator:

The twist

The fair method is flat: every order lands within about a percent of 10,000. The lazy one favours some orders by roughly two to one over others, because a sort never compares every pair and a random answer to a question the algorithm assumes is consistent breaks its assumptions. The bias depends on the engine’s sorting method, so it is also different from one browser to the next.

Why Ziggle cares

Our scores are checked on a server against the puzzle everyone is supposed to have, so a board must come out the same on every device. We shuffle with a seeded Fisher–Yates, and “never sort with a random comparator” is one of the written rules of the project. Fairness here is not just manners: it is how the daily puzzle stays the same puzzle for everyone.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What one riffle really does

The standard mathematical model of a riffle shuffle, named for Gilbert, Shannon and Reeds, describes it in two steps. Cut the deck: the chance that exactly k of the n cards go in the first packet is the number of ways to choose them divided by 2n, which is what you would get if every card flipped a coin to pick its packet. Then interleave the two packets, dropping cards from each with probability in proportion to how many it has left. For 52 cards the most likely cut is an exact 26–26 split, and even that happens only about 11% of the time (computed from the formula), so the cut is rarely as even as people imagine.

The model has an odd consequence. One shuffle that leaves every card where it started, the identity, can come from many different cut-and-interleave choices, so it is more likely than any other single order: by a factor of (n + 1) / 2n against 1/2n. For a standard deck, doing nothing is 53 times more likely than any other particular order[5]. After one shuffle the deck is still far from random, which is the starting point of the seven-shuffle story.

The seven is a threshold, not a magic number

Bayer and Diaconis showed that for n cards the transition from “not mixed” to “mixed” happens, sharply, near (3/2) log2 n shuffles[5], and it is sharp: a few shuffles fewer and the deck is plainly unmixed, a few more and it is nearly perfect. For n = 52 that expression is about 8.6 (computed), which is why the practical answer lands at seven. The article gives the figures: after five riffles the distance from a uniformly random deck is still close to one (the worst it can be), and after seven it has fallen to 0.334.

The result depends on how you measure “random”. Total variation distance asks how differently a perfect shuffle and yours could ever behave, over every possible test of the deck. It is a strict yardstick, and a different measure could give a different number of shuffles.

The twist

The model that every one of these results rests on was never a journal article in its first life. It was reported in a 1955 technical report by Gilbert and in an unpublished 1981 manuscript by Reeds. And the number of shuffles you need grows only as the logarithm of the deck size: doubling the deck to 104 cards adds about 1.5 shuffles to the formula (our arithmetic), not twice as many.
