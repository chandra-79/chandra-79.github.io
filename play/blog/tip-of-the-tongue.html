It’s on the tip of my tongue

You know the word. You know it starts with S. You know it has two syllables. Here’s what’s happening, and how Word Cloud uses it.

It’s a small kind of torture. You know the word. You can feel the shape of it. It starts with an S, maybe, and it has about two syllables, and you could pick it out of a line-up instantly. You just can’t say it.

Psychologists call it the tip-of-the-tongue state, and it’s one of the most studied annoyances in the field.

The 1966 experiment

Roger Brown and David McNeill wanted to catch the feeling in the act. So they read people the definitions of rare words and asked them to name the word. Plenty of people landed in exactly that maddening in-between place: sure they knew it, unable to produce it.

And when they were stuck, people could still report a surprising amount, better than chance: the first letter, the number of syllables, words that sounded similar, words that meant something similar, even where certain letters sat. The word was there. The path to it was what had gone missing.

It happens to everyone

Tip-of-the-tongue moments are normal at every age. They do become more frequent as people get older, especially in later life, but an occasional one is just part of having a large vocabulary. The bigger the library, the more often a book gets misshelved.

The best way out

Research points to a simple trick. Being given the first letter of the target word makes people more likely to break out of a tip-of-the-tongue state. The first sound is like a key turning in a lock you’ve been rattling.

That’s why Word Cloud’s “Reveal a letter” always gives you the first missing letter, not a random one in the middle. It costs 20 sparks, so we suggest trying your clues first. But if you’re hovering on the edge of a word, that one letter is often all you need.

Tips for the next time it happens

  1. Say what you know. “Starts with S, two syllables, means rude.” Describing it often pulls it closer.
  2. Run the alphabet. Try each first sound in turn. It feels silly and it works suspiciously often.
  3. Walk away. The word has a habit of turning up while you’re making tea.
  4. Drop the near-miss. If the same wrong word keeps popping up, think about something else for a minute and come back.

(The word, by the way, was “surly”. You’re welcome.)

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

A phenomenon with two names and a long history

The Wikipedia article calls it TOT, or lethologica: failing to retrieve a word from memory, together with partial recall and the feeling that retrieval is about to happen. William James described it in The Principles of Psychology in 1890 without naming it. The first experimental study was Brown and McNeill’s in 1966. They read people the definitions of rare words and asked them to say the word, then, when the target was read out, to say whether they had been in a tip-of-the-tongue state. They identified three kinds of positive state: the person recognised the word when it was read; they produced the right word before it was read; or they produced a different word that was not the one they had been looking for. People in the state could report the first letter of the word, the number of syllables, similar-sounding and similar-meaning words, and the position of some letters, all better than chance. That showed the feeling was not an illusion: it carried real information.

The article distinguishes TOT from the feeling of knowing (FOK), the sense that you would recognise the answer from a list. They may be different processes: it reports some evidence that TOTs, but not FOKs, are associated with the anterior cingulate and the right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex. It notes that an occasional TOT is normal at every age but becomes more frequent with age. It can be called a medical condition only when it is frequent enough to get in the way of learning or daily life, and when it is caused by brain damage it is called anomic aphasia.

Is it universal?

The experience is reported by men and women in young adulthood, middle age and later life, and education level is not thought to matter. In a 1999 study by B. L. Schwartz, 45 of 51 languages surveyed[2] had an idiom for it that mentions the tongue, mouth or throat; the exceptions were American Sign Language, Amharic, Icelandic, Kalenjin and Kiswahili. The article adds that signers report “tip of the finger” experiences, so the state itself is not tied to speech. Idioms include “on the tip of the tongue”, “sparkling at the end of the tongue” and “in the mouth and throat”.

Why does it happen? Two schools

The causes, the article says, are largely unknown, and the explanations fall into two groups. The direct-access view says the word’s memory trace is strong enough to set off the state but not strong enough to retrieve it. One version, the blocking hypothesis, says a plausible but wrong word comes to mind quickly and blocks the right one; those wrong words are called blockers. The inferential view says the target is not inaccessible but that the person pieces together clues about it, and the feeling that retrieval is near depends on how much information they can get. In cue-familiarity theory, recognising the cue strongly creates the feeling, whether or not the word is known; the accessibility heuristic says the feeling is driven by the amount and strength of what you can retrieve. The two schools make different predictions, which is how researchers tell them apart.

What gets you out

The article’s section on priming supports the post’s advice. Most words in tip-of-the-tongue states are low-frequency, words not used or recalled for some time, and being given the first letter of the word makes people more likely to overcome the state[2]. One prime is enough. But there is a catch: a prime that sounds like the target can also raise the number of TOT states, because similar-sounding wrong words come to mind involuntarily and act as blockers. Changing the grammatical class of the primer can fix that. Other findings: bilinguals report the same number of TOTs as monolinguals for proper names but more for other words, especially in the less dominant language, and the number rises with age. The article puts the age effect down to changes in the brain regions used for retrieval, including a loss of grey matter in the left insula.

How much does a first letter help? Our own list, counted

The post says Word Cloud’s “Reveal a letter” gives the first missing letter. How much does a first letter narrow things? We counted Ziggle’s 809 five-letter Letter Lock answers (computed):

Knowing the first letter takes the field from 809 to about 58.4 on average, while the last letter leaves about 89.9, partly because the most common last letter, E, covers so many words. A first letter is a strong cue in a word list, as well as a good prime in a mind, which is why games that give one away, like Letter Lock’s easy daily, make a puzzle feel approachable.

The twist

Several of the cues people use to break out of a tip-of-the-tongue state are the same facts Brown and McNeill’s volunteers could report in 1966: the first letter and the rhythm of the word. When you say “starts with S, two syllables”, you are reading out the information the state itself contains.
