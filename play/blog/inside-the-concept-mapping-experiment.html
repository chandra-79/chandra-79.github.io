Inside the experiment: 200 students, four ways to study, one surprise

A close reading of Karpicke and Blunt (2011): how the two experiments were built, what they measured, and the numbers that did not make the headlines.

“Practising recall beats concept mapping” is easy to say. This article opens the study up, because the design is as interesting as the result, and a few of its numbers are quietly spectacular.

Experiment 1: four ways to study one text

Eighty undergraduates[1] studied a science text in one of four ways. Study once: read it once. Repeated study: read it four times in a row. Concept mapping: read it once, then draw a concept map on paper while looking at the text (a common “elaborative” method). Retrieval practice: read it once, write down everything they could recall, read it again, and recall again.

The researchers matched the learning time exactly between concept mapping and retrieval practice, and checked the starting points: students’ first concept maps contained 0.78[1] of the ideas in the text, against 0.81 recalled in the first retrieval attempt. Nearly identical, so any later gap could not come from one group having learned more at the start.

A week later

Everyone returned one week later for a short-answer test with two kinds of question: verbatim (facts stated in the text) and inference (connecting several ideas). Retrieval practice came out on top on both. Averaged over the two kinds, retrieval practice scored 0.67[1] and concept mapping 0.45, which the authors describe as about a 50% improvement, an effect of d = 1.50, one and a half standard deviations.

Experiment 2: change everything that could explain it

A skeptic can ask three things, and the second experiment answers each. Would it work on other kinds of text? They used texts with an enumeration structure (properties of different muscle tissues) and a sequence structure (the stages of digestion). Was it just an average effect? They tested 120 students[1], each one doing both methods on different texts, so they could count individuals. Was the test unfairly like the retrieval task? Half of the students took a short-answer test and half had to draw a concept map from memory, which should favour concept mapping.

Retrieval practice still won on both final tests, by more than one standard deviation each (d = 1.07 and 1.01[1]), even though the concept-mapping group started better this time (0.74 of the ideas produced on the first maps against 0.65 recalled).

  • 101 of 120students (84%) did better after retrieval practice than after concept mapping
  • 90 of 120students (75%) expected concept mapping to work as well or better
  • d = 1.50size of the advantage in Experiment 1
  • d = 1.07 / 1.01in Experiment 2, on the two kinds of final test
The part to remember

Students’ own predictions pointed the wrong way. In Experiment 1 they predicted that repeated reading would give the best memory and that practising recall would give the worst, which is the exact reverse of the result. Judging how well you have learned is itself a skill, and an easy one to get wrong when rereading feels so fluent.

What the study does not show

The students were undergraduates reading science passages, and the final test came one week later. Nothing here says concept maps are useless: the authors’ claim is narrower, that retrieval practice produced more learning than this particular elaborative activity on these materials. A week is also short; spacing studies cover months. Read it as strong evidence about a specific comparison, not a law of studying.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The idea being tested

The full text of the report, in[2] Science, starts from two assumptions it wants to overturn. One is that learning happens when people encode knowledge. The other is that retrieval only measures what was learnt and does not itself produce learning, like weighing an object without changing its weight. The authors point out that recent National Research Council books on how students learn contain no mention of retrieval processes. Their claim goes further than “retrieval helps”: a retrieval event may be a more powerful learning event than an encoding event.

Concept mapping was a well-chosen opponent. It is considered an active, elaborative task, in which students build a diagram with concepts as nodes and relations as links, and it is common in science teaching. The authors say it was not known whether retrieval activities are more effective than other active, elaborative ones, since retrieval might simply match them. The paper also notes that most earlier retrieval research used verbal-learning materials, not the complex texts students meet in class, and rarely tested understanding.

Experiment 1 in detail

At the end of the learning phase students predicted what percentage of the text they would remember in a week. Then they returned for a short-answer test with verbatim questions (facts stated in the text) and inference questions (connecting several ideas). All three of repeated study, concept mapping and retrieval practice beat studying once. Retrieval practice came first, and concept mapping was not significantly better than the extra reading. Collapsed over question type, retrieval scored 0.67 and concept mapping 0.45, which the authors call about a 50% improvement (our arithmetic: 0.67 ÷ 0.45 = 1.49, a 22-point gap).

Experiment 2: the sceptic’s objections

The second experiment tested 120 students, each doing both methods on different texts, with two kinds of text: an enumeration (properties of different muscle tissues) and a sequence (the stages of digestion). Half took the short-answer test and half had to draw a concept map from memory, a final test that could have favoured concept mapping. The concept-mapping condition also started ahead this time: 0.74 of the ideas on the first maps against 0.65 recalled. Retrieval still won both, with d = 1.07 on the short-answer test and d = 1.01 on the map test.

Student by student

Our arithmetic: only 25% of students (30 of 120) expected retrieval to win, yet 84% of them did better with it. In Experiment 1 the same pattern showed up in the group averages: students predicted that repeated studying would give the best memory and practising retrieval the worst.

Why would recall teach more than a diagram?

The authors offer a candidate mechanism. Elaboration enriches a memory by adding encoded features. Retrieval, by contrast, uses cues to reconstruct what was learnt, and may improve the diagnostic value of those cues, meaning how well a cue singles out one piece of knowledge from the other candidates. Rather than multiplying features, retrieval narrows the set of candidates a cue points to.

The caveat the authors add themselves

Concept mapping is not inherently a bad study task or an elaborative one. If students draw the map in the presence of the text, it is elaborative study. If they draw it without the text, the same activity is retrieval practice. And the authors say that whether a task counts as “active” does not tell you how much learning it will produce: the specific nature of the activity decides that.

The twist

The same activity can be either kind of study. A student who builds a concept map while looking at the text is elaborating. A student who builds it from memory is, in the authors’ words, practising retrieval, which is why the final test in Experiment 2, a map drawn without the text, was a retrieval test as well as a mapping one.
