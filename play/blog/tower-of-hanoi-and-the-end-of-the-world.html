The Tower of Hanoi and the end of the world

A Victorian mathematician invented a legend to sell a puzzle. The sums behind it are real, and gloriously large.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 13 May 2026

In this article 5 sections

  3. The deeper read

In 1883 the French mathematician Édouard Lucas launched a puzzle under the name of a made-up Siamese professor, “N. Claus (de Siam)”[2], an anagram of “Lucas d'Amiens”. It was three pegs and a stack of discs, and it came with a story: in a temple, priests are moving a tower of 64 golden discs one at a time, never placing a bigger disc on a smaller one, and when they finish the world will end.

Each disc doubles the work

The fewest moves for n discs is 2n − 1. Add one disc and you do everything you did before, move the new disc, and do it all again. With 64 discs that is 18,446,744,073,709,551,615 moves. At one move a second, it would take about 585 billion years[2], roughly 42 times the estimated age of the universe.

Ten discs is a pleasant half-hour at a table. Thirty is a working lifetime. By forty it needs thirty-five thousand years of non-stop moving.

The twist

The “ancient temple” was marketing. The puzzle and its legend were presented in 1883 as a discovery from Siam, and the author’s name was an anagram of the real one. A good story made a toy sell, and made a piece of exponential arithmetic memorable: that is why you probably already know what 264 feels like.

Why puzzle designers love it

Hanoi is the cleanest picture of exponential growth anyone has built from wood. It is also a gentle introduction to recursion: to move n discs, first move n − 1 out of the way. Ziggle’s own levels are tamer, but the lesson is the same: a small step in difficulty can be a large step in work.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Only a sliver of the positions are on the shortest path

With n discs there are 3n legal positions (each disc is on one of three pegs, and the order on a peg is forced). The shortest solution visits 2n of them. For eight discs that is 256 of 6,561; for 64 discs it is a fraction of about 5.4 × 10−12, about five in a trillion. The great majority of positions are ones you would never reach if you played perfectly.

At the other extreme, the longest route that never revisits a position takes 3n − 1 moves[2]. For 64 discs that is 3,433,683,820,292,512,484,657,849,089,280 moves, which at one move a second would take more than 1023 years (we get 1.09 × 1023), about 190 billion times longer than the shortest. The legend’s priests, wisely, take the short way.

You do not need to think: a two-step rule

The shortest solution can be done without any planning. Alternate two kinds of move: move the smallest disc one peg onward in a fixed direction, then make the only other legal move that does not involve the smallest disc. Repeat. This iterative rule completes the tower in the fewest moves. There is a still deeper regularity: the position after move m can be read off the binary representation of m, one bit per disc, so the details of move 216 of an eight-disc puzzle can be computed without simulating anything before it.

The twist

A puzzle whose solution grows exponentially can still be solved by a rule a child can follow. Difficulty in counting and difficulty in doing are different things: Hanoi’s answer is astronomically long, yet each step is trivial, which is the exact opposite of a Sudoku, where the answer is short and each step is hard.
