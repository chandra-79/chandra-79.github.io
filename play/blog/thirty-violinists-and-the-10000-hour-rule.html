The 10,000-hour rule started with thirty violinists

The famous study behind the famous number compared three groups of ten young violinists. What it actually found is stranger and more useful.

Science of practice · The Ziggle team · 6-minute read · 29 August 2026

“Ten thousand hours” has launched a thousand self-help chapters. Its source is a 1993 paper by Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, and it is worth meeting the people in it. The study compared violinists at a Berlin music academy in three groups of ten: those the professors nominated as the best, a matched set of good violinists, and ten music-education students who would become teachers. Each group had 7 women and 3 men[1], and the young violinists averaged 23.1 years old.

What the hours really were

The researchers asked each player to estimate their weekly practice alone with the violin for every year since they started, and added the years up. By the age of 18 the totals looked like this:

Notice that the best group’s average at 18 is 7,410 hours[1], not 10,000. In the running text of the paper, “10,000 hours” appears once, as a round number for “more than a decade” of deliberate practice in a discussion about how the body adapts. It is not a threshold, and the paper never says that anyone needs to cross it.

How a figure became a rule

Malcolm Gladwell’s 2008 book Outliers turned an average from one small sample into a rule of thumb for greatness. The 2014 meta-analysis we wrote about before pooled 88 studies[2] and found practice explained 26% of the difference in performance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions. Practice matters; the size of the effect depends on the field.

The twist

The paper’s real claim is about how the violinists practised. The authors defined deliberate practice as activity designed to improve performance, and the players’ own ratings drew the line: practising alone scored 8.00[1] for effort and 9.82 for relevance, while playing for fun alone scored just 3.27 for effort. Playing a lot is not the same thing as practising hard, which is a very different claim from “put in the hours”.

What it means for a puzzle site

Ziggle’s trainers ask for short, focused sessions with feedback after each one, and the games get harder as you do. That is the shape of deliberate practice. It is not a promise of mastery in 10,000 hours, or in any number of hours: the paper was about thirty violinists.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Deliberate, purposeful, or just practice?

The paper was not about practice in general. Ericsson’s claim was that the gap between experts and ordinary adults reflects a lifelong period of deliberate effort to improve in one domain, and that how expert someone becomes depends more on how they practise than on how many times they simply perform the skill. Later writers sorted practice into kinds. In Ericsson’s sense, deliberate practice has a teacher who designs the task and gives clear instruction; if a session meets every criterion but the teacher, it is, by his definition, purposeful practice[3] rather than deliberate. Medical educators boiled it down to four ingredients: repetitive performance of the intended skill, rigorous assessment, specific feedback, and better performance as a result.

Not much fun, which is the point

Ericsson found that deliberate practice is rarely enjoyable in itself, and that people in a field seldom start it spontaneously. The motivation has to come from the wish to improve. He observed that children had to want to improve before deliberate practice could begin; a few months of simply being exposed to an activity in a fun way came first. That is a useful warning for a game site: the fun and the practice are different things, and the fun is what keeps you coming back to it.

The legend of the book

Gladwell’s Outliers used two stories to carry the rule. The Beatles played live in Hamburg more than 1,200 times between 1960 and 1964[4], which Gladwell counts as more than 10,000 hours on stage. Bill Gates, Gladwell says, got access to a high-school computer in 1968, at 13, and spent 10,000 hours programming on it. The same book is also about luck: the cut-off dates for youth hockey leagues (a child born on 1 January plays in the same league as one born on 31 December) and Gates’s unusual access. For scale, 10,000 hours at 20 hours a week is 9.6 years of 52 weeks (computed), a decade, which is roughly the length of the “ten-year rule” the practice literature already used.

The twist

Gladwell’s argument is easy to remember as “practise for 10,000 hours and you will be great”. The book’s own examples are about opportunity as much as effort: where you were born in the year, which computer you could get to. The oldest version of the story in the paper is also more modest: the best violinists had accumulated about 7,410 hours by 18, about three-quarters of the famous round number.
