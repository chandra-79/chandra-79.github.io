What word games are really training

A 1913 newspaper filler, a game built for one person’s partner, and the mental move study researchers love most.

Word puzzles have a surprisingly scrappy history. Most of the famous ones were invented by somebody trying to fill a gap, sometimes on a page and sometimes in an evening.

A very short history of staring at letters

The crossword first appeared on 21 December 1913, in the Sunday New York World, made by a Liverpool-born journalist called Arthur Wynne. It caught on fast. The New York Times held out with great dignity for almost thirty years, publishing its first crossword on 15 February 1942.

The word search is usually credited to Norman E. Gibat, who published one in the Selenby Digest on 1 March 1968, in Norman, Oklahoma. (Yes: a man named Norman, in a town called Norman. We didn’t make that up, and we’re a little jealous.)

Wordle is the most modern fairy tale. Josh Wardle, a software engineer, built it for himself and his partner, Palak Shah, who went through the word list and removed the obscure words. On 1 November 2021, 90 people played it. By 2 January 2022 it was over 300,000, and a week later more than 2 million. The New York Times bought it at the end of that month for a price “in the low seven figures”, and it was played 4.8 billion times in 2023. Even the famous coloured-square share grid wasn’t Wardle’s idea. Players in New Zealand came up with it, and he had the good sense to add it to the game.

What your brain is actually doing

Here’s the honest version. Word games make you better at word games: faster at spotting “ING” in a wall of letters, quicker at guessing a five-letter word from two yellow tiles. That’s real, and it’s satisfying. But two of Ziggle’s word games also lean on a mental move that learning researchers rate very highly: pulling something out of your head instead of looking at it again.

In a classic 2006 study, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students learn a passage of text. Some reread it several times. Others read it once or twice, then practised writing down what they remembered. A week later, the practise-recalling group remembered 61% of the material. The rereaders remembered 40%. The rereaders also felt more confident, which is the cruel twist: rereading feels like learning, while recalling actually does more of it.

Word Cloud is basically that move turned into a game. You get a meaning, a near-synonym, maybe an opposite, and you have to fetch one word out of your memory to fit them all. No list to look at, no letters to rearrange. Just you, three clues and a word you know is in there somewhere.

Letter Lock is a detective game in disguise

Letter Lock looks like a word game, but it’s really about information. Every guess is a question, and the tiles are the answer. A good guess cuts down the possibilities; a lucky guess just happens to be right.

That’s why we built the Lock coach. After each game it shows how many of the 809 possible answers were still in play after each guess. A decent opener can leave twenty or so. You’ll quickly notice that the guesses that felt clever were usually the ones that cut the list the most.

Tips from people who play too much

  • Letter Lock: open with common letters. In English, E, T, A, O, I, N and S turn up most. Try not to reuse grey letters in your second guess unless you’re on hard mode.
  • Word Cloud: read the opposite clue first. “Not hot” narrows things faster than “a feeling”. Reveal a letter before your third wrong guess; it costs less than flailing.
  • Crossword: fill in the short answers first. Every one you get hands you letters in the longer ones.
  • Word search: stop reading and start scanning. Hunt for a rare letter in the word (a Q, a Z, a double letter) rather than the first one.

And when you’re stuck, walk away for a minute. The answer has a funny habit of arriving the moment you stop chasing it.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The crossword: a diamond with FUN in it

Wikipedia’s article on Arthur Wynne says[2] he was born in Liverpool on 22 June 1871, the son of the editor of the Liverpool Mercury, and emigrated to the United States on 6 June 1891, aged 19. In New York he made the puzzle page for the “Fun” section of the Sunday New York World. On 21 December 1913 he ran a diamond-shaped puzzle with a hollow centre and the letters F-U-N already filled in, and called it a “Word-Cross Puzzle”. A few weeks later a typesetting error turned it into “Cross-Word”, and the name stuck.

Wynne’s version was built on older word shapes such as the word diamond, but he brought in the lines and boxes for entering letters, and afterwards the symmetric black squares that separate words. Apart from the numbering scheme, the article says, his form is the one in use today. The word appeared in the Oxford English Dictionary in 1933, and the New York Times ran its first crossword on 15 February 1942, with Margaret Petherbridge Farrar as the first puzzle editor, a post she held until 1969. Google marked the 100th anniversary with an interactive Doodle on 20 December 2013.

The word search: first by whom?

The post credits Norman Gibat, and Wikipedia’s article on the word search does too[4]: published in the Selenby Digest on 1 March 1968 in Norman, Oklahoma. The same article adds caveats. Pedro Ocón de Oro was publishing Spanish letter soups before that date, and a James Patrick Carr of Illinois is said to have made such puzzles earlier. Our reading: “credited to Gibat” is the safe wording, and “first” is harder to prove.

Wordle, step by step

  1. 2013Josh Wardle builds a prototype called Mr. Bugs’ Wordy Nugz, inspired by the colour matching in Mastermind.
  2. 2014The prototype is finished, then set aside.
  3. January 2021Wardle puts Wordle on the web, shared only with himself and his partner.
  4. 1 November 202190 players.
  5. 2 January 2022More than 300,000 players. A week later, more than 2 million.
  6. January 2022The New York Times buys it for a “low seven-figure sum”.
  7. 2023The game is played 4.8 billion times.

The early word list is the interesting design decision. The prototype used all of the roughly 13,000 five-letter words in English, but his partner Palak Shah struggled with obscure ones, so he had her sort them into words she knew, did not know and might have known, and the list shrank to about 2,000 familiar words, which Wikipedia says is enough for roughly five years at one a day. Our arithmetic: 2,000 ÷ 365 = 5.5 years, and Letter Lock’s 809 answers last 2.2 years. Both sit at about 15 to 16% of the allowed guesses, 2,000 of 13,000 and 809 of 5,049 (computed).

The growth from 90 to 300,000 players in 62 days is a factor of about 3,300, which is nearly 12 doublings, one every 5.3 days (computed). The emoji grid that made it spread came, the article says, from a group of friends in New Zealand who had made their own way of sharing results, which Wardle adopted.

What practice does and does not transfer

The post’s claim about the 2006 recall study is accurate to the numbers (the reread group remembered 40% a week later against 61% for the group that took three recall tests, in the second experiment of the paper). The wider evidence on whether word practice carries over to other skills is in the post on whether puzzles make you smarter: the large review found extensive evidence of improvement on the practised tasks, less for related tasks and little for distant ones.

For the information idea in Letter Lock, see the posts on the best first guess and the maths of a good guess: of 5,049 allowed guesses, the best leaves 18.7 answers on average (computed there).

The twist

Two of the four stories in this post have a footnote. Wordle’s word list was shaped by one person’s knowledge of which words she knew, and the word search’s “first” has competing claimants. The crossword’s name came from a typesetting slip. Most word games were invented by people filling a gap, and the paperwork about who was first is untidy.
