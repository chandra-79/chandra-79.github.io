The 10,000-hour rule, and what it got wrong

It sold a lot of books. The researchers behind the original study weren’t fans. Here’s what practice actually explains.

In 2008, Malcolm Gladwell’s book Outliers gave the world a beautifully round number. Become world-class at anything, it suggested, with about 10,000 hours of practice. That works out at roughly 20 hours a week for ten years. It is the kind of number that ends up on a mug.

Where it came from

The idea was based on research by the psychologist Anders Ericsson on expert performance. But the authors of that original study disputed how the rule was used. Their work was about deliberate practice: focused, effortful work on specific weaknesses, usually with a teacher. Not just hours. Not just any hours.

Gladwell’s examples were memorable. By his account the Beatles played more than 1,200 shows in Hamburg between 1960 and 1964, putting them past 10,000 hours on stage, and Bill Gates, who started programming in 1968 at 13, put in about 10,000 hours too. Those are his estimates, not measurements. Paul McCartney, asked about the theory, was kind but firm: lots of bands played Hamburg for that long and didn’t make it, so it’s “not a cast-iron theory”.

What practice really explains

In 2014, Brooke Macnamara and colleagues pooled 88 studies[1] to measure how much deliberate practice explains differences in skill. The answer depended a lot on the field:

  • Games: 26%
  • Music: 21%
  • Sports: 18%
  • Education: 4%
  • Professions: under 1%

So practice matters, and it matters most in games, but it isn’t the whole story anywhere. Starting age, the quality of teaching, basic abilities and plenty of luck all play a part.

What actually makes practice count

The writer Ulrich Boser made a sharp point. Most of us have spent thousands of hours handwriting and driving, and very few of us are superb at either. What’s missing is feedback: a coach, a tutor, or a careful way of watching yourself. Hours without feedback mostly make you better at doing it the way you already do it.

That’s why every Ziggle game tries to tell you something useful when you finish. The Lock coach shows how much each guess narrowed things down. The typing trainer’s heat map shows which keys are slow or slippery. Word Cloud shows what each clue cost you. Small mirrors, held up often.

Our not-on-a-mug version

Practise a little, regularly, on the part you find hard, and look at the results. You won’t hit 10,000 hours of Letter Lock. (Please don’t.) But you will get better at it, and you’ll know exactly why.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Where the number really came from

The Wikipedia article on Outliers says the book appeared on 18 November 2008 and went straight to number one[3] on the New York Times and Globe and Mail lists, holding the top spot on the first for eleven weeks. Gladwell’s subject is the set of things behind success beyond talent: the birth-month pattern in Canadian junior hockey, the opportunities Bill Gates had, the Beatles’ years in Hamburg. The 10,000-hour rule is one thread in it, and the article is careful to say it is “based on a study by Anders Ericsson”, whose authors have disputed how it was used.

What did that study find? In the 1993 paper by Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, expert violinists had, on average, accumulated about 10,000 hours of deliberate practice by the age of twenty. Ericsson later wrote that the popular rule is wrong in several ways. The 10,000 was an average, not a threshold: the violinists were, at that point, “nowhere near masters”. The time needed varies with the field. And Gladwell did not separate deliberate practice from other kinds. Ericsson, who died in June 2020, spent his career on expert performance[4] in medicine, music, chess and sport.

  2. 1993Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer publish the violin study.
  3. 2008Gladwell’s Outliers turns “about 10,000 hours” into a slogan.
  4. 2014Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald pool 88 studies of how much deliberate practice explains.
  5. 2016Ericsson and Robert Pool publish Peak, which says the rule skipped the key distinction.

The Beatles, counted

Gladwell’s evidence, as the article reports it, was that the Beatles played live in Hamburg more than 1,200 times between 1960 and 1964, “amassing more than 10,000 hours of playing time”, and that Gates, given access to a school computer in 1968 at the age of 13, spent about 10,000 hours programming. Take the two Beatles figures at face value and divide: 10,000 hours over 1,200 appearances is about 8.3 hours on stage per appearance (computed from the two numbers as reported). That is possible only if the count includes rehearsal and long club nights, and the article does not say how the hours were counted. Both numbers are estimates, and the example that made the rule famous is the least checkable part of it.

How long is 10,000 hours?

The first row reproduces Gladwell’s 10 years, rounded: 9.6 years of unbroken 20-hour weeks. The others show why the rule is a slogan for professionals and not a plan for a hobby. Fifteen minutes a day would need about 110 years. Whatever the rule is for, it is not a target for a daily puzzle.

What deliberate practice is, and what it is not

The article on practice sets out Ericsson’s tests for a session to count as deliberate. The task is well defined[5], with a clear goal the student understands. The student can do it alone. They get immediate feedback. They can repeat the task or similar ones. And the task is designed by a teacher, who gives clear instruction. Drop the last condition and Ericsson calls it purposeful practice. In another definition the article quotes, deliberate practice is “highly structured” activity that is most relevant to improving performance, cognitively effortful, and has no immediate reward. That last point matters: Ericsson wrote that it is not inherently motivating, and that people in a domain rarely start it spontaneously, which is why children who first enjoyed an activity, and only later began structured practice, did well in his examples.

The article also notes a nuance from medical education: repetition seems to help beginners most, and as expertise grows, learners must plan around their specific weaknesses. The same list therefore describes a dial, not a switch. Plain repetition at the start, focused work on weak points later.

How much does practice explain?

The same article adds a later analysis of sport alone: deliberate practice accounted for 18% of the variance overall, but only about 1% among elite performers. Put the other way round, in games the remaining 74% of the differences came from something else, in music 79%, and in sport 82% (computed, from the shares above). Games are where practice explained the most, which is a reason to take puzzles seriously and a reason to stay modest about the rest: starting age, teaching, ability and luck all count, and the studies cannot say how they divide.

The twist

The man behind the 10,000-hour rule began, in 1976, by studying people solving the 8-puzzle, the little sliding-tile game the smaller cousin of the 15-puzzle, and later trained a student to remember more than 100 digits in a row. Both are puzzles with fast, exact feedback, which is exactly the setting deliberate practice describes.
