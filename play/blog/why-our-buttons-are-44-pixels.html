Why so many of our buttons are 44 pixels tall

A 1954 experiment about moving a metal stylus explains why small buttons feel hard to hit, and why doubling a button saves exactly one bit.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 10 June 2026

Fingers are bigger than cursors. The web’s accessibility guidelines say so in numbers. WCAG 2.2, the current standard, asks for touch and click targets of at least 24 × 24 CSS pixels[1] at its AA level, and at its stricter AAA level for 44 × 44[1]. Many controls on Ziggle, such as the how-to buttons and the title-bar buttons on a phone, are built to be at least 44 pixels tall.

An old experiment explains it

In 1954 the psychologist Paul Fitts[2] asked people to tap back and forth between two metal plates as fast as they could. He found that the time depends on two things: how far apart the targets are (D) and how wide they are (W). He measured the difficulty in bits, borrowing the word from information theory: log2(2D ÷ W).

Small gains add up

Going from 24 to 44 pixels removes about 0.9 of a bit; going from 44 to 88 removes a whole bit. That is why the standard stops at 44: beyond that you are paying screen space for ever-smaller gains, and a game board has only so much of it.

The twist

Bigger is not the only trick. WCAG 2.2’s 24-pixel rule also lets spacing stand in for size: a small target is acceptable if it is far enough from its neighbours. Two small buttons packed together are the real hazard on a phone, which is why the title bar in a full-screen game keeps the corner button, Help and More apart and the same size.

What this means for a puzzle

A word search lives and dies by drags and taps. If tiles were 20 pixels wide on a phone, half the errors you made would be the interface’s, not yours. The point of a puzzle is to be stuck for good reasons, and a missed tap is a bad one.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Two numbers, two levels

WCAG 2.2 has two target-size rules, and the distance between them has a story. The “Minimum” rule (level AA) asks for 24 × 24 CSS pixels. The “Enhanced” rule (level AAA) asks for 44 × 44. The working group explains that it settled on the smaller figure for the minimum after reviewing the average target size on various high-traffic sites[3], and kept the larger one as the threshold for the stricter version. In other words, 24 is “what the web could realistically be asked to do”, and 44 is “what is comfortable”. We aim for the second wherever the screen allows.

What counts as a target

The measurement is concrete: it must be possible to draw a solid 24 × 24 square entirely inside the target[3]. A button with large rounded corners can fail it, since the corners leave too little room. The rule is also in CSS pixels, so zooming does not change it. And a target smaller than 24 pixels does not automatically fail. There is a spacing exception: centre an imaginary 24-pixel circle on each undersized target, and it passes if none of those circles touches another target or another circle.

The part of Fitts’s law the textbooks warn about

Fitts’s law has been shown to hold across an astonishing range: hands and feet, a pointing device or a head-mounted sight, underwater, and for young and old users. It is not a perfect law. Because difficulty depends only on the ratio of distance to width, it implies that a target can be scaled up or down without changing the time, which cannot be true. In 1968 Welford proposed[4] separating distance and width into their own terms, which predicts movement time better. And, the article notes, distance has a greater impact on the total time than target size does, even though different tasks can share the same difficulty in bits.

How accurate is a 44-pixel tap?

The standard way to measure a real interface, written into ISO 9241-9, replaces the nominal width W with an effective width: 4.133 times the standard deviation[4] of where people really landed. That span covers 96% of the landings, so if the error rate is 4% the effective width equals the nominal one. Turn the formula around for a 44-pixel button and a 4% error rate: the scatter of taps would have a standard deviation of 10.6 pixels (computed), and anyone who is steadier than that hits it more often than 96 times in 100. A 24-pixel target at the same error rate would need people to be accurate to 5.8 pixels. The same finger has to be nearly twice as precise.

The twist

The original 1954 index of difficulty was measured in bits because Fitts was borrowing from information theory: he wanted a number for how much “information” a movement carries, and a rate in bits per second for how well a person performs. Today that rate is called throughput, and ISO 9241-9 recommends it as the measure for pointing devices. A button is, in a quite literal sense, a channel you have to send a signal through.
