Where 15.87% comes from: the optimal error rate is one standard deviation of noise

The “85% rule” is not a rounding of folk wisdom. It is a number that falls out of an equation, and it is the area of a bell curve.

The 85% rule says learning is fastest when you fail about 15% of the time. The precise figure in the paper is 15.87%[1], and it is unusually easy to see where that comes from, because it is a number you can look up in a statistics table.

The model in three lines

Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia and Cohen consider a learner sorting stimuli into two classes (a binary classification). Internally the learner forms a decision variable h = Δ + n: the true difficulty-dependent signal Δ plus noise n, assumed to be random with a Gaussian (bell-curve) shape and spread σ. The learner picks class A if h is above the boundary and class B if below, so errors occur when noise pushes h across the boundary. The easier the stimulus, the larger Δ is relative to the noise, and the fewer the errors.

Learning here is gradient descent, the standard way a machine (and, the authors argue, a brain) adjusts itself in small steps in the direction that reduces error. The rate of learning depends on the error rate, and they ask: at what difficulty is it fastest?

One standard deviation

Their answer, for Gaussian noise, is written ER* = ½ (1 − erf(1 / √2)) ≈ 0.1587. The “erf” is the error function, and ½(1 − erf(z/√2)) is exactly the share of a bell curve lying more than z standard deviations above the middle. With z = 1 that tail is 15.87%. In words: learning is fastest when the stimulus sits one standard deviation of noise away from the decision boundary.

Two things the headlines leave out

It depends on the noise. The authors state that under fairly mild assumptions the optimum is around 15.87%, “a number that varies slightly depending on the noise in the learning process”[2]. Change the shape of the noise and the best error rate shifts a little. Hence “around 85%”.

The demonstrations are simulations. The paper shows the rule works for artificial neural networks and for networks that are biologically plausible models of human and animal learning. That is a strong theoretical result, offered to explain a long history of empirical findings about difficulty, but the paper’s own evidence is mathematical and computational, not a trial in which people practised at different difficulties.

The twist

A tidy fact the equations reveal: the best difficulty is the same fraction of errors at every skill level. As you improve (the noise shrinks), the optimal stimulus gets harder in absolute terms, but the error rate to aim for stays pinned at 15.87%. The authors express it exactly: optimal learning can be achieved “by clamping the error rate during training at a fixed value”[2]. A game that keeps you at about one miss in six, whatever your level, is following the rule.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The paper’s road to the number

A version of the preprint was posted on bioRxiv on 27 January 2018. Nature Communications received the paper on 14 February 2018, accepted it on 2 September 2019 and published it on 5 November 2019, which is about 21 months from submission to print (computed). The argument is short enough to follow in four steps.

  1. The learner. It decides with a variable h, the true signal Δ plus noise. Learning means raising the precision β, which rises as the noise falls. The paper models it as gradient descent on the error rate, written dφ/dt = −η∇φER (its equation 4).
  2. The split. That gradient factors into ∂ER/∂β times how the parameters change the precision (equation 5). Only the first factor depends on the difficulty Δ, so the best difficulty is the one that maximises ∂ER/∂β.
  3. The bell curve. Our working from the paper’s set-up: with Gaussian noise the error rate is the tail beyond z = βΔ, the distance from the boundary in units of noise. Its slope with respect to β is proportional to z times the bell curve’s height at z, and the paper’s equation 9 contains the same product. That product peaks where its derivative, the height times (1 − z²), is zero, which is at z = 1.
  4. The answer. The tail beyond one unit is 0.1587. Because the best distance is always one unit of whatever the noise currently is, the paper notes that the optimum is constant as precision grows, so the best way to train is “clamping the error rate during training at a fixed value”.
  • 15.87%error rate at one noise unit from the boundary
  • 68.27%share of a bell curve within one standard deviation
  • ~21 monthsfrom receipt to publication (computed)

Wikipedia’s article on the normal distribution gives[3] the share within one standard deviation of the middle as 68.27%. That leaves 31.73% outside, and half of it, 15.87%, sits in the tail on the wrong side of the boundary. So the 15.87% is nothing more exotic than the “68” of the 68–95–99.7 rule, cut in half and turned upside down.

How sharp is the peak in distance?

The speed falls away on both sides of the peak. A stimulus half a unit from the boundary, which you get wrong about 31% of the time, still teaches at about 73% of the best speed. Move out to two units, where you are wrong only 2.3% of the time, and the speed falls to about 45%. Stimuli three units away, almost never missed, teach about 5% as fast.

Two clocks: fixed error rate against fixed difficulty

The paper compares two ways to train. If you clamp the error rate, the precision grows as the square root of time, β(t) = √(β₀² + 2ηKf(t − t₀)) (equation 8). If you keep the difficulty Δ fixed instead, there is no closed form for Gaussian noise, but an approximation at long times gives β growing as the square root of the logarithm of t, which the authors describe as exponentially slower.

The practical reading, ours and not the authors’: a task that stays the same difficulty stops teaching, because the learner outgrows it, while a task that follows the learner keeps teaching at a steady pace. That is the case for the adaptive levels in a game.

The family it belongs to

The paper’s introduction places the idea among old ones. In animal learning it is behind shaping and fading, where a complex task is taught by raising its difficulty step by step. It is visible in the “levels” of nearly every video game, which push the player up once a performance criterion is met. In machine learning it appears as curriculum learning and self-paced learning. The discussion adds two psychological relatives: the desirable difficulties framework, in which the difficulty has to be of a useful kind such as spacing, and the region of proximal learning, built on the work of Piaget and Vygotsky, where the best difficulty is just beyond the learner’s present ability. The authors write that in all these areas the best difficulty is usually found by experiment, and offer a derivation for the special case of binary choices and gradient-descent learners.

When there is no sweet spot

The paper is careful about the limits. A Bayesian learner, which keeps every example and updates its beliefs exactly, ends up the same whatever order the examples arrive in, so it would learn equally well from hard cases first or easy cases first. That is why the authors say not all learning models will show a sweet spot. They add that the maths applies to any process that changes the precision of the brain’s representations, and they name attention, engagement and cognitive control as examples.

The twist

The Perceptron, one of the paper’s test learners, changes its weights only when it makes a mistake. You might therefore expect it to learn most when it is wrong most often. The authors raise exactly that expectation and then show it is false: because its rule is still gradient descent, it learns fastest at the same 15.87%, not at 50%. A learner that only learns from errors still does best when it is right about five times in six.
