Why the hard way feels worse and works better

The psychologist Robert Bjork has a name for practice that feels slow and sticks: desirable difficulties.

Here is a rude fact about learning: how well you do while practising is a poor guide to how well you’ll remember it later.

In 1994, the psychologist Robert Bjork gave a name to the strange gap between the two. He called the useful kind of struggle desirable difficulties. They are ways of practising that slow you down and make you feel worse at the time, and that tend to produce better long-term learning.

The usual suspects

  • Testing yourself instead of rereading. It feels clumsy, and it works.
  • Spacing practice over days instead of cramming it into one night.
  • Interleaving: mixing different kinds of problems instead of doing them in neat blocks.
  • Delayed feedback, in some cases, as long as the feedback reliably arrives and you actually read it.

What they have in common is that each one makes the easy, fluent feeling go away. Easy practice produces strong short-term performance, and that is very easy to mistake for lasting learning. You ace the practice round, feel great, and then find out next month that it mostly didn’t stay.

“Desirable” is doing a lot of work

Not every difficulty is a good one. Reading a textbook in a font you can barely see is difficult; it is not desirable. The useful difficulties are the ones that make you do the thinking the skill actually needs: retrieving, choosing, connecting. Struggling with a bad interface just makes you grumpy.

That’s the line we try to walk with Ziggle. We want the puzzle to be hard in the right places and smooth everywhere else. The clue can be tricky. The button you press to submit your guess should never be.

Where Ziggle builds them in

  • Word Cloud starts you with two clues. Extra clues and letters cost sparks, so the default is to try retrieving first.
  • Letter Lock’s hard mode makes you use every clue you’ve found, which forces more careful thinking about each guess.
  • The typing trainer waits for the right key. It’s slower at first than barrelling on with mistakes. It’s also how clean habits form.
  • The archive and practice modes let you come back to a kind of puzzle days later, which is spacing in disguise.

How to tell if a difficulty is working for you

Ask one question: “Am I doing the thinking the skill needs?” If yes, keep going, even though it feels slower. If you’re mostly fighting the setup (bad lighting, noise, a confusing app), fix the setup. And then judge your progress a week later, not in the moment. The moment always lies a little.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Performance is not learning

Elizabeth and Robert Bjork put the whole idea into one distinction[3]. Performance is what you can see and measure during practice. Learning, the lasting change in what you know, has to be inferred, and current performance can be a highly unreliable guide to it. The distinction is old. In latent-learning experiments, animals let to explore a maze with no reward looked aimless but had learned a good deal, which showed once a reward appeared. In motor skills, learning continued across trials in which fatigue held performance down. The Bjorks’ point is the reverse case: performance can climb fast during practice with little learning to show for it once you wait or change the setting.

Two strengths in memory

Their theory (Bjork and Bjork, 1992) separates storage strength, how deeply entrenched and linked a memory is, from retrieval strength, how accessible it is right now. Current performance reflects retrieval strength alone, but storage strength is what slows forgetting and speeds relearning. The conditions that raise retrieval strength fastest differ from the ones that build storage strength. So a learner who reads retrieval strength as learning will prefer the worse conditions. The chapter’s claim follows: when a skill or fact is as accessible as it can be, little or no learning results from more practice.

Four difficulties, four experiments

The mistaken forecasts are as informative as the scores. In a keystroke study the people who had practised interleaved predicted their next-day performance closely, while the blocked group were markedly overconfident (Simon and Bjork, 2001). In the painters’ study the chapter shows that most participants judged blocking to have helped them more, the opposite of what the test showed. Feeling you are learning is a poor meter.

Why does mixing help?

The chapter offers two accounts. One is that resolving the interference between different topics makes you notice similarities and differences, building a higher-order representation that helps retention and transfer. The other is reloading: after A, B and C the memory for A has to be loaded again, and the loading is itself learning. A then A again involves none of that. Both fit the storage and retrieval picture, in which losing a little retrieval strength is the chance to gain storage strength.

Tests as the hardest, best study event

The chapter pairs these with the generation effect, the long-term benefit of producing an answer over being given it, which it says rivals spacing in generality. Looking up, or being told, what you could have worked out robs you of a learning opportunity. A test or retrieval attempt, even without any feedback, can beat rereading in the long run, and the authors warn about the “tape recorder” model of memory, in which reading again writes the material in. Tests also show you what you do not know and, in a 2009 result they cite, can make later study more effective even when you answer wrongly.

The test of a good difficulty

Wikipedia’s article gives three guidelines[4]. The processing at the time of learning should match the processing at the time of retrieval and during practice, and the task must be possible. Too hard a task may put the learner off and prevent full processing. It mentions the challenge point framework, with an optimal challenge point between the task’s difficulty and the learner’s skill, and a combined method called 3R (read, recite, review), in which students beat rereaders in one experiment. The Bjorks make the same point from the other side: for a learner without the background to respond, a difficulty is undesirable.

Where the hard way meets the 85% rule

The 85% paper cites desirable difficulties as one of the relatives of its own result. A difficulty that stretches you and leaves you right about five times in six is what both ideas describe, one from experiments and one from equations. Our reading: the Bjorks tell you which kinds of difficulty are worth having, and the 85% paper suggests how much.

One caution from Wikipedia: most of the research behind these effects lasts hours or days, and it is still unknown what the effect is over decades. The article reports Harry Bahrick’s result that a substantial part of high-school Spanish stays for life, a store he named permastore, and that spaced study sessions helped.

The twist

The Bjorks say they came to this through students. Elizabeth Bjork was prompted by students who came to her office hours lamenting how hard they had studied before performing poorly on an exam. Robert’s route was through forgetting: why inducing it so often improves later learning. Asked about the real-world impact of the work, they answered: “Overall, the impact has been slight.”
