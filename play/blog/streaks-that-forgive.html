Streaks that forgive

Losing a streak hurts more than building one feels good. That’s why Ziggle’s streak has freezes, and why any one daily counts.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 24 April 2026

In 1979, the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published prospect theory, the idea that people weigh losses more heavily than equal gains. Later work put the ratio at roughly two to one: losing £10 stings about as much as winning £20 pleases.

Streaks run straight into this. A 40-day streak is lovely; losing it on day 41 because of a busy Tuesday can feel bad enough that people quit the game altogether.

How Ziggle’s streak is softer

  • Any one daily keeps it. You don’t need all eight. A two-minute word search counts.
  • Freezes. Every seven-day run earns a freeze (you can bank up to two). Miss a day and a freeze covers it automatically.
  • The archive never counts. Replaying a past day is for fun; it can’t fake a streak, and it can’t break one either.
  • Best streak stays. Your longest run is shown alongside the current one, so a broken streak doesn’t erase what you did.

Why not drop streaks entirely?

Because a gentle nudge to come back is genuinely useful for building a habit. The trick is making the nudge kind, so a missed day is a shrug, not a reason to give up.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Where the two-to-one comes from

Prospect theory was published by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in 1979, and it was cited when Kahneman was awarded the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics. It describes how people actually judge gains and losses, as against how a perfectly rational agent would. The article’s own example[3]: for some people, the pain of losing $1,000 can only be made up for by the pleasure of earning $2,000. In its first draft, which the economist Richard Thaler received in 1976, the theory was called “value theory”; Kahneman later said the new name, prospect theory, was meaningless.

Two ideas carry most of the weight. First, people judge outcomes against a reference point, usually their present situation, and not against an absolute amount. Second, the value curve is steeper for losses than for gains, which is loss aversion: the same change looks worse as a loss than it looks good as a gain. The loss aversion article adds that, empirically, losses tend to be treated as if they were about twice as large as an equal gain[4], and that it should not be confused with risk aversion, which is valuing an uncertain outcome at less than its expected value.

For a streak, the reference point is obvious: the number on the screen. A 40-day streak is something you own, and breaking it is not “going back to zero” but losing 40 days at once. A related finding is the endowment effect, the fact that people value a thing they own more than an identical thing they do not. Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler first explained it by loss aversion in 1990. The article notes that marketers use trial periods and rebates for just this reason, because a buyer values a good more once it counts as theirs.

The same facts, framed differently

The framing point matters for design. The article observes that the same price change, shown as a $5 discount or as a $5 surcharge avoided, has a significant effect on what consumers do. A streak counter can be framed either way too: “you have lost your streak” is a loss; “your best run was 40 days and you are on day 1” is a restart. Ziggle’s home page keeps the best streak beside the current one for that reason.

What Ziggle’s streak actually does

The freeze rule is simple, but its effect is easier to see by running it. We ran the real streak code on a set of play patterns (computed), where the streak shown counts days you played:

  • 7days played earn one freeze
  • 2freezes you can keep
  • 1 in 8days can be missed, at the very most (computed)

The number worth remembering is the ratio. A freeze is earned for every seven days played, and used freezes reset that count, so over a long run you can miss at most about one day in eight, and only in the pattern of seven days played, one covered. That is a lot more forgiving than “never miss a day” and far from “never mind”. It also never makes the streak look longer than it is: of the 100 days in the last row, 88 were days you played, so the streak says 88.

How far can the two-to-one be pushed?

The article is careful about this, and so should we be. Loss aversion is a lab finding about choices, usually involving money, and its size depends on the situation. David Gal (2006), and later Gal and Rucker (2018), argued that many effects once credited to loss aversion, including the endowment effect and the status quo bias, are better explained by psychological inertia[4], meaning a tendency to stay with what you have. A 2019 study by Mrkva, Johnson, Gächter and Herrmann replicated loss aversion in five separate samples and found that its size varies in predictable ways. The article adds that the loss aversion pattern may be less general than first thought, that its size varies with the size of the payoff, and that it may be more noticeable when people compete.

So “a lost streak hurts twice as much” is a useful picture, not a measurement of anybody’s streak. The design principle does not need the exact figure. Whatever the real size of the effect, a number that can vanish in a day will sometimes make people stop, and a number that bends once or twice will not.

The twist

Prospect theory also says people give too much weight to small chances and too little to large ones. The article’s example is that a 1% chance can feel like 5%, and a 99% chance like 95%. Streak rules deal in exact numbers, and the exact numbers here are kind: with two freezes banked, a run is safe against up to two missed days in a row.
