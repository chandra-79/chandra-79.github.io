Bulls, cows and the five-letter lock

Letter Lock belongs to a family of guessing games that is older than most of the board games in your cupboard.

A secret, a guess, and a little feedback about how close you came. That loop is one of the oldest in puzzling.

Bulls and Cows

Long before it had a box, Bulls and Cows was played with pencil and paper. One player picks a secret number with no repeated digits. The other guesses, and is told how many digits are right and in the right place (bulls) and how many are right but in the wrong place (cows).

Jotto and Mastermind

In 1955, Morton M. Rosenfeld published Jotto, a version with five-letter words: you only learn how many letters your guess shares with the secret. In 1970, Mordecai Meirowitz invented Mastermind, with coloured pegs and the same bulls-and-cows feedback, and it became a worldwide bestseller.

From TV to phones

The word version reached television with Lingo in the late 1980s, and phones in 2021 with Wordle, whose coloured squares turned a quiet logic game into something people shared every morning.

Where Letter Lock fits

Letter Lock keeps the core: six tries at a five-letter word, with each letter marked as in place, elsewhere in the word, or absent. What we added is about understanding your guesses, not hiding the answer better:

  • A coach that can tell you how many words were still possible after each guess, so you can see which guesses really narrowed things down.
  • Hard mode, which makes you use every clue you have uncovered.
  • Endless practice locks alongside the daily, and a verified leaderboard that checks every result.

It is a very old idea. It is still a very good one.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Bulls and Cows, in detail

The Wikipedia article says the game is also known as cows and bulls, or pigs and bulls, and that it predates the board game Mastermind. Each player writes down a secret number of four digits, all different, and the players take turns guessing. A digit that is right and in the right place is a bull; a digit that is right but in the wrong place is a cow. The article’s example: with the secret 4271 and the guess 1234, the answer is one bull (the 2) and two cows (the 4 and the 1). A version with letters in place of digits, which must spell a five-letter word, is also described, and the article says its word-based variant predates the game show Lingo and the web game Wordle.

It had a second life on computers. A program called MOO was written by Frank King at the Cambridge Computer Laboratory sometime before the summer of 1970[1], for the multi-user system on the Titan machine. It became so popular that the administrators had to bring in measures to stop it clogging the system. It was ported to the Multics system at MIT in 1972 and rewritten for early Unix at Bell Labs, and a version called BASIC MOO was published for PDP computers in 1971. A FOCAL version for the PDP-8 later became the basis for Milton Bradley’s handheld game Comp IV. These programs kept league tables of players’ scores, and the article notes that protecting the league table’s integrity became a popular case study for researchers in computer security. It is the same problem Ziggle’s leaderboards face today.

Mastermind, and what the mathematics says

Mastermind was invented in 1970 by Mordecai Meirowitz, an Israeli postmaster and telecommunications expert. After showing it at the Nuremberg International Toy Fair he sold the idea to Invicta Plastics, whose founder, Edward Jones-Fenleigh, refined it further. It went on sale in 1971 to 1972. The codemaker hides four pegs of six colours, and the codebreaker gets a red or black key peg for each peg right in colour and place, and a white one for each peg right in colour but in the wrong place.

With four holes and six colours there are 64 = 1,296 possible codes. In 1976 Donald Knuth showed that the codebreaker can always win in five guesses or fewer[3], using a minimax method: after each reply, remove the codes that are no longer possible, and choose the next guess that leaves the fewest codes in the worst case. In 1993 Kenji Koyama and Tony Lai showed by exhaustive search that the best average is 5,625 ÷ 1,296, about 4.340 guesses, with a worst case of six. For Bulls and Cows the article reports that any four-digit secret can be found within seven turns, and that the average length of the best possible game is 26,274 ÷ 5,040, about 5.21 turns (computed from the fractions).

Counting the secrets

How much does one guess have to tell you? A simple counting argument gives a floor. If there are N possible secrets and a guess can get R different replies, one guess can at best split the secrets into R groups, so you need at least logR N guesses in the worst case. Letter Lock’s feedback is rich: three marks for each of five letters, up to 35 = 243 different replies, against 14 for Bulls and Cows. With 809 answers the counting floor is two guesses (809 is more than 243, so one guess cannot always settle it), which is why a good first guess is worth studying. The real game is harder than the floor, because the replies are very uneven: a few are common and most are rare.

Jotto, and the reply that only counts

Jotto was invented in 1955 by Morton M. Rosenfeld[2] and sold by his New York company, Jotto Corp. Each player writes a secret five-letter dictionary word. On your turn you guess a five-letter word and your opponent says how many letters of the guess match a different letter in the secret, wherever they sit. The article’s example: with the secret OTHER and the guess PEACH, the E and the H match, so the reply is “2”. Playing it means keeping a list of letters crossed out of the alphabet. A strong plan for five letters, the article says, is to attack the middle three positions with vowels.

The variants show the family resemblance. In the six-letter version, played with pencil and paper in UK computer departments at least as far back as 1970, only exact matches count. A computer game of the same name, Sixicon, appeared in 1979. Three-letter Jotto is played entirely in the head, so only exact matches count, to keep it from being too hard. And the TV game show Lingo, the article says, is largely a Jotto variant in which the player is told which matching letters are in the correct position and which are not, with words chosen by the show and a limited number of guesses.

The twist

The article on Bulls and Cows says its word version is older than Lingo and Wordle, and the Jotto article gives 1955 as the year it was invented. The “new” word game of 2021 is the youngest member of a family that is at least seventy years old, and in its numbers form, perhaps a century.
