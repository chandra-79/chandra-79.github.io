From 176 tiny pictures to 3,953 emoji

A pager heart, a phone company in Japan, a museum, and why your calendar emoji says July 17.

Emoji feel like they’ve always been here, like the alphabet or the weather. They haven’t. They’re younger than most of the people who use them.

Before emoji were emoji

Pictures in messages are older than the name suggests. In 1997, the phone company SoftBank’s J-Phone arm launched the SkyWalker DP-211SW with a set of 90 tiny pictures. Researchers have since found earlier sets still: a Sharp device from 1988 holds what Wikipedia calls the earliest known emoji set that resembles today’s keyboards, and a 1988 manual for a Toshiba word processor already uses the Japanese word 絵文字 (“emoji”) beside a set of symbols.

The set most people call the first arrived in 1999. Shigetaka Kurita designed 176 characters for Docomo’s i-mode phones, each on a tiny grid of pixels: weather, hearts, faces, a mobile phone, a bowl of noodles. In October 2016, the Museum of Modern Art in New York added that original set to its collection. Not bad for something drawn with a handful of pixels.

Becoming a world standard

For years, emoji only worked within one Japanese phone company. Send one to a friend on another network and it might arrive as a blank box. The fix was to add emoji to Unicode, the standard that gives every character in every writing system its own number.

In October 2010, Unicode 6.0 brought in a standard set of 722 emoji. From then on, a smiling face sent from one phone was the same character on another, even if each company drew it slightly differently.

Then they took over

  • 2015: skin-tone modifiers arrived, based on the Fitzpatrick scale dermatologists use. The same year, Oxford Dictionaries made 😂 its Word of the Year, which delighted some people and appalled others.
  • 2017: the flags of England, Scotland and Wales were approved, using a brand-new mechanism called tag sequences.
  • September 2025: Unicode 17.0 brought the recommended total to 3,953, including an orca, a trombone, a treasure chest and a distorted face (for those days). The same year, Docomo retired its original set.
  • September 2026: Unicode 18.0 (released on 16 September) added 9 more emoji and 10 more sequences, taking the total to 3,972. The title of this post keeps the number it had when we wrote it.

Why the calendar says July 17

Look closely at the calendar emoji on many Apple devices and it shows July 17. That’s the date Apple announced its iCal calendar app in 2002. Some fans started calling July 17 “World Emoji Day”, which is why it gets celebrated in the middle of summer. It’s a lovely example of how emoji culture works: half official standard, half inside joke.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The word, and the first sets

Wikipedia’s article on emoji says[1] the word comes from Japanese e (絵, “picture”) plus moji (文字, “character”), and that the resemblance to the English words emotion and emoticon is purely coincidental. The article also gives the more recent finds behind the post’s timeline: the earliest known set that resembles today’s emoji keyboards is on the 1988 Sharp PA-8500, uncovered in 2024, and the earliest known use of the word 絵文字 beside a recognisable set of symbols is in the manual of the 1988 Toshiba JW-95F Rupo word processor, whose set includes a seahorse. SoftBank’s 1997 set came to light in a 2019 Emojipedia article, which is why the 1999 set was long thought to be the first.

What Kurita actually drew

Wikipedia’s article on Shigetaka Kurita says[2] the NTT DoCoMo pager heart was the seed: it led him to design a set of 176 coloured pictograms on a 12 × 12 pixel grid, with one colour per symbol, inspired by the symbolic marks of manga, called manpu, such as a water drop. The article adds a point the post does not make: the general-use symbols (sports, weather, actions) trace back to his set, but the yellow faces we think of as the standard emoji evolved from other emoticon sets and cannot be traced to Kurita. In 2016 the original 176 entered the collection of the Museum of Modern Art, which showed them in Inbox: The Original Emoji. They are also held by M+ in Hong Kong.

How the standard grew

  1. October 2010Unicode 6.0 brings in a standard set of 722 emoji, chosen after feedback from bodies in the United States, Europe and Japan.
  2. 2011Apple’s emoji keyboard reaches people outside Japan, in iOS 5.0.
  3. June 2014Unicode 7.0 adds the Webdings and Wingdings characters, about 250 more emoji.
  4. June 2015Unicode 8.0 adds 41 emoji and five skin-tone modifiers, U+1F3FB to U+1F3FF, based on the Fitzpatrick scale.
  5. 2015Oxford Dictionaries picks 😂 as its Word of the Year.

Our arithmetic: 3,953 is 22.5 times Kurita’s 176, and 5.5 times the 722 of 2010. In Unicode 18.0, the 3,972 emoji are built from only 1,447 characters, about 2.75 emoji per character, because skin tones, flags and joined sequences are made by combining characters. Of those characters, 26 are the regional indicators for flags and 12 are the bases for keycaps.

What is in Ziggle’s learner

The first line is the one to notice. Of the learner’s 1,624 emoji, 697 (43%) are from the original 2010 set. The octopus (U+1F419) is among them. The learner’s astronaut, the gender-neutral one, is a person (U+1F9D1), a zero-width joiner and a rocket (U+1F680), and arrived in 2019 as a sequence.

Why the calendar says July 17

Following an Apple tradition, the calendar emoji on Apple products always shows 17 July, the date in 2002 when Apple announced its iCal application. That led some Apple users to nickname the day World Emoji Day. Other fonts show different dates or none, which the article offers as an example of how the same code point is drawn differently by each vendor.

The twist

The set in the museum was long called the first, yet the article’s sources show sets from 1988 and 1997, and the yellow faces that now stand for emoji do not trace back to it. The first emoji, the first-known emoji and the emoji that took over the world are three different stories.
