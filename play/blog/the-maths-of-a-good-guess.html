Letter Lock and the maths of a good guess

Your first guess is worth up to 7.9 bits of information. Most of us waste about half of it. A friendly introduction to Claude Shannon.

Letter Lock looks like a word game. Underneath, it’s a game about information. And the person who gave us the maths for that was an engineer worrying about telephone lines.

A bit is a question with two answers

In 1948, Claude Shannon published “A Mathematical Theory of Communication”. In it, he measured information in bits. One bit is what you learn from a fair coin toss: one of two equally likely outcomes. Two bits pick one of four, three bits one of eight. To pick one thing out of n equally likely things, you need log₂(n) bits.

Letter Lock’s daily answer comes from a list of 809 everyday words. So finding today’s word means collecting about 9.7 bits of information (log₂ 809). You have six guesses to do it.

How much can one guess tell you?

Each tile comes back one of three ways: right spot, wrong spot, or not in the word. Five tiles, three possibilities each, gives 3⁵ = 243 possible patterns. So in a perfect world, one guess could tell you up to log₂ 243 ≈ 7.9 bits. If every guess did that, you’d nearly always be done in two.

Real guesses do less, because most patterns never happen. “All grey” turns up a lot; some patterns no word could produce. The best guesses are the ones that spread the possible answers as evenly as possible across the patterns, so that whatever comes back, the list shrinks a lot.

Cutting the list beats being right

This is the counterintuitive bit. Early on, a guess that can’t possibly be the answer can still be your best move, if it splits the remaining words well. A guess that might be right but only tests letters you already know tells you almost nothing when it’s wrong.

That’s exactly what the Lock coach shows after each game: how many of the 809 possible answers were still in play after each guess. A decent opener can bring it down to twenty or so. Watch which guesses made the number drop the most. Usually it wasn’t the one that felt cleverest.

Practical tips, now with maths

  • Open with common letters. E, T, A, O, I, N and S appear most in English words, so testing them gives you the most chances to learn something.
  • Don’t retest what you know. A green letter in the same place in your second guess is a wasted slot (except in hard mode, which makes you keep them on purpose).
  • Beware the trap. When you’re down to _ATCH and five candidates (batch, catch, hatch, latch, match), guessing them one by one might take five turns. A guess that tests B, C, H, L and M at once can split them in one.

Is hard mode harder?

Yes, and now you know why. Hard mode forbids exactly that list-splitting trick: every guess has to fit what you’ve already found. You give up some information per guess in exchange for a puzzle that tests how well you can hold every clue in your head at once. Different skill, same five tiles.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Where the bit comes from

Wikipedia’s article on Shannon’s paper says[2] it formally introduced the term bit, and that it was published in 1948 as the founding work of information theory; a 1949 book edition added an overview by Warren Weaver for a general audience. The entropy article gives the working definition: the entropy of an outcome is the sum of −p × log p over its possibilities, and base 2 gives the unit of bits. A fair coin is exactly 1 bit. A fair die is log2 6 bits.

How much of the 7.9 bits does a real guess deliver?

The post says one guess is worth up to 7.9 bits. In practice the best of all 5,049 guesses delivers 6.03 (CRATE), which is 76% of the ceiling (computed). Two things hold it down. First, only 238 of the 243 patterns ever occur, since the five with four greens and one yellow are impossible. Second, the patterns are very unevenly used. For CRATE, 127 patterns occur, the commonest holds 53 of the 809 answers, and 47 patterns point to a single answer.

The 9.66 bits, spent

By the second guess the program has gained 9.3 of the 9.66 bits it needs. That is why it solves 90 answers on guess two and the great majority on guess three, and none takes more than 4.

Is hard mode harder? We measured it

The post says hard mode costs some information per guess and tests a different skill. The numbers agree: a second guess is worth 3.28 bits in normal mode and 3.16 in hard mode, the average rises only slightly, from 3.05 to 3.08 guesses, and the cost shows up in the tail: the worst case goes from 4 guesses to 6, and 10 answers need five or six.

The _ATCH trap, checked

The post’s example, five words ending in ATCH, works: among BATCH, CATCH, HATCH, LATCH and MATCH, one allowed guess separates all five at once (CLIMB). Letter Lock’s own answer list is a little different. Its five _ATCH answers are MATCH, WATCH, CATCH, PATCH, BATCH, and HATCH and LATCH are allowed guesses but not answers. For those five answers no allowed guess separates all of them: the best we found separates 4 (for example BLIMP), so there is no one-guess escape and you could need a second guess to separate the last pair.

Which letters really come up most?

The post lists E, T, A, O, I, N and S as the commonest English letters. In Letter Lock’s own 809 answers the order differs. Counting the number of words that contain each letter, the top ten are E (407), R (338), A (332), T (277), O (235), S (227), I (226), L (208), N (195), H (167). E is in 50.3% of the answers, and R is second. N is ninth.

The twist

Shannon’s 1948 paper was about sending messages through a noisy channel, not about word games. Our reading: the same arithmetic explains why CRATE beats ADIEU, and why “which word is most likely?” is the wrong question when the real one is “which guess shrinks my list most?”.
