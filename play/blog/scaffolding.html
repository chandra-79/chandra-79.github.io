Scaffolding: why the cube trainer teaches seven steps first

Good teaching holds up the parts you can’t do yet, then takes the support away. The cube coach is built on that idea.

Training design · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 4 September 2026

In 1976 the psychologists David Wood, Jerome Bruner and Gail Ross described how adults help children with a task that is just beyond them. They called it scaffolding: the helper handles the parts the learner can’t manage yet, and gradually withdraws support as the learner takes over.

It fits neatly with Lev Vygotsky’s earlier idea of the zone of proximal development: the band of tasks you can’t do alone but can do with help. That band is where learning happens fastest.

A Rubik’s Cube is a perfect case

Solving a cube from scratch, unaided, is beyond most people. With the right support, almost anyone can do it in an afternoon. So the cube trainer is built as a set of scaffolds that come down one at a time:

  • Seven small steps (white cross, white corners, middle edges, then four steps for the yellow side), each practised on its own.
  • A live coach that reads your cube, says which step you are on and how far through it you are, and shows the next few turns. It re-plans if you turn something else.
  • Then less help: a full solve with the coach hidden unless you ask for it, then faster methods (two-look, then full) once the beginner method is automatic.

The aim is for every scaffold to become unnecessary. When you stop opening the coach, it has done its job.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Three features, and a good deal of care

The Wikipedia article says scaffolding has three essential features. The first is interaction between learner and expert, and it has to be collaborative. The second is that the learning happens in the learner’s zone of proximal development, so the expert must know what the learner can already do and work a little beyond it. The third is that the support is gradually removed as the learner becomes more proficient. The name comes from the frames on a building site, which the article describes as adjustable and temporary support for the building going up.

It also lists what a teacher has to do for scaffolding to work. Choose a task that is neither too hard nor too easy and that is interesting enough to keep the learner involved. Anticipate the errors the learner is likely to make, so that they can be steered away from dead ends. Apply the supports during the task. And look after emotions, because scaffolding is not only cognitive: the helper may need to manage the frustration and loss of interest the learner can feel, and encouragement is part of it.

Where the word came from

The article says scaffolding theory was first introduced in the late 1950s by Jerome Bruner[2], who used the term for young children’s oral language learning. Helped by their parents when they first speak, children are given informal formats within which learning is made easier. One format that Bruner and his postdoctoral student Anat Ninio studied in detail is joint picture-book reading, where the adult and child look and talk together. By contrast, bedtime stories and read-alouds are described as book-centred, without that interaction. The 1976 paper by Wood, Bruner and Ross, “The role of tutoring in problem solving”, is among the article’s references.

Scaffolding was inspired by Lev Vygotsky’s idea of an expert helping a novice. The zone of proximal development article says Vygotsky (1896 to 1934) introduced the concept in the last three years of his life[3], and that his early death left it mostly incomplete. He developed it to argue against using academic knowledge tests as the measure of a child’s intelligence. His question was whether two children who score the same on a test are at the same level of development, and he concluded that they are not: it is better to look at what a child can do alone and what they can do with an adult’s help. The helper is called the “more knowledgeable other”, and a good teacher, he said, should not set material that is too hard and simply pull the students along.

The zone, in one line

The zone of proximal development is the space between what a learner can do unsupported and what they cannot do even with support. Inside it they can succeed, but only with help. The article adds the limit that makes the design problem real: a learner can fail even with support if the task is cognitively impossible for them. A cube solved cold is far outside most people’s zone, which is why the trainer breaks it into steps, each inside the zone.

Soft scaffolds, hard scaffolds

The article distinguishes two kinds. Contingent or “soft” scaffolding is improvised by the helper[2] in response to what the learner needs at that moment. Embedded or “hard” scaffolding is planned in advance for a task that is known to be difficult, such as hints prepared for a lesson on the Pythagorean theorem. It also names reciprocal scaffolding, in which a group share the support among themselves, and technical scaffolding, in which computers play the expert, using web links, tutorials and help pages.

The article says whether a strategy counts as good scaffolding depends on how it is used in practice, and more specifically on whether it is applied contingently and is part of a process of fading and handing responsibility over to the learner. A scaffold that never comes down is just a crutch.

Does it last?

The article cites a study that recorded mothers’ spoken scaffolding as they played with their three- and four-year-old children. When the children were six, their working memory and language skills were related to how much verbal scaffolding their mothers had given, and scaffolding worked best when the mother made explicit conceptual links during play. The article reads that as evidence that the quality of scaffolding matters, not just the quantity. It is an association in one study, so it is a hint, not a promise that more help always means more learning.

The argument about help

There is a long argument in education about how much guidance to give. Approaches grouped under “minimal guidance”, such as discovery learning and project-based learning, hold that learners learn best by working things out for themselves. One strand of criticism, from cognitive load theory, is that this does not fit the way beginners’ minds work and is inefficient for them in particular. Critics also say there is little empirical evidence for learner-centred approaches compared with teacher-led ones, and that minimal guidance lacks worked examples and gives fewer chances to practise. The article’s conclusion is to drop the either-or and adapt the style to the learner’s expertise, with experts needing less direct instruction. That is the same fading idea as in the post on worked examples, from the other direction.

The twist

Vygotsky died in 1934 while still in his thirties. His work on the zone of proximal development was left mostly incomplete, and the article says the definition has been expanded and modified since. The idea has been scaffolded by other people ever since.
