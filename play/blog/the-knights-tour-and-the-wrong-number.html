The knight’s tour, and the count that was wrong for a year

A knight can visit every square of a chessboard once. How many ways? A famous published answer turned out to be more than double the truth.

A knight moves two squares one way and one the other. A knight’s tour is a route that lands on every square of the board exactly once. It is an old puzzle: Euler studied it in 1759, and the history is older still: the Wikipedia account describes a fully symmetric closed tour in the work of an Indian author, Nilakantha, that predates Euler’s by at least sixty years.

A tour you can follow

One tidy method is Warnsdorff’s rule (1823): always jump to the square from which the knight has the fewest onward moves. We ran it from the corner of an 8×8 board. The numbers are the order of the visits:

How many tours are there?

In 1996 Martin Löbbing and Ingo Wegener published a paper whose title announced the answer: The number of knight’s tours equals 33,439,123,484,294. The following year the mathematician Brendan McKay pointed out a mistake in the count. The correct number of closed tours on the 8×8 board (ones that end a knight’s move from where they began) is 13,267,364,410,532[2] when a tour and its reverse count as one, or 26,534,728,821,064[3] when direction matters.

  • 26,534,728,821,064directed closed tours on 8×8
  • 13,267,364,410,532undirected: half as many, since each can be run in reverse
  • 33,439,123,484,294the figure published in 1996, later corrected
The twist

The wrong number came from a carefully built computer method, and the correction came from an independent recount by Brendan McKay. A second count is the closest thing mathematics has to a lab replication, and it is why the encyclopedia of integer sequences today notes bluntly that the 1996 authors “incorrectly gave”[2] their figure.

A tour that is almost magic

One historical tour is also a semimagic square: every row and column adds to 260. Its diagonals do not, and it is known that no fully magic knight’s tour exists on an 8×8 board, although they do on larger ones. A puzzle inside a puzzle.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Which boards allow a closed tour?

On a standard board a knight can return to its start. For rectangles, Allen Schwenk proved a complete rule in 1991: for an m × n board with m ≤ n, a closed tour is always possible unless one of three things holds: m and n are both odd; m is 1, 2 or 4; or m is 3 and n is 4, 6 or 8. A 5 × 5 board, for instance, can never have a closed tour (both sides are odd), though it can have an open one.

Warnsdorff’s rule has been pushed a long way

The “fewest onward moves” rule described above is a heuristic, a rule of thumb that usually works and is not guaranteed to. It has been used to build a very large 130 × 130[3] open tour. Ties between equally good squares can be broken in several ways, including methods by Pohl and by Squirrel and Cull.

Tours in literature and in chess

A knight’s tour has even shaped a novel: the order of the chapters in Georges Perec’s Life A User’s Manual follows a 10 × 10 tour. In a game at the 2010 World Chess Championship, Viswanathan Anand played 13 consecutive knight moves[3] (using both knights) against Veselin Topalov, which commentators joked looked like an attempt at a tour.

Older than Euler, by nearly nine centuries

Wikipedia’s article puts the earliest known reference[4] in the ninth century, in Rudrata’s Sanskrit work on poetry, where a half-board knight’s tour appears as an “arrangement in the steps of a horse”. In the fourteenth century the poet Vedanta Desika wrote two verses of 32 letters each, where the second can be obtained from the first by a knight’s tour on a 4 × 8 board. Nilakantha’s tours, written about 1600 or 1700, are closed and symmetric, which is why the post says he predates Euler (1759) by at least sixty years.

Counts on smaller boards

Our arithmetic from the table: going from 5 × 5 to 6 × 6 multiplies the count by about 3,841; from 6 × 6 to 7 × 7 by about 24,944; from 7 × 7 to 8 × 8 by about 118,326. No 5 × 5 tour is closed (both sides are odd), which the search confirms: none of the 1,728 ends a knight’s move from where it began.

A rule of thumb that nearly always works

On the 8 × 8 board the plain rule finds a tour from 63 of the 64 squares, and on the 30 × 30 board from 855 of 900. It is weakest on the small odd boards: 12 of 25 starts on 5 × 5 and 18 of 49 on 7 × 7. The article describes it as a heuristic that on many graphs finds a solution in linear time, not a guarantee. Separately, it says tours on most rectangular boards can be built in linear time by dividing the board into pieces, and that a tour exists on every board whose smaller side is at least 5 (Cull and colleagues, and Conrad and colleagues).

The twist

The count that turned out wrong in 1996 and the heuristic from 1823 sit at opposite ends of the same problem: counting every tour is a task for supercomputers, but finding one takes a rule you can run in your head. Easy to find, hard to count is a pattern worth recognising in puzzles.
