The sweet spot: getting it right about 85% of the time

A 2019 paper found a surprisingly precise number for how often learning should go wrong. We read the small print too.

If you always get everything right, you might not be learning very much. If you always get everything wrong, you definitely aren’t. Somewhere in between is a sweet spot, and in 2019 a team of researchers tried to put a number on it.

The eighty five percent rule

Robert Wilson, Amitai Shenhav, Mark Straccia and Jonathan Cohen published “The Eighty Five Percent Rule for Optimal Learning” in Nature Communications. They worked out, mathematically, how hard training should be for a wide family of learning systems, the kind used in artificial neural networks and in models of how animals learn.

The answer came out remarkably precise: learning was fastest when the error rate was about 15.87%[1]. That’s roughly 85% right and 15% wrong. They also found that earlier animal-learning studies fit the same pattern.

The small print (it matters)

  • The core result is for simple two-choice tasks with a clear right and wrong answer. Life is rarely that tidy.
  • The lead author has said it doesn’t mean students should aim for a B average. His softer point: someone who aces everything may be getting less out of it than someone who struggles a bit and keeps up.
  • Applying it to complicated human skills is, as the researchers put it, future work.

So treat 85% as a compass, not a law. If you’re cruising at 100%, it’s time to turn the dial up. If you’re failing most of the time, turn it down.

What this means for your puzzles

A puzzle you can always solve in one go is pleasant, and that’s fine on a tired Tuesday. But if you want to get better, find the level where you sometimes don’t make it.

  • Sudoku: if Medium feels automatic, try Hard for a week. Getting stuck sometimes is the signal you picked well.
  • Letter Lock: if you’re regularly winning in three, switch on hard mode, where every clue must be used.
  • Word Cloud: try solving with no extra clues. Your spark count tells you how often you’re stretching.

Wait, what about typing at 95%?

Good catch. The typing trainer asks for at least 95% accuracy, which sounds like it breaks the rule. It doesn’t, because it measures something different. That 95% is about keystrokes: we want clean habits in your fingers. The challenge, how fast the level asks you to go, is where the stretch lives. You should miss the speed goal fairly often. You just shouldn’t miss it by mashing keys.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the researchers actually did

The paper is open to read in[2] Nature Communications. It sets out to answer one question: for a learner that improves by gradient descent, nudging its settings a little after each error, how hard should the training examples be? For a binary choice, with noise in the learner’s decision, the answer is that learning is fastest when the error rate is about 15.87%. The abstract says the rule holds for a broad class of such learning algorithms, and it tests it on three.

  • A Perceptron. The classic one-layer network, which only changes its settings when it makes a mistake. You might expect it to learn most from a world full of errors; the maths says otherwise. The team simulated it at error rates from 1% to 50% in steps of 1%, with 1,000 simulations of 1,000 trials at each rate (computed: that is 50 million trials), holding the error rate steady by adjusting the difficulty as it learned. Learning was fastest at the optimal rate throughout.
  • A two-layer network on handwritten digits. The network had 400 input units (the image’s pixels), 50 hidden units and one output, and learned either parity (odd or even) or magnitude (below 5 or not) from the MNIST set of 70,000 images, 60,000 for training and 10,000 for testing. The training error rate was clamped between 5% and 30% for 5,000 trials, and the whole process repeated 1,000 times per task. Test accuracy differed by around 10 points between the best and worst training rate, and for both tasks the best was at 85% training accuracy.
  • A model of monkeys learning. The Law and Gold model, which captures both the behaviour and the neural firing of monkeys learning to say which way a patch of dots is moving, also learned fastest at 85%.

  • 1,000 × 1,000simulations × trials, per error rate (Perceptron)

How steep is the sweet spot?

The paper’s equation 9 gives the speed of learning at a fixed training error rate. With Gaussian noise it is proportional to z times the bell-curve height at z, where the error rate is the tail of the curve beyond z. We evaluated it, scaled so that the best is 100% (computed from that equation):

The shape explains the post’s advice. Being too easy is costly (at 1% errors, learning runs at about a quarter of the best speed), and being too hard is costly too, but the top is broad. A learner anywhere between roughly 10% and 25% errors is within about ten points of the best speed. That is the useful reading: 85% is the centre of a comfortable range, not a tightrope.

Where the number changes

The discussion section lists what would move it. The number comes from Gaussian noise. Assume a Laplacian distribution and the optimum is about 82% accuracy; for Cauchy noise it is about 75%. It is for binary choices; with more than two categories the maths would be more complex, and with learning in batches the best difficulty depends on how fast learning is relative to the batch size. And not every learner has a sweet spot at all: the authors point out that a Bayesian learner with perfect memory would learn the same from the same examples in any order, so difficulty ordering would not matter to it.

Is it a rule for people?

The authors are plain about it. They say it accords with the informal intuition of experimenters that engagement is highest when a task is neither too easy nor too hard, and that staircase procedures, which adjust difficulty to hold errors steady, are commonly set to produce about 80–85% accuracy. They also note that, when free to choose, participants tend to pick tasks of intermediate difficulty as they learn. But they say that, to the best of their knowledge, no formal theory had addressed the effect of training accuracy on learning, and that a test with people is an important direction for future work. The result is a theorem about a class of algorithms, supported by simulations. It is an illuminating guess about people, not a measurement of them.

They also link it to older ideas: the zone of proximal development, desirable difficulties (where the difficulty must be of a useful kind, such as spacing), and curriculum learning in computer science. Desirable difficulties adds a point the paper makes itself: the difficulty has to be of a useful kind. Our reading for puzzles is that a puzzle can be hard in an unhelpful way, through unclear rules or a clumsy interface, as well as in a helpful one.

Your own sweet spot, in numbers

Suppose you play all eight dailies for a week. That is 56 puzzles. At 85% clean solves you would expect about 9 of them to go wrong, one or two a day (computed: 56 × 15.87%). Someone who never misses is at 100%, and the rule would suggest the puzzles are easy for learning purposes, though not necessarily for pleasure. If your week looks like that, the dial is the difficulty setting: a harder Sudoku, hard mode in Letter Lock, or no extra clues in Word Cloud.

The twist

The 15.87% is not a rounded guess. It is the area of the bell curve beyond one standard deviation, 0.1587, written as a percentage. The paper’s optimum sits exactly one unit of noise from the answer, which is why the number looks so precise.
