Sleep on it: the overnight puzzle solver

People who slept were about twice as likely to spot a hidden shortcut in a puzzle as people who stayed awake.

Science of practice · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 28 July 2026

Everyone has had this happen. You go to bed stuck on something, and in the morning the answer is just… there, sitting on the pillow like a cat with a mouse.

In 2004, Ullrich Wagner, Jan Born and colleagues tested it properly, in a paper in Nature with the excellent title “Sleep inspires insight”.

The hidden shortcut

Volunteers worked through a number puzzle (the “number reduction task”) that had to be solved step by step. What they weren’t told was that it had a hidden rule: a shortcut that let you jump straight to the answer if you noticed it.

After their first session, people either slept for a full night, stayed awake overnight, or stayed awake during the day for the same length of time. Then they came back and tried again.

The ones who had slept about eight hours were roughly twice as likely to discover the hidden rule as the ones who had stayed awake.

What’s probably going on

Sleep isn’t a pause button for the brain. While you sleep, memories from the day are reorganised and connected with things you already know. Sometimes that reshuffling surfaces a pattern you couldn’t see while you were staring right at it. Researchers are still working out exactly how; what’s clear is that the effect is real enough to show up in a lab.

What this means for your puzzles

  • Stuck at night? Stop. Ziggle saves every puzzle as you go. Close it, sleep, and look again at breakfast. Today’s daily is valid until midnight, and your streak forgives a missed day if breakfast runs late.
  • Do the hard thinking before bed, not the answer-checking. Give your brain something to chew on overnight.
  • Don’t trade sleep for a streak. A puzzle at 1am is a bad deal for the puzzle and for you.

We think “sleep on it” is the most underrated puzzle strategy there is. It requires no skill, costs nothing and you were going to do it anyway.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the 2004 study was, and what came after

The Wikipedia article on sleep and creativity says the 2004 task was a number reduction task: volunteers had to translate a string of digits using two simple rules that reduced it to a single digit, and a hidden third rule gave a shortcut. Of three groups, those who slept for about eight hours were twice as likely, when retested, to find the hidden rule. The article collects several other findings in the same spirit:

  • REM sleep and anagrams. People woken from REM sleep had a 32% advantage on an anagram task[2] over people woken from non-REM sleep. They did not do better than people who had stayed awake, which the article reads as REM sleep offering a different but equally good way of solving problems.
  • Dreaming of a problem. In a 1993 study in Dreaming, Harvard Medical School’s Barrett asked students to think of a problem before sleep. Half of them dreamed about it, and a quarter dreamed of a solution.
  • One minute at sleep onset. A 2021 study in Science Advances found that brain activity in the first stage of sleep, N1, increased creativity after waking, with even one minute enough. The effect vanished if the sleepers reached deeper sleep.
  • Joining facts into a picture. In a relational-memory study, volunteers learned premise pairs such as A is bigger than B, and later had to say which of two items outranked the other, including pairs they had never seen. Twelve hours later, the group who had slept gained a 25% advantage on the inferred pairs over the group who had not.
  • Missing sleep hurts. After 32 hours awake, people did severely worse on tests of flexibility and originality.
  • 2×as likely to find the hidden rule after eight hours of sleep
  • 32%advantage on anagrams after REM against non-REM waking
  • 25%advantage on inferred pairs after sleep

The inference study, worked out

The article says the volunteers learned premise pairs from a hidden ranking A > B > C > D > E. Five items give 10 pairs in all (computed). Only four of them, the neighbours A > B, B > C, C > D and D > E, were taught. The other six had to be inferred: three that skip one item (A > C, B > D, C > E), two that skip two (A > D, B > E) and one that spans the whole chain (A > E). The article tests the taught neighbours (first-degree pairs) and the inferred pairs that skip one item (second-degree: A > C, B > D, C > E). Both groups did well on the taught pairs after 12 hours, but on the inferred pairs the group that had slept gained a 25% advantage over the group that had not. (The article says “five premise pairs” but lists four, so the exact design in the source study may differ.) In short, sleep helped most with the part that required putting facts together, not with the facts they had been told.

The caution: sleeplessness and creativity

The article also includes a section on “creative insomnia”, the idea that poor sleep and creativity go together, and it is careful about it. The famous examples, from Proust writing through the night to a long list of poets, are anecdotes. One study of thirty highly creative New Zealand children and thirty comparison children found more sleep disturbance in the creative group: seventeen of the creative children against eight of the others. Another found that creativity was not significantly related to any dimension of sleep, and a third found that people who fell asleep quickly scored higher on a creativity test. The article’s summary is that no study has shown a causal link[2], and that the relationship is more complicated than previously thought.

Anecdotes about dreams, too, are collected in the article, from Kekulé’s benzene ring to Paul McCartney’s tune for “Yesterday”, and it labels them anecdotal. We would not build a puzzle strategy on them. The controlled studies are the ones we cite.

The twist

Look at what the sleepers found: a rule the volunteers had not been told about. Our reading is that sleep helped with seeing a different kind of answer, not with grinding on the one in front of them. That is why Ziggle saves your board, and why a puzzle left half done at bedtime is a fair plan.
