The 82, the 62 and the 39: reading the habit study properly

The habit paper behind the “66 days” headline starts with 96 people and ends with 39 clean curves. Here is what happened in between.

Popular summaries of habit research tend to quote one number. The 2010 paper by Phillippa Lally and colleagues deserves better, because the funnel it describes is the real finding: how much data survives, and what that means for the average.

The funnel

It began with 96 volunteers[1], each choosing one everyday behaviour to do daily, in the same situation, for 12 weeks. Every day they logged whether they had done it and rated how automatic it felt (the Self-Report Habit Index). Then the numbers shrink:

The researchers fitted an asymptote, a curve that rises and levels off, to each person’s automaticity scores over the 84 days[1]. Performing the behaviour more consistently went with a better fit. They then asked how long each person took to reach 95% of their own plateau.

An answer between 18 and 254

That time ranged from 18 to 254 days[1]. Notice the problem: the study stopped at day 84, so any estimate beyond it is the curve’s prediction, not an observation. The “66 days” everyone quotes is the typical value, and the same abstract warns that the true message is how long it can take and how much people vary.

The sentence to keep

The abstract ends with this: missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process[1]. Single lapses did not reset anything. If you are building a habit, the useful instruction is “resume”, not “start again”.

Why the funnel matters

Each exclusion is reasonable (people who stopped logging cannot be modelled), but it means the 66-day figure describes people who stuck with a dull task for twelve weeks and whose curves behaved. That is probably a more motivated group than average. For anyone else the time may well be longer, and that is not failure; it is the population being described.

Ziggle’s forgiving streak is built on the single-lapse sentence. A game does not need to be stricter than the science.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the volunteers actually did

The BPS Research Digest’s summary of the paper says[2] 96 undergraduates (mean age 27) each chose a new health-related behaviour to repeat once a day for 84 days, and each had to link it to a daily cue. Examples were a 15-minute run before dinner, a piece of fruit with lunch and 50 sit-ups after the morning coffee. Every day they logged on to a website to say whether they had done it the day before and to fill in a self-report of how automatic it felt, with items such as “I do it automatically”, “I do it without thinking” and “I’d find it hard not to do”. UCL’s habit group describes a habit as an action triggered automatically by the situation in which it has been repeated: you get home, you eat a snack, and after enough repetitions getting home prompts the snack.

The funnel, as proportions

Our arithmetic: about two in five of the people who began (40.6%) end up in the group the best-known number describes.

The 18, the 66 and the 254, against the 84

Computed: 66 days is 79% of the study’s length, the fastest person took 21% of it, and the slowest prediction is three times the study’s length. UCL’s account adds that people differed in how strong the habit became as well as how fast: for some it peaked below the halfway mark of the 42-point strength scale and for others at the very top. The digest’s summary of one result is worth keeping next to the headline: even after 84 days, about half the participants had not reached an automaticity score high enough to count as a habit.

What made it longer

The BPS digest reports that exercise behaviours took about one and a half times as long to reach their plateau as eating or drinking ones, and UCL gives the same contrast, simple water drinking against 50 sit-ups. Early repetitions bought the largest gains in automaticity, then the gains dwindled to a plateau. On missed days the digest is more careful than the post’s one sentence: a single missed day had little effect, early or late, which suggests William James overstated the case in 1890 when he said habits must be repeated without exception, but there was some evidence that too many misses, even spread out, reduced the plateau reached.

The researchers’ own limits

The digest says the study depended entirely on participants’ ability to report their own automaticity, and that the amount of data made it hard to draw clear conclusions about the need for consistency. UCL’s advice is that it is unwise to attach a number to the process, because the time depends on who you are and what you are trying to do.

Where the 21 days came from

UCL’s blog traces the 21-day myth[3] to the 1960 book Psycho-Cybernetics by the plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz, who wrote that it takes a minimum of about 21 days for an old mental image to dissolve and a new one to form, citing patients getting used to a new face, phantom limbs after amputation and the weeks it takes a new house to feel like home. The blog suggests habituation, getting used to something, may have been mistaken for habit formation. Scientific American adds that no formal experiment tested the claim.

  1. 1890William James says a habit must be repeated without omission.
  2. 1960Maltz’s Psycho-Cybernetics gives the 21 days.
  3. 1988A book says a behaviour is habitual after being done at least ten times, at least twice a month (per the BPS digest).
  4. 2010Lally and colleagues publish the 84-day study.
  5. 2015A study finds new gym-goers needed at least four sessions a week for six weeks to form an exercise habit (per Scientific American).

Scientific American also reports a later machine-learning study by Camerer and colleagues in which a handwashing habit took a few weeks and an exercise habit about half a year, which fits the digest’s point that the simpler the behaviour and the more often it can be practised, the sooner it settles.

What this means for a game streak

Our reading, not the paper’s: the single-lapse finding is about habit formation in daily behaviours, not about streak design, and the same digest warns that many misses together cost something. A streak that forgives a missed day agrees with the first result and is consistent with the second as long as the days are the exception.

The twist

The most famous number is an average for people who stuck with the task, and about half the participants had not reached a plateau high enough to count as a habit by day 84. “It takes 66 days” reads better as “about nine or ten weeks on average, with some people far quicker and some much slower”.
