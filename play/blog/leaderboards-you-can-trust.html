Leaderboards you can trust

A top score is only fun if it’s real. Ziggle’s server replays every run before it joins the board.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 29 May 2026

Any online leaderboard attracts impossible scores. If a game simply trusts the number your browser sends, someone can send a better number.

Speedrunning communities solved a version of this long ago: a record only counts if there is a video, and volunteers check it. We can’t watch videos, but we can do something similar with maths.

Replay, don’t trust

Every Ziggle daily is built from the date, so the server can rebuild the exact same puzzle. When you post a result, you send your moves, not just your score:

  • Arrow escape: the order you freed the arrows. The server replays them and checks each one really had a clear path.
  • Letter Lock: your guesses. The server checks each is a real word and works out the colours itself.
  • Sudoku: your finished grid and time; the server checks the grid against the rules and refuses times no person could manage.
  • Sum Spark: your chains. The server recalculates each one with the day’s tiles and operators.

If the replay doesn’t match, the score doesn’t join. And a daily rescued with a life never goes on a leaderboard at all.

What it doesn’t need

None of this needs an account, an email address or a tracker. A leaderboard name is optional, and kids mode keeps leaderboards hidden entirely.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The trick that Doom taught

Leaderboards were part of games from the start. Wolfenstein 3D (1992) recorded a “par time” for each level[1], based on the personal records of its designer, John Romero, and the official hint book printed his best times so that players could try to beat them. Earlier games such as Metroid (1986) and Prince of Persia (1989) showed the player’s time at certain points. The article says speedrunning did not draw broad interest until 1993, and that the community is generally thought to have started with Doom.

Doom kept the par time and added something new: it could record and play back gameplay in small files called demos. They were much lighter than video, so people could pass them around bulletin board systems. A demo is a list of what the player did, and the game could run it again and show exactly what happened. That is the same idea as Ziggle’s: do not send the result, send the moves, and let the other side replay them.

  1. 1992Wolfenstein 3D shows a “par time” taken from its designer’s own records.
  2. 1993Doom adds shareable demo files. The speedrunning community is generally traced to it.
  3. Jan 1994A University of Waterloo student, Christina Norman, sets up an FTP server for collecting demos: the LMP Hall of Fame.
  4. Nov 1994Simon Widlake creates COMPET-N, a leaderboard for Doom completion times.
  5. June 1997Quake Done Quick takes speedrunning to a wider audience.
  6. 2003 to 2004Speed Demos Archive, set up for Quake, begins accepting runs for every game.
  7. 2014Speedrun.com starts hosting leaderboards, and by July 2021 it hosted them for more than 20,000 games.

The problem video could not solve

Before Speed Demos Archive broadened out, the article says, speedrun videos were mostly sent to the early record-keeper Twin Galaxies, and they were often never made public. That created verifiability concerns: it was often impossible to know what strategies had gone into a record, which in turn held back the development of speedrunning techniques. Today the runner normally records footage, often with a timer showing splits, and the moderators of each leaderboard review submissions and decide whether they are valid. That is the “volunteers check the video” system the post describes.

It has limits, and the article lists the ways people cheat. The most popular method is splicing: building a run out of video snippets recorded at different times, sometimes with gameplay taken from tool-assisted runs or from other players. At Summer Games Done Quick in 2019 a spliced run was used to claim a world record on Clustertruck, and it was treated with suspicion partly because it had not been officially submitted. The article also describes the 2020 case of the Minecraft runner Dream, whose luck was so extreme that Speedrun.com’s moderators and several commentators considered it exceedingly unlikely to be real. The maths communicator Matt Parker estimated the chance at about 1 in 20 sextillion[1] (a 2 followed by 22 zeros).

What a replay can and cannot prove

A tool-assisted speedrun (TAS) is a run built with emulation tools to make a “theoretically perfect playthrough”. The article describes a TAS as an input file showing which buttons are pressed at which moment. In 2003 a TAS video of Super Mario Bros. 3 spread widely, and viewers felt “cheated” when its maker, who used the name Morimoto, explained that it had not been played by a human. The video had not said what it was.

This is the same distinction that matters for a replay check. If Ziggle’s server replays your moves and every one is legal, it knows the run was possible. It cannot know that a person made it, because a program can send legal moves as well. Our own reasoning, not the article’s: that is why replay is paired with limits on time. In the code, an Arrow Escape time must be at least the larger of 10 seconds and 0.3 seconds an arrow, and a Sudoku time at least the larger of 15 seconds and 0.8 seconds a blank square. These floors are crude, and they are meant to be. They stop an impossible time, not a clever one. Between them, replay and time floors catch the two sorts of fake that matter most here: moves that cannot be played, and times that cannot be human.

What stays off the board

Two more rules keep the boards honest, and they are the ones speedrunning has also had to learn. A daily rescued with a life is never posted, because it is no longer a clean run. And a leaderboard name is optional, and kids mode hides leaderboards completely, so a board does not need to know who you are in order to rank you.

The twist

The community the article describes is as much about sharing as about racing. Sites were used to swap tips as well as runs, and the article says many records are the product of years of collective research. Games Done Quick, the biggest marathon of runs, had raised over $37 million for charity by January 2022. A leaderboard people trust becomes a place people help each other.
