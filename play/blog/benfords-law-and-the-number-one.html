Why so many numbers begin with 1: Benford’s law

Pick a number from a river, a price list or a newspaper and it starts with 1 about 30% of the time. A bowl of doubling numbers shows why.

Open an almanac at random and look at the first digit of each number. If digits were evenly spread, each of 1 to 9 would lead about 11% of the time. They do not. The digit 1 leads about 30.1% of the time, and 9 only 4.6%. This is Benford’s law: the share of numbers starting with digit d is log10(1 + 1/d).

The astronomer and the physicist

The astronomer Simon Newcomb noticed in 1881[1] that the early pages of a book of logarithm tables were far more worn than the later ones. Fifty-seven years later the physicist Frank Benford, who got the law named after him, tested it on 20,229 observations[2] from 20 different kinds of data: river areas, populations, physical constants, street addresses and more. The law fits remarkably well, which is also an example of Stigler’s law: nothing is named after the person who found it.

The twist

Going from 1 to 2 doubles a number, and so does going from 5 to 10. Steady growth by multiplication needs as much time for the first as for the second, yet the first stretch covers only the 1s and the second covers the 5s, 6s, 7s, 8s and 9s together. Numbers that grow by proportion must therefore spend far more of their time with a leading 1. The pattern is not a quirk of data; it is what multiplication looks like through a first-digit window.

What you can do with it

Auditors use it as a first-pass screen: invented figures tend to start with digits too evenly, while real accounts follow Benford. It is a hint, not proof; plenty of honest data (such as telephone numbers or heights) does not follow it at all. On a puzzle site, it is simply a good reminder that “random” does not mean “evenly spread”.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

When the law applies, and when it does not

Benford’s law tends to hold best for data that span several orders of magnitude[2]: river lengths from metres to thousands of kilometres, populations from villages to megacities, prices from pence to millions. It fails when the data are squeezed into a narrow range. The article’s own example: if you count as a “settlement” only villages with a population between 300 and 999, almost every number starts with 3 to 9, and Benford’s law does not apply at all.

The law also makes predictions beyond the first digit. If digits were spread evenly, each would appear about 11.1% of the time; Benford’s law predicts the distribution of second and later digits too, and in 1995 Ted Hill proved[2] a result about mixed distributions, which explains why data pooled from many different sources often obey the law even when no single source does.

The second digit, and how many series obey

Wikipedia’s article says the law also makes predictions[3] for second and later digits. Summing the first-digit formula over every possible leading digit gives the second digit’s share.

The article lists the Fibonacci numbers, the factorials, the powers of 2 and the powers of almost any other number as sequences that provably satisfy the law in the long run. We counted all four, plus three that should not.

The four growth sequences sit within about 0.6 of a percentage point of the law on average. The squares, the primes below a hundred thousand and the whole numbers from 300 to 999 do not. The last is the article’s own example of data squeezed into a narrow range: none of the numbers starts with a 1 or a 2.

Change the units and the law still holds

The article explains that the law is scale invariant: it should not matter whether you measure in metres or feet. We multiplied the same 1,000 powers of 2 by 3.28084, the metres-to-feet factor, and counted again: 30.1% start with 1 and 4.5% with 9, against 30.1% and 4.6% from the law. A set that obeys the law keeps obeying it under any change of unit. The article’s example with real data is the 58 tallest structures: in metres 39.7% of heights start with 1, in feet 25.9%, against 30.1% predicted, a reminder that 58 values are too few for the shares to settle.

Who has used it, and what is disputed

Benford’s 1938 paper collected 20,229 observations, including the addresses of the first 342 people in American Men of Science and 418 death rates. The article lists election data, price digits and scientific fraud detection among applications. Walter Mebane first applied a second-digit test to election results, which the article calls a simple but not foolproof way of finding irregularities. A 2011 study argued that the law is problematic and misleading as an indicator of election fraud, and the article states that scientific consensus has not been reached.

The twist

The law is a probe, and it works only on data that fit its conditions: values spread over several magnitudes, not squeezed into a band like the villages of 300 to 999 people. Know the conditions and you know when a mismatch means something and when it means nothing.
