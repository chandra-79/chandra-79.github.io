Counting queens up to 14: the sequence, its ratios and a formula that is far off

We counted every n-queens solution up to a 14 × 14 board. The numbers climb about fivefold per step, and the formula for large boards says something else for small ones.

The eight queens article counted the solutions up to 12 squares. We carried on, with a plain backtracking search, to 14. It took about twenty seconds on a laptop.

The ratios tell the story

From one size to the next the count is multiplied by 3.8, 2.1, 3.7, 5.3, 5.2 and 5.0. The swings at the start are real: even and odd boards behave differently. But for n of 12 and up the multiplier is steady near five. That is also why the problem is hard: each extra square brings roughly five times as many solutions to find, but also many more dead ends.

A formula that is far off for small boards

For large n the number of solutions is about (0.143 n)n[2], an asymptotic law quoted on the encyclopedia page for the puzzle. Try it at n = 14: (0.143 × 14)14 is about 16,615, while the true count is 365,596, about 22 times higher. The formula describes the long run; at small sizes lower-order terms still dominate, a useful reminder that an asymptotic result is a statement about infinity, not an estimate you can use at 14.

The twist

You might expect that a count so precisely stated would have a formula. It does not: no closed formula is known for the number of n-queens solutions, and the exact count has only been found by computer up to n = 27[2]. The puzzle that fits on a kitchen table is, at the edge, an open research frontier.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Who posed it, and how it was first solved

Wikipedia’s article on the puzzle says[4] the chess composer Max Bezzel published the eight queens puzzle in 1848 and Franz Nauck published the first solutions in 1850, extending it to n queens on an n × n board. Gauss worked on it too. In 1874 S. Günther proposed a method using determinants, which J. W. L. Glaisher refined, and in 1972 Edsger Dijkstra used the puzzle to show off structured programming, with a detailed depth-first backtracking program.

How much of the search can be skipped

That is why an exhaustive search of the 8 × 8 board is instant, and why it stops being instant: the permutation count 20! is about 2.43 × 1018, which the article calls intractable for brute force from n = 20.

Distinct solutions and fundamental ones

Of the 92 solutions to the eight queens puzzle, only 12 are different once rotations and reflections are treated as the same. Eleven of the 12 have eight variants. One, the only symmetric solution, equals its own 180° rotation and has four variants, so 11 × 8 + 1 × 4 = 92. We recomputed the fundamental counts by search for every board from 4 to 12 (1, 2, 1, 6, 12, 46, 92, 341, 1,787) and they agree with the published table.

The ratio climbs to 8 and then stays at 8.000 from n = 20 on, probably because nearly every solution on a large board has no symmetry, so each one comes in eight copies (our reading).

The multiplier does not stay near five

The post says that from 12 upwards the multiplier is steady near five. That holds up to 14 but not beyond: it rises from 5.0 at n = 14 to 6.2 at 15, 7.9 at 20 and 10.5 at 27. A count that grows like nn has a multiplier that grows with n, which is what we see. The straight line on the log chart the post describes never arrives.

The large-n law, tested at every size

In 2021 Michael Simkin proved that the count is about (0.143 n)n. More precisely, it is (n e−α)n where the constant α lies between 1.939 and 1.945, which makes e−α about 0.143.

The last column shows why the formula is far out at small sizes: the implied base is 0.220 at n = 8, 0.178 at 14, 0.163 at 27, and falling slowly towards 0.143. Even at the edge of what has been counted the formula is about 34 times too low.

One solution without any search

Solutions exist for every board except 2 × 2 and 3 × 3. For any n of 4 or more, the article gives a recipe that needs no search. Write the even numbers up to n, then the odd numbers; if n leaves remainder 2 on division by 6, swap 1 and 3 in the odd list and move 5 to the end; if it leaves remainder 3, move 2 to the end of the evens and 1 and 3 to the end of the odds. Put the queen in each column at the listed row. For n = 8 it gives 2, 4, 6, 8, 3, 1, 7, 5. We ran the recipe for every n from 4 to 200 and checked every pair of queens: all 197 placements are valid (computed).

Cousins of the puzzle

  • On a donut-shaped (toroidal) board, Pólya showed a solution exists only when n is not divisible by 2 or 3. The count there grows like (n e−3)n.
  • The fewest queens that attack every square of the 8 × 8 board is 5 (the domination number).
  • If some queens are already placed, deciding whether the rest can be completed is NP-complete. Any placement of up to n/60 queens can be completed.
  • On an 8 × 8 board you can place 32 knights, 14 bishops, 16 kings or 8 rooks so that none attacks another.
The twist

The 27 × 27 board is the highest that has been completely counted: 234,907,967,154,122,528 solutions, about 2.35 × 1017. A plain search reached n = 16 in about ten seconds on this machine, and the published counts go eleven sizes further, where each size has more than six times as many solutions as the one before (computed from the table).
