Mixed practice looked worse on day one and won by day thirty

In a three-month maths experiment, shuffling the problems helped a little the next day and much more a month later.

Science of practice · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 21 September 2026

Most homework is blocked: a dozen problems that all use the method from today’s lesson, so you know the method before you read the question. In 2015 Doug Rohrer and colleagues tested the alternative, interleaving, in real classrooms: 126 seventh-grade students[1] got the same practice problems over three months, arranged either in blocks or mixed together.

The same problems, a different order

The practice ended with a review session, then an unannounced test one or thirty days later[1]. Mixed practice beat blocked practice on both tests, and the gap between them grew with the wait.

Why a delay should help

Blocked practice lets you ride the method of the moment. Mixed practice forces you to work out which method each problem needs, which is exactly what an exam asks. One explanation for the growing gap is that choosing a method leaves a longer-lasting trace than repeating one. Mixed practice also feels harder while you do it, which can make it feel like the worse option.

A larger gap in an earlier study

In a related experiment by the same lab, students who practised mixed problems scored 72%[2] on a later test against 38% for blocked practice (d = 1.05). Different classes and a different test, so we do not add the studies together; the direction is the same every time.

In Ziggle

When you mix categories in a word game or move between Sudoku variants, you stop knowing which trick is coming. It is slightly more work and, on this evidence, more worth doing. Our earlier post on shuffling has the 2007 study that started this line of work.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the students actually did

The experiment ran at a large public middle school in Tampa[3], Florida, in the 2013–14 school year, with three maths teachers and nine of their seventh-grade classes. Parents and students agreed for 150 of the 164 students, and 126 of them (84%) were present on both the review day and the test day, so only their data count. Nearly all were 12 years old, 61 were girls, and 38% got free or reduced-price lunch.

Every student saw the same two kinds of problem, graphing a line from its equation and finding the slope between two points, twelve of each, spread over ten practice sheets of twelve problems. The other problems on the sheets came from unrelated topics. A tutorial on graphing came before the first sheet and one on slope before the second, and each of those sheets carried the first four problems of its kind. The remaining eight were either added to that same sheet, so that all twelve came together (blocked), or scattered across later sheets (interleaved). The ten sheets were handed out between day 1 and about day 88.

Every student was their own control

Practice schedule was counterbalanced within students. One group of classes had interleaved graph problems and blocked slope problems, and the other group had the reverse, so each child learned one skill each way. The delay before the test was assigned at random within each class, 63 students to a 1-day test and 63 to a 30-day test. A review sheet on day 93 gave one graph problem and one slope problem, so the blocked skills also got a last, spaced look. Then came an unannounced six-problem test (three of each kind), marked by two raters who did not know the condition, with 18 minutes allowed and a basic calculator.

Our arithmetic: after a month the interleaved skills lost 6 points (80 to 74), while the blocked skills lost 22 (64 to 42). The authors put it as near immunity against forgetting, since a 30-fold longer delay cost interleaved practice less than a tenth of its score. Comparing across the two groups, which is looser, the interleaved skills a month on (74%) beat the blocked skills a single day on (64%).

The practice sheets told the opposite story

On the practice sheets themselves, blocked problems were answered 81% correct and interleaved problems 84%, with 82% for the last eight of the interleaved ones. On the review sheet interleaved skills scored 94% and blocked skills 89%. Those scores were not a true measure of learning, because students corrected their own work as the teachers went through the answers. The authors used them to show that students engaged equally with both versions. The point holds in a second way: during practice the two conditions look almost the same, and the difference only appears on a test you did not see coming.

Why blocked practice flatters you

The paper’s own example: a girl hikes 8 km east and then 15 km north. How far is she from where she started? The answer, 17 km, needs the Pythagorean theorem, but nothing in the question says so. In a block of Pythagoras problems you know that before you read. In the paper’s other example, “Chloe baked 24 cookies. Her father ate 10. How many remain?”, a student in a block of subtraction questions can answer without reading anything but the two numbers. Blocked practice, the authors say, can let students solve word problems without reading the words. Interleaving makes them choose the strategy as well as run it, which is the half of the job a test asks for.

What the authors say the study cannot show

  • Interleaving also spaces practice, so some of the benefit may be spacing. The review session gave blocked practice some spacing, which may have shrunk the gap.
  • The size of the gap at one day against 30 days did not differ reliably (the interaction had p = .09), so “it grew with the wait” is a likely reading and not a proven one.
  • The test problems looked like the practice problems, so the results may be smaller if the test needs bigger leaps, and the review held only one problem of each kind.
  • Three teachers and two skills, so it is unknown whether it holds for wider material.
  • Classroom effects (d = 0.42 and 0.79) were smaller than laboratory ones the authors list: 1.34 for college students finding solid volumes a week later, and 1.21 for fourth-graders on prisms a day later.
  • Students were not surveyed. The three teachers, anonymously, each said they would use it again and recommend it to others.

A small block still has a place

The authors do not argue for abolishing blocks. A small block of problems on the day a skill is taught may be best, because it lets students focus on executing the steps. After a few of the same kind in a row, they write, extra effort brings sharply diminishing returns. Their informal check of several middle-school textbook series found that review problems made up less than 15% of the practice problems, and they note the Saxon series is unusual in being mostly interleaved. They also point out that this is not evidence about Saxon textbooks in general, which differ in other ways.

The twist

The oldest studies of this idea were not about maths. The authors cite one in which college baseball players practised hitting three kinds of pitch, fastball, curveball and change-up, either one kind at a time or mixed. The mixed group hit better on a final test where the pitch could be any of the three, which is how a game works, and a maths test is the same kind of surprise.
