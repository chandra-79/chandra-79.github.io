Birthdays and goals: why a new age makes a good starting line

People visit the gym more, sign more goal pledges and run more first marathons around their birthdays. Here is what the studies found, and how to use your own.

Habits · The Ziggle team · 7-minute read · 28 November 2026

A birthday is an odd sort of day. Nothing about you changes between the evening before and the morning after, and yet one number, the one you give when somebody asks your age, has ticked over. There is cake. Somebody may sing. And at some point in the day, usually a quiet one, most of us do a little private sum: where was I a year ago, and where would I like to be at the next one?

That small sum turns out to be useful. Researchers who study how people begin things keep finding the same pattern: we are more likely to begin just after a date that feels like the first page of something. A birthday is the one such date that belongs only to you.

A date that turns the page

In 2014 Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis gave the pattern its name: the fresh start effect. They looked at three very different records of people trying to do something good for themselves: searches for the word “diet”, visits to a university gym, and pledges on a website where people commit to a goal in writing.

All three rose after what the authors call temporal landmarks, the dates that divide time into chapters. Their explanation is that a landmark opens a new period in the mind and pushes earlier slips into the past, where they belong to a slightly different person. It also lifts your eyes from the daily to-do list to “take a big-picture view”[1] of your life.

Most landmarks are shared: Mondays, the first of the month, the first of January. The birthday is the personal one, and it showed up too. Among the 11,912 students in the gym records, the chance of a visit went up by 7.5% after a birthday[1]. On the pledge website, which held 66,062 goal contracts, sign-ups rose by 2.6% after a birthday[1].

Look at the table honestly and the birthday is the smallest landmark in it. A Monday beats it easily. That is no reason to wave it away: a birthday comes once a year, the weeks come fifty-two times, and a nudge of a few per cent across thousands of people is still thousands of gym visits that would not otherwise have happened. It simply means a birthday is a starting line, not a magic spell.

The birthdays that end in nine

Some birthdays feel bigger than others. Adam Alter and Hal Hershfield wondered whether the year before a round number, at 29, 39, 49 and so on, sets people thinking. They called the people at those ages “9-enders” and went looking for them in six studies.

The clearest sighting was at the marathon. Of 500 people running their first one, 74 had an age ending in nine. If ages were spread evenly there would be 50, so 9-enders were over-represented by 48%[2]. In a second sample of 100 runners who had raced at several ages, people ran 2.30% faster[2] at 29 or 39 than in the two years on either side.

Two cautions belong here, because this is the part of the story most often oversold. First, where the authors asked people directly, in a world survey of 42,063 adults, 9-enders said they questioned the meaning of life only a shade more often than everybody else (3.28 against 3.25 on a four-point scale)[2], and the authors themselves call the effects small. Second, other researchers tested the idea on records that follow the same people year after year and reported no sign of end-of-decade upheavals[3]; the original authors replied that their evidence still stands. So: the marathon numbers are real counts, and the grand theory behind them is still being argued over. A big birthday may set you thinking. It does not have to.

Borrowing a birthday on purpose

If landmarks help people begin, can you hand someone a landmark? John Beshears and three colleagues tried it with something far duller than a marathon: pension saving. They posted letters to 6,082 university employees[4] inviting them to start saving more, either now or from a later date. For some, that later date was described plainly, as a number of months away. For others the very same wait was tied to a landmark: after their next birthday, or after the first day of spring.

The dates dressed as fresh starts did better: people offered them were more likely to say yes, and they had put more into their plans over the eight months that followed. One landmark fell flat, though, and it is the famous one. New Year’s Day drew no strong response[5], perhaps, one write-up suggests, because it has become a cliché for fresh starts. A birthday has no such baggage. Nobody else is making resolutions on yours.

What kind of goal to make

A starting line still needs a race. The longest-running research on goals is Edwin Locke and Gary Latham’s, summed up in 2002 after thirty-five years of studies. Their central finding fits in one sentence: “specific, difficult goals consistently led to higher performance than urging people to do their best.”[6] Across the pooled studies the size of that advantage ranged from 0.42 to 0.80[6] on the usual scale, where 0.5 counts as medium and 0.8 as large.

“Do your best” fails, they argue, because it has no finishing post: everyone decides privately what their best is, and it tends to be whatever happened. A clear goal does four jobs instead. It points your attention at what matters, it raises effort, it keeps you going for longer, and it sends you hunting for better ways to do the thing.

So a birthday wish such as “read more” is a do-your-best goal. “Twelve books before my next birthday” is the other kind. Then cut it down until today’s share is almost laughably small, because the year is long and the arithmetic is on your side.

A birthday plan in five small steps

  1. Pick one. A birthday invites a list. One goal you keep is worth more than six you remember in March.
  2. Make it countable. Give it a number and a date. Your next birthday is a deadline that comes ready-made.
  3. Shrink today’s share. Five minutes, one page, one puzzle. Small enough that a bad day cannot argue with it.
  4. Hang it on something you already do. The first coffee, the train, the kettle. We wrote about why that works in Five minutes a day.
  5. Keep the smaller landmarks in your pocket. If the birthday plan wobbles, the table above says a plain Monday is a stronger restart than the birthday was. There are fifty-two of them before the next cake.

Where Ziggle fits

We build a daily puzzle habit, so we have borrowed all of this shamelessly. One daily is a whole day’s share; all eight is a treat, not a duty. The twist

One birthday in the gym study ran backwards. Students who had just turned 21, the age at which Americans may first buy alcohol, went to the gym less afterwards. The authors offer that as a possible reason and call their own explanation “entirely speculative”[1]. Still, it is a useful reminder that a landmark only opens a door. What walks through depends on what the day means to you.
