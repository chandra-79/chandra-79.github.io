A shoebox of flashcards that changed studying

Sebastian Leitner’s 1972 card-box method is the simple idea behind the Review tab in Ziggle’s trainers.

Training design · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 7 August 2026

In this article 7 sections

In 1972 the German science journalist Sebastian Leitner published a book on learning that described a simple system using a box with several compartments.

New flashcards start in the first compartment. Answer a card correctly and it moves up a compartment; get it wrong and it goes back to the first. You review the first compartment often and the later ones less and less. Cards you know drift towards the back and stop wasting your time; cards you don’t know keep coming round.

Why it works

It combines two well-studied ideas. Retrieval practice: actually trying to recall an answer helps more than rereading it. And spacing: reviewing something just as you are about to forget it is more efficient than cramming. The forgetting curve, first charted by Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s, is what the spacing pushes back against.

In Ziggle

The emoji and flags trainer and the memory palace keep a Review list built on the same principle. Each card you answer is scheduled to come back after a day, then three days, a week, three weeks and two months. A miss sends it back to the start of the climb. The tab shows how many cards are due, and when nothing is due it says so.

No shoebox needed, though we admire the original.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the box really looked like

Leitner’s book was called So lernt man lernen (“How to learn to learn”). In his original method the schedule was not kept by a calendar at all. It was kept by the box. The compartments were 1, 2, 5, 8 and 14 centimetres wide[1], and you reviewed a compartment only when it was full. The later compartments were wider, so they took longer to fill, and a card that kept being answered correctly sat longer before it was due again. The box was the timer.

The simplest version used by most people today has three boxes. Box 1 holds the cards you often get wrong, Box 2 the ones you rarely miss and Box 3 the ones you know very well. You might look at Box 1 every day, Box 2 every third day and Box 3 every fifth. A right answer promotes a card one box; a mistake in Box 2 or Box 3 demotes it to Box 1, which makes you study it more often. The aim, the article says, is that the hard cards stay in the first few boxes and the time you need goes down.

There are variations. In one the cards of a wrong answer drop back by only one box instead of all the way to the start. In another, with twelve boxes named by numbers such as 0-2-5-9, each box is reviewed in the learning sessions whose number appears in its name, so you need no calendar at all, and the effect is identical to a five-box system.

Who thought of it first?

Spacing is older than the box. Hermann Ebbinghaus charted the forgetting curve in the 1880s and suggested that reviewing at several intervals could flatten it. The idea of increasing gaps was proposed in print by C. A. Mace in his 1932 book Psychology of Study, which advised revising at gradually increasing intervals, “roughly intervals of one day, two days, four days, eight days, and so on”. In 1939 H. F. Spitzer tested a form of it on more than 3,600 sixth-grade pupils in Iowa[2] learning science facts, and showed that it worked. The article says this early work went unnoticed, and the field stayed quiet until the late 1960s, when Melton and then Landauer and Bjork studied the timing of repetition. The Pimsleur language courses were an early practical use of the idea, and Leitner’s box followed in the early 1970s. (The two Wikipedia articles read for this post give 1972 and 1973; we use the first.)

Ziggle’s ladder, day by day

The Review list in the trainers uses six boxes with waits of 0, 1, 3, 7, 21, 60 days. If you answer a card right every time it comes up, this is when you meet it (computed from the schedule in the code):

  • 92days from first sight to the sixth sitting
  • 6sittings to get there
  • day 11when a card counts as “known” (fourth right answer)

That is six sittings across 92 days, against 93 if you looked at the card every day. A card counts towards the “known” figure once it reaches the fifth box, and the earliest that can happen is the fourth right answer. A wrong answer sends it back to the second box, so it is due again tomorrow, and the climb restarts.

What the evidence says, and does not say

The article says spaced repetition is widely accepted as a strong learning strategy and has been tested well beyond vocabulary. In one study a simple maths principle was learned better with spaced practice than with massed practice, and it also helped when the learner was shown the right answer after a wrong one. A pilot study found it useful for training surgeons on a simulator.

It is less sure about the shape of the schedule. Several studies found that expanding intervals do as well as or better than equal ones, while meta-analyses tend to conclude that the two give similar results. The article adds that the extra benefit sometimes credited to expanding gaps may come from other things, such as when the first retrieval happens, how many repetitions there are, or the total spacing, and that the best schedule may depend on the learner[2]. In plain terms, spacing is well supported and Ziggle’s particular ladder is a reasonable choice, not a proven best.

The twist

The software that grew out of the box now schedules each card by itself. The SuperMemo family of algorithms runs from SM-0, a paper-and-pencil prototype, to SM-18, and the article names newer schedulers too, such as the free FSRS in Anki. Without a computer, the article says, scheduling is time-intensive and limits you to simple methods such as the Leitner system. The shoebox lasted because it needs no software.
