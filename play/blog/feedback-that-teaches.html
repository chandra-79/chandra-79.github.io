Feedback that teaches

A 2007 review found feedback works best when it answers three questions. Every Ziggle end-of-level popup tries to.

Training design · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 1 October 2026

In 2007 John Hattie and Helen Timperley published “The Power of Feedback”, a widely cited review of research on how feedback affects learning. Their conclusion was that feedback can help a great deal, but not all feedback does, and some kinds can even hurt.

They suggested that useful feedback answers three questions:

  • Where am I going? What is the goal?
  • How am I going? How close did I get, and what went well or wrong?
  • Where to next? What should I do now?

They also found that feedback about the task (“this word had a doubled letter”) tends to help more than feedback about the person (“you’re so clever”).

In Ziggle

Every level of every game ends with the same popup, built around those three questions:

  • What happened: the numbers that matter for that game, such as time, guesses, hearts, hints or sparks.
  • What went wrong, on a loss: the answer, or how many words were still possible, or one way to the target.
  • A clear way on: the next level, another go, or a life to keep going.

We do add a cheerful word when you win. But the useful part is the bit about the puzzle, not the bit about you.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the review is, and how big the feedback effect is

The review opens with a benchmark.[2] Hattie and Timperley note that the average effect of schooling is 0.40 on the scale of effect sizes, and that the 12 meta-analyses of classroom feedback they collected, covering 196 studies and 6,972 effects, average 0.79. By our arithmetic that is almost exactly double (0.79 ÷ 0.40 = 1.98), and the authors say it puts feedback in the top 5 to 10 of the influences on achievement in Hattie’s 1999 synthesis of more than 500 meta-analyses.

The average hides a very wide spread

The authors write that the highest effects came when students received information feedback about a task and how to do it better, and the lower ones when the feedback was praise, reward or punishment. They add that it is doubtful whether rewards should be thought of as feedback at all, because tangible rewards carry so little information about the task.

The study that found a third of feedback made things worse

Kluger and DeNisi’s 1996 meta-analysis, the largest in the review, used 131 studies with a control group and at least 10 participants, giving 470 effects from 12,652 participants. The average was 0.38, and 32% of the effects were negative. The review lists what moved that number.

Note the praise row: praise about the task was worth 0.09, against 0.34 when it was absent. Telling students the answer was right was worth 0.43 and telling them it was wrong 0.25 (Table 3, 114 and 197 effects), so the review concludes feedback works better when it says what is correct and builds on progress.

The three questions and the four levels

The review frames feedback as answering three questions: where am I going (the authors call this feed up), how am I going (feed back) and where to next (feed forward). It also says feedback works at four levels:

  • Task: whether work is right or wrong, including “you need to include more about the Treaty of Versailles”.
  • Process: how the work was done, such as “this page may make more sense if you use the strategies we talked about earlier”.
  • Self-regulation: self-evaluation and the confidence to engage further.
  • Self: comments about the person, unrelated to the task.

Feedback aimed at moving students from task to process and from process to regulation is the most effective. It also warns that teachers commonly mix corrective feedback with the self level, which dilutes it, with the example of “Good boy, that is correct”. Feedback at the task level often does not generalise to other tasks: in one study improvement was specific to the questions that got feedback.

Timing: sooner is not always better

The review says the timing question has been studied mostly without recognising the levels. In a meta-analysis of 53 studies, delay helped at the task level (0.36) and immediate feedback helped at the process level (0.28). Positive against negative

The authors argue the level matters more than whether the feedback is positive or negative. At the self level they say no praise is more effective than praise if it comes with task feedback, and that negative feedback can be more potent than positive there. They also cite a meta-analysis in which tangible rewards undermined intrinsic motivation, especially for interesting tasks (−0.68) as against uninteresting ones (0.18).

The twist

The review counts a pat on the back as one of the weakest kinds of feedback, and the strongest as the plainest: cues and information about the task. Our reading, not a claim made in the review: a popup that says what happened in the puzzle is closer to this evidence than one that only says “brilliant”.
