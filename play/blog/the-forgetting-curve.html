The forgetting curve is real, and it’s beatable

In the 1880s a German psychologist memorised nonsense for five years so you wouldn’t have to. Here’s what he found.

Between 1879 and 1885, Hermann Ebbinghaus did something nobody had tried. He used himself as the only subject in a long series of memory experiments, and he used material designed to be impossible to like.

He made up nonsense syllables, things like WID and ZOF, so that nothing he learned would hook onto words he already knew. He memorised lists of them, waited, and tested himself again and again. In 1885 he published the results in a book called Über das Gedächtnis, “On Memory”. It is one of the founding documents of experimental psychology, and it was produced by a man voluntarily reciting ZOF for half a decade.

What he measured

Ebbinghaus didn’t just ask “do I remember it?”. He measured savings: how much less time it took to relearn a list than it had taken the first time. If you relearned a list instantly, you’d kept 100%. If it took as long as the first time, you’d kept nothing.

Plot savings against time and you get the famous forgetting curve. It drops steeply at first and then flattens out. Most of what you lose, you lose early. What survives the first stretch tends to hang on.

Did it hold up?

Here’s the delightful part. In 2015, the researchers Jaap Murre and Joeri Dros repeated the experiment, also with a single subject, and got results very similar to the original. A finding from 1885, run by one stubborn person on himself, survived 130 years and a modern replication[1]. Most diet advice doesn’t survive a fortnight.

How to bend the curve

The forgetting curve isn’t a sentence; it’s a schedule. Every time you successfully recall something, the curve that follows is flatter. So the trick is when you review, not how hard.

  • Review just before you’d forget. In a study of more than 1,350 people[2], the best gap before a review was about 20–40% of the time until the test when that was a week away, and fell to about 5–10% when it was a year away. Need it in a week? Look again after a day or two. Need it in a year? Look again after three to five weeks.
  • Space it out. Several short reviews spread over days beat one long session. Cramming fills the bucket just in time for it to drain.
  • Make each review a test. Don’t reread; try to recall first, then check. (More on that soon.)

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The replication, in detail

Murre and Dros’s replication is a small masterpiece of stubbornness. One subject spent about 70 hours[1] learning lists of nonsense syllables and relearning them after 20 minutes, 1 hour, 9 hours, 1 day, 2 days and 31 days, between 1 December 2011 and 13 February 2012. In all, 69 lists[1] were learned and relearned, about ten per interval (nine for the 9-hour gap). As in 1885 the measure was savings, the share of learning time you save by relearning, and the raw data are free to download from the Open Science Framework.

Two curves for one experiment

Ebbinghaus did not settle on one formula. The authors note that he fitted a power function in 1880 and a logarithmic function in 1885, as many theorists have since. The two look alike over short times and part company over long ones, which is why “what shape is forgetting?” is still a live question in memory research and not a closed chapter.

Ebbinghaus’s own formula, evaluated

Wikipedia’s article gives an equation[4] from the 1885 book to approximate the curve: savings, as a percentage, equal 100k ÷ ((log t)c + k), where t is the time in minutes counted from one minute before the end of learning and the constants are c = 1.25 and k = 1.84. Savings is the share of learning time saved on the second attempt. We evaluated it at the six gaps used in the 2015 replication.

The formula agrees with the idea in the post: most of the loss is early. Between 20 minutes and one day it gives up about 26.1 points, and between one day and 31 days only about 9.2. That is a lot gentler than the widely drawn graph in which retained information halves each day; the article’s own caption for that picture calls it a representation, and says a typical graph purports to show memory halving over days or weeks.

How the replication was run

Murre and Dros’s subject learned each list until it could be recited correctly once, and the learning time in seconds was recorded. At relearning he went through the list again to the same criterion, and savings were computed from the two times. The paper tested whether the average number of repetitions needed for first learning differed between the six intervals, to rule out an accidental advantage for any interval, and found it did not. The subject’s recall attempts were recorded, which allowed an analysis of serial position, that is, which items in a list were forgotten first.

The paper compares the savings with Ebbinghaus’s own and with those of Mack and Seitz, and overlays four curves, each normalised to start at 1.0 at 20 minutes. It calls the resemblance remarkable. The greatest deviation is the replication’s 31-day point, which is much lower than the other three. The authors suggest the subject may simply have more long-term forgetting, or that the 31-day lists were all learned early, when learning times were shorter because of greater enthusiasm or less interference. The raw data are on the Open Science Framework.

What Ebbinghaus left out

The article notes that Ebbinghaus studied the rate of forgetting but not how spaced repetition raises retrievability. That is the half the post’s “bend the curve” section borrows from later work.

The bump nobody expects

The abstract’s verdict is not “a perfectly smooth curve”. The authors conclude that the forgetting curve “is not completely smooth but most probably shows a jump upwards starting at the 24 hour data point[1]”: you keep a little more after a day than a smooth line would predict. The replication’s own 24-hour point showed it too, and the authors checked that it was a valid measurement. Others have linked such a bump to sleep: the 1920s study by Jenkins and Dallenbach was prompted by exactly this oddity in earlier data, and in another early data set the savings were 0.474 after eight hours but 0.689 after a day, then 0.609 after two.

What this does and does not tell you

One person, nonsense syllables and a lab: this is a statement about the shape of forgetting under tidy conditions, not a promise about how much you will remember of a lecture. Nonsense syllables were chosen precisely because they are unlike the things you care about. What the replication buys is confidence that the basic effect Ebbinghaus found by reciting nonsense to himself is real.
