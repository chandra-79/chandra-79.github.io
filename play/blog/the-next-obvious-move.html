Sudoku, arrows and the joy of the next obvious move

A retired architect, a Japanese name change, and 6.67 sextillion grids. Why logic puzzles feel so good to finish.

The name sounds Japanese, the craze went global in the 2000s, and the inventor was a 74-year-old retired architect from Indiana. Sudoku has a complicated passport.

Where Sudoku came from

The modern puzzle was most likely designed by Howard Garns, from Connersville, Indiana. Dell Magazines first printed it in 1979 under the wonderfully plain name Number Place. Garns didn’t get a byline.

In April 1984, the Japanese publisher Nikoli brought it to Japan as Sūji wa dokushin ni kagiru, roughly “the digits must be single”. That was a mouthful even for puzzle people, so it was shortened to Sudoku. Two decades later it was in newspapers everywhere.

Some numbers to impress people at parties

  • There are 6,670,903,752,021,072,936,960[2] possible completed 9×9 grids. That’s about 6.67 sextillion. If you solved one a second, you would not finish.
  • Strip out the ones that are just rotations, reflections or relabellings of each other, and you’re still left with 5,472,730,538[3] truly different grids.
  • The fewest clues a proper Sudoku can have is 17. Researchers proved there’s no 16-clue Sudoku[1] with a single solution, in a paper titled, with admirable directness, “There is no 16-Clue Sudoku”.

Why logic puzzles feel so good

Good logic puzzles don’t ask you to be a genius. They ask you to find the next obvious move, again and again, until there aren’t any left. Each one is small. Each one is certain. And each one makes the next a little easier to see.

That chain of small certainties is close to what the psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called flow: a clear goal, instant feedback, and a challenge that matches your skill. Sudoku gives you instant feedback in the form of a number that fits. Arrow escape gives it to you as an arrow sliding off the board, or very much not sliding off the board.

Arrow escape is about order, not speed

Every arrow escapes eventually; the puzzle is only ever about when. Fire the wrong one first and it hits another arrow and costs you a heart. The trick strong players use is to work backwards: find the arrows nobody else is blocking, clear those, and watch new free arrows appear. It’s the same next-obvious-move idea, with more slapstick.

What you get better at (honestly)

You get better at Sudoku and at arrow boards. You start spotting patterns in a glance that used to take a minute. That’s a real skill, and it’s yours. We won’t pretend it turns into a better memory for phone numbers. What it does reliably is give you ten quiet minutes and the small, clean pleasure of a thing that is finished and correct. Which, on most days, is more than email offers.

Three tips

  1. Look for the lonely number. Scan one row, column or box for the single place a digit can still go.
  2. Use notes, then trust them. Pencil-mark the candidates once, and you stop doing the same arithmetic over and over.
  3. On arrow boards, find the free arrows first. Any arrow with an empty runway is safe. Clear those and the board opens up.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Before Number Place

Wikipedia’s article on Sudoku says[4] number puzzles appeared in French newspapers in the late nineteenth century, when setters began removing numbers from magic squares. Le Siècle printed a partly completed 9 × 9 magic square on 19 November 1892, not yet a sudoku because it needed arithmetic. On 6 July 1895 its rival La France refined the idea into the “carré magique diabolique”, in which every row, column and broken diagonal held the digits 1 to 9. The boxes were not marked, though each 3 × 3 block did contain 1 to 9. Such puzzles ran in French papers for about a decade and disappeared about the time of the First World War.

Who made the modern puzzle

  1. 1979Dell Magazines prints Number Place. The designer is most likely Howard Garns, then 74 (born 2 March 1905), a retired architect and freelance puzzle constructor from Connersville, Indiana (age computed).
  2. April 1984Maki Kaji of Nikoli runs it in Monthly Nikolist as 数字は独身に限る, “the digits must be single”, where dokushin also means an unmarried person.
  3. 1989Garns dies, having never seen the craze.
  4. 1997Wayne Gould, a retired Hong Kong judge, sees a puzzle book in a Tokyo bookshop and spends six years writing a program to make and rate puzzles.
  5. September 2004The Conway Daily Sun of New Hampshire becomes the first newspaper outside Japan to print one.
  6. 12 November 2004The Times of London launches it as “Su Doku”.
  7. 2005Will Shortz notices Garns’s name in the contributor lists of the Dell issues that had Number Place and nowhere else.

The article says Kaji made the short name by keeping the first character of each word, and that Nikoli claims trademark rights to “sudoku” in Japan. The article’s introduction dates the shortening to 1986, while its history section gives April 1984 for the first Nikoli publication and no date for the short name. Wikipedia adds one detail from The Times: its first letter about the puzzle, on 13 November, was from a reader who said it had made him miss his stop on the tube.

The 6.67 sextillion, taken apart

Wikipedia’s article on the mathematics lists the transformations that keep a grid valid: relabelling the nine digits, rearranging rows within a band, rearranging the three bands, the same for columns, and reflecting along a diagonal.

Divide the 6,670,903,752,021,072,936,960 grids by that group and you get about 5,472,447,994 (computed). The published count of essentially different grids, found by Russell and Jarvis with Burnside’s lemma, is 5,472,730,538. The two differ by 282,544, or 0.005% (computed). Our reading: the plain division slightly undercounts because some grids have symmetries of their own, and Wikipedia says the 26 kinds of symmetry appear in only about 0.005% of grids, the same size as the gap.

The smaller boards

How the 16-clue proof was done

The paper says the search was exhaustive and proved that no proper puzzle has 16 clues. Their method was to go through the catalogue of essentially different grids and, for each, look for 16-clue puzzles contained in it using hitting-set enumeration, a problem that is one of Karp’s 21 classic NP-complete problems. The year-long search ran at the Irish Centre for High-End Computing in Dublin. An earlier checker, from 2006, was estimated to need more than 300,000 processor-years; the improved algorithm took about 800, which is 375 times less (computed).

The paper adds some background. People had by then collected close to 50,000 different 17-clue puzzles, 49,151 on Gordon Royle’s list, with at most 29 of them hiding in any one grid. Nikoli’s own rule is that none of its puzzles has more than 32 clues. Wikipedia’s mathematics article adds that a proper puzzle must give at least eight of the nine digits, since two missing digits could be swapped throughout a solution to give a second one.

A puzzle you can’t solve quickly in general

Wikipedia’s article also says that deciding whether a sudoku has a solution, in the general n2 × n2 version, is NP-complete. A solver can also treat it as an exact-cover problem: one set for every placement of a digit in a cell, 9 × 9 × 9 = 729 of them (computed), each covering four conditions, which makes 324 conditions in all, 81 for each of four kinds: the cell itself, and the digit in its row, its column and its box.

The twist

Howard Garns never saw his creation take off, and his name was found by noticing which issues carried it. Sixteen years after his death, in 2005, Will Shortz saw that Garns was listed as a contributor in exactly the Dell issues that had Number Place. A puzzle credited to nobody turned out to have an inventor all along.
