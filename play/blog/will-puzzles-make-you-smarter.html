Will puzzles make you smarter?

Short answer: no. The longer answer is better news, and it fits in a coffee break.

Let’s get the awkward part out of the way first.

In January 2016, the US Federal Trade Commission ordered Lumos Labs, the company behind the brain-training app Lumosity, to pay $2 million. Its ads had promised the games would help people perform better at work and at school, and would even hold off age-related decline. The FTC’s view was that the science behind those promises didn’t exist. The full judgment was $50 million, suspended only because the company couldn’t afford it.

Later that year, a team of psychologists led by Daniel Simons did something wonderfully tedious. They read the 132 papers[2] that brain-training companies kept pointing to as proof, and published their verdict in Psychological Science in the Public Interest. People get better at the tasks they practise, they wrote. There is “little evidence for broad transfer” to other tasks, and even less to everyday life.

So no. Sudoku will make you better at Sudoku. It will not make you better at your taxes, at remembering the names of your cousin’s children, or at parallel parking. We checked.

We think that’s fine. Honestly, we think that’s the whole point.

Getting better at a thing is the good part

What the research says loud and clear is that practice works on the thing you practise. In 2014, Brooke Macnamara and her colleagues pooled 88 studies[1] covering more than 11,000 people to ask how much practice explains skill. In games, deliberate practice explained 26% of the difference between players. In music it was 21%, in sports 18%. In education it fell to 4%, and in professions to under 1%.

Look at that list again. Games are where effort shows up most clearly in the results. You can watch it happen in a few weeks: your Letter Lock average slides from five guesses to four, your arrow times drop, your typing climbs from 30 words a minute to 45. Most adults rarely get to see themselves improve that plainly. School had report cards. Work has… annual reviews. A daily puzzle quietly hands you a scoreboard you actually want to look at.

Why it feels so good

In 1975, the psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi gave a name to that state where you look up and twenty minutes have gone: flow. He found it turns up when three things line up. You have a clear goal. You get instant feedback. And the challenge roughly matches your skill, so it’s hard enough to hold you but not so hard you give up.

A clear goal, instant feedback and a fair challenge is also a decent description of a good puzzle. The goal is one word. The feedback is a tile turning green. The challenge is tuned so a Monday doesn’t crush you (Tuesday’s crossword may have other plans).

So what is Ziggle for?

  • A small daily habit that’s yours. Five minutes, the same time each day, a streak that forgives a missed day. More on why that works in a later post.
  • Getting visibly better at specific skills you can be proud of: words, logic, typing, and soon cubes and the memory palace technique. Each one comes with numbers that show your progress.
  • A win you can hold. Not a dopamine slot machine. Eight puzzles, then you’re done, and that’s on purpose.

The fine print we actually mean

We won’t tell you Ziggle will make you sharper, younger or more employable. If a game ever promises you that, ask to see the study, and then ask who paid for it. Here’s what we will promise: the puzzles are fair, nobody is tracking you, your streak survives a sick day, and you will get better at them. That last one is the part the research agrees on.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the 132 papers were

The review by Simons and colleagues began with a list of 132 papers[3] put together by the signatories of an open letter defending brain training. They added every published article cited as evidence on the websites of the leading companies. Of more than 200 companies in the industry, 30 had substantial market or research traction, 6 of those sold something outside the review’s scope, and of the other 24, the websites of half cited no peer-reviewed evidence at all.

The designs matter because of what each can show. A study with no baseline group cannot separate training from simply taking a test twice. A passive control group, which does nothing, cannot separate the training from the attention and expectation of being in a study. An active control, one that does a different task for the same time, can. Only 49 of the 132 papers were randomised trials with an active control. The authors point to drug testing for a standard: of the “promising” drugs that survive the first two phases of testing, only 25% to 30% show enough benefit against a placebo in Phase III to go on the market.

The big trial

The largest study in the field, ACTIVE, tested 2,832 adults aged 65 or older in six sites, starting in 1998, with three different 10-hour training courses and a control group that did nothing. Its measures included real-world tasks such as preparing food, driving, taking medication and managing money. The review recalls that it gave minimal evidence of transfer beyond the trained abilities, and that the speed training, the one closest to the games on sale, showed little evidence of benefiting other cognitive tasks. Fifteen of the 132 papers on the list come from it, so one trial is cited fifteen times.

The review’s verdict, in the abstract, has three tiers. Extensive evidence that training improves the trained tasks, less that it improves closely related tasks, and little that it improves distantly related tasks or everyday cognition. It adds that many of the published studies had major shortcomings in design or analysis.

Where the marketing came in

The review dates the turn to mass marketing to Nintendo’s Brain Age in 2005, which it calls the first product marketed to the public on a massive scale. Wikipedia says Lumos Labs was founded in 2005[4], Lumosity launched in 2007 and by January 2015 had 70 million members. On 5 January 2016 the company settled the FTC’s case: a $50 million judgment, reduced to $2 million because of its financial condition. The order bars the company from claiming its products improve performance at school, at work or in athletics, or delay age-related decline, without competent and reliable scientific evidence.

The games where practice matters most

The 2014 meta-analysis the post cites found that deliberate practice explained 26% of the differences in performance among games players, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions. Our arithmetic: even in games, 74% of the difference is left to other things.

Flow, as the researchers describe it

Wikipedia’s article says flow was first presented[5] in Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi’s 1975 book Beyond Boredom and Anxiety. He began studying it after becoming fascinated by painters who got so absorbed that they forgot food, water and sleep. The name came from interviewees who said things like “I was carried on by the flow”. Jeanne Nakamura and Csikszentmihalyi list six features of the experience:

  • intense, focused concentration on the present moment;
  • merging of action and awareness;
  • loss of reflective self-consciousness;
  • a sense of personal control;
  • a distorted sense of time;
  • the activity feeling rewarding in itself.

The same article says flow is most likely at moderate levels of arousal, where you are neither overwhelmed nor bored. That is a similar idea to the 85% result, though it describes an experience and not an equation, and it says nothing about transfer.

The twist

The review’s three tiers say practice helps the trained task most, closely related tasks less and distant ones hardly at all. The chess work reaches the same place from the other side, with a master who is helpless on a random board. Neither says anything against a daily puzzle you enjoy. They say what to expect from it: you get better at that puzzle.
