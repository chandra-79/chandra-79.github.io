THE ZIGGLE BLOG

One puzzle for everyone

Why every Ziggle daily is the same for every player, and what that small rule makes possible.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 30 December 2025

Newspapers have always worked this way. Everyone who buys Saturday’s paper gets the same crossword, so you can moan about 14 across with a stranger on the train. In 2021, Wordle brought the same idea to phones: one word a day, the same for everyone, and a little grid of squares to share.

How Ziggle makes the same puzzle everywhere

Each Ziggle daily is built from the date. The date goes into a seeded random number generator: a recipe that always produces the same sequence of “random” choices from the same starting seed. Your phone, your laptop and our server all follow that recipe and arrive at exactly the same board, without any of them downloading it.

That has a few nice side effects:

  • It works offline. Your device builds today’s puzzle itself.
  • Scores can be checked. Because the server can rebuild the same board, it replays your moves before a result joins a leaderboard.
  • The archive is exact. Replaying a past day gives you the same puzzle everyone had that day.

Why not personalise everything?

A puzzle tuned only to you is useful for practice, and Ziggle has plenty of that. But a shared puzzle gives you something practice can’t: a conversation. “Did you get today’s lock in three?” only works if we all had the same lock.

So the dailies are shared, the practice is yours, and the two never mix: replaying the archive or practising never changes your streak.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

A word game that started in 2013

The Wikipedia article on Wordle gives the history in some detail. The Welsh software engineer Josh Wardle made a first prototype in 2013, which he called “Mr. Bugs’ Wordy Nugz”[1], inspired by the colour-matching of the board game Mastermind. It held all of the roughly 13,000 five-letter words in English, but his partner, Palak Shah, struggled with the obscure ones, and guessing started to feel as random as Mastermind. So Shah sorted the list into words she knew, did not know, and might have known, and it shrank to about 2,000 familiar words, which at one a day was enough for roughly five years. Wardle lost interest and put it aside.

The idea came back during the pandemic, when he and his partner got into the New York Times Spelling Bee and daily crossword. He borrowed the minimal design and the one-puzzle-a-day format of Spelling Bee, named the game as a pun on his surname, and published it on the web for the two of them by January 2021. It spread to relatives, then friends, then, in mid-October 2021, the wider world.

  1. 2013First prototype, with all 13,000 or so five-letter words.
  2. Jan 2021Wordle is published on the web, for Wardle and his partner only.
  3. Oct 2021It starts to spread among friends and family, and then virally.
  4. 1 Nov 2021About 90 players.
  5. Late Dec 2021Wardle adds the shareable emoji grid, after friends in New Zealand had shared results that way, and the game goes viral on Twitter.
  6. 2 Jan 2022More than 300,000 players, and over 2 million a week later.
  7. 31 Jan 2022The New York Times Company buys it, for a “low seven-figure” sum.

The article says the emoji sharing is what turned it viral, and that Wardle attributed the appeal in part to scarcity: one puzzle a day leaves players wanting more, and keeps the time spent to about three minutes a day. Between 1 and 13 January 2022, 1.2 million results were shared on Twitter. Wardle also said that he had no plans to make money from it, and that he does not know each day’s word so that he can play himself.

How long does a list last?

A daily game needs a list of answers, and the size of the list sets how long before anything repeats. Ziggle’s Letter Lock takes the answer for day n from entry n − 1 of its list, going round again when the list runs out (computed from the code), so the number of answers is the number of days before the cycle repeats.

  • 2,309words in Wordle’s curated answer list today
  • 809answers in Letter Lock’s list
  • 2.2years before the cycle comes round (computed)

So Letter Lock’s answers repeat after about 2.2 years, a lot sooner than a game with a list of 2,309. A short list is a known cost of a curated one: regular players will meet a repeat.

When two copies of the same list disagree

Wordle’s story contains a warning about shared puzzles. The article says Wordle ran entirely in client-side JavaScript, so when fans feared the game would be put behind a paywall, some saved the web page to keep playing offline. After the New York Times took over, it removed words from the answer list that it judged insensitive or offensive, and removed some British spelling variants such as “fibre”; by July 2022 it had taken seven words out of the original 2,315[1]. The article notes that this left the Times version out of step with the older, cached versions, and players using different versions found it hard to compare scores.

That is the problem a seeded generator is meant to remove. Everyone’s device runs the same recipe on the same date, so there is nothing to fall out of step, as long as the recipe itself never changes. Ziggle treats the recipe as part of the game’s contract: puzzles must come out identical on every device, which is why shuffles use a fixed algorithm and never a random comparator.

What “random” means here

The article on pseudorandom number generators explains the trick. A PRNG makes a sequence of numbers that looks random but is completely determined by its starting value, the seed. That is a weakness for cryptography, where output must not be predictable, and a strength for games, where reproducibility is the point. John von Neumann joked that anyone who tries to produce random digits by arithmetic is in a state of sin, and the article spends a good deal of space on generators that turned out to be flawed. One, called RANDU, was used for decades on mainframe computers and was seriously flawed, though the flaw went undetected for a very long time. Java relied on a low-quality linear congruential generator until 2020 and was upgraded in Java 17.

The famous good one is the Mersenne Twister, published in 1998, with a period of 219937 − 1, which the article gives as about 4.3 × 106001[2]. Ziggle does not need anything like that. Its generator is a small one that keeps a 32-bit counter, adds a fixed odd number to it for each value and scrambles the result. The article describes a family called counter-based generators, whose only state is a counter, so that they can jump to any point in the sequence at once. Because the added number is odd, the counter takes all 232 = 4,294,967,296 values before it returns to where it started (computed). A puzzle needs thousands of choices, not billions, so that is plenty.

Because the generator is not secret, there is a trade-off that Wordle did not have. Wardle can say he does not know tomorrow’s word. Anyone who reads Ziggle’s code can work out any day’s puzzle in advance, because it is only a function of the date. We accept that. A daily is for conversation, not for secrets, and the server’s replay check does not depend on the puzzle being hidden: it works whether or not you have seen the board before.

The twist

The article records that a different, unrelated game also called Wordle!, released five years earlier, saw a surge of downloads because people confused the two. Its developer teamed up with Wardle to donate $50,000 to a tutoring charity. A name can be shared as easily as a puzzle.
