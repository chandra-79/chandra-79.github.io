43 quintillion ways to be wrong: the Rubik’s Cube by numbers

A sculptor’s teaching tool, 500 million sales, and a proof that every scramble can be solved in 20 moves.

In 1974, a Hungarian sculptor and architecture professor named Ernő Rubik made a puzzle out of little cubes to help explain three-dimensional movement. Then he scrambled it, and had to work out how to unscramble it. It reportedly took him a while. You are in excellent company.

From Budapest to everywhere

Rubik applied for a Hungarian patent in January 1975. The first test batches of the “Magic Cube” appeared in Budapest in late 1977, and in 1980 it went worldwide as the Rubik’s Cube. By January 2024, about 500 million had been sold, making it the best-selling puzzle toy in the world. A fair share are still sitting in drawers with one stubborn corner twisted.

The numbers

  • A standard 3×3 cube has 43,252,003,274,489,856,000 possible arrangements. That’s about 43 quintillion. Only one of them is solved.
  • In July 2010, a team including Tomas Rokicki proved that every single one of those arrangements can be solved in 20 moves or fewer[1]. Cubers call that number “God’s number”, which feels like a lot of pressure for a number.
  • The official 3×3 world records, as the World Cube Association lists them in November 2026, are 2.51 seconds[2] for a single solve (reported in October 2026 as set by nine-year-old Xuanyi Geng of China, who took it from Teodor Zajder’s 2.76 of February 2026) and 3.51 seconds for an average of five, by Yiheng Wang of China. Records move fast: check the WCA records page.

How people actually get fast

Nobody memorises 43 quintillion positions. Fast solvers learn a method that breaks the cube into stages, and learn the common patterns for each stage.

  1. Beginner method: solve it layer by layer, using a handful of short move sequences. Most people can learn it in a weekend.
  2. Two-look last layer: 16 algorithms that make the final layer much quicker.
  3. Full CFOP: the method every recent record holder has used, with 119 algorithms in total: 41 for pairing pieces, 57 for orienting the last layer and 21 for finishing it.
  4. Lookahead: the real secret. Top solvers spot the next pieces while they’re still solving the current ones, so they never stop to look.

Notice the pattern: it’s chunking again, the same way chess masters see positions. A beginner sees 54 stickers. An expert sees a handful of familiar cases.

Where the 43 quintillion comes from

Wikipedia’s article on the cube derives the number[3] from the pieces. There are eight corners and twelve edges. The corners can be arranged in 8! = 40,320 ways, and each corner has three orientations, but the last is fixed by the other seven, giving 37 = 2,187. The edges can be arranged in 12!/2 = 239,500,800 ways, halved because the edges must be in an even permutation exactly when the corners are, and eleven edges can be flipped freely, giving 211 = 2,048.

The cube was first advertised as having “over 3,000,000,000 combinations but only one solution”. Our arithmetic: the true count is about 14.4 billion times larger than that advertised figure. Taking the cube apart and rebuilding it freely would allow 8! × 38 × 12! × 212 = 519,024,039,293,878,272,000 arrangements, exactly twelve times as many, and only one in twelve of them can be solved by turning. No sequence of turns can swap one pair of pieces or twist a single corner or edge, so the twelve “universes” never meet.

Why at least 18 moves are needed, by counting

The article gives a counting argument[4] for the lower bound. At most 19,973,266,111,335,481,264 positions can be reached in 17 moves or fewer, which is less than the 43.25 quintillion positions in total (computed: the total is 2.17 times as large). So some positions need at least 18 moves. It is not constructive, because it names no position. The superflip, in which every corner is right and every edge is flipped, needs 20 face turns: a 20-move solution was found in 1992 by Dik Winter and proved the shortest in 1995 by Michael Reid.

The fast side: from 22.95 seconds to 2.51

Speedcubing began with a championship on 5 June 1982 in Budapest, with nineteen competitors, won by Minh Thai with 22.95 seconds. The sport faded after 1983 and revived on the internet, and the World Cube Association, founded in 2004, counted 12 competitions in 2004 and over 1,150 in 2018. The record today is 2.51 seconds for a single solve (Xuanyi Geng, October 2026) and 3.51 for an average of five (Yiheng Wang, June 2026). Our arithmetic: the first champion’s time is 9.1 times today’s record.

CFOP, the method every record holder[5] of the last decade has used, was published online by Jessica Fridrich in 1997, building on Singmaster’s layer-by-layer method of 1980 and Guus Razoux Schultz’s improvements for the first two layers. It asks you to learn 41 first-two-layer cases, 57 orientation algorithms and 21 permutation algorithms, 119 in all. A beginner-friendlier route that Wikipedia calls four-look last layer cuts the 78 last-layer algorithms to 16, the figure the post gives for its two-look route.

How the cube reached the world

  1. 19 May 1974Rubik invents the cube, per the speedcubing article.
  2. 30 January 1975Rubik applies for the Hungarian patent.
  3. Late 1977The first test batches go on sale in Budapest.
  4. February 1979Tibor Laczi takes a cube to the Nuremberg Toy Fair, where Tom Kremer sees it.
  5. September 1979Seven Towns and Rubik’s partners sign with Ideal Toys.
  6. 1980The cube goes worldwide as Rubik’s Cube.
  7. January 2024About 500 million sold.

Wikipedia adds a point that the post’s story slightly sharpens: Rubik’s main aim, according to the article, was an engineering one, a structure whose parts all move independently without falling apart, and he did not realise he had built a puzzle until he scrambled it and tried to restore it, which took about a month.

The twist

In the face-turn count, where God’s number is 20, hundreds of millions of positions need all 20 moves. In the quarter-turn count, where the answer is 26, only a single position, plus its two rotations, is known to need all 26. The same cube is far more crowded at the top in one measure than the other.
