Hailstone numbers: a simple rule nobody can prove

Halve it if it is even, triple it and add one if it is odd. Everyone who has tried ends at 1. Nobody knows why.

Logic games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 5 August 2026

Here is a rule a child can follow. Pick any whole number. If it is even, halve it. If it is odd, triple it and add one. Repeat. Start with 6: 6, 3, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Start with 7 and you wander for 16 steps before reaching 1. The Collatz conjecture says that every starting number ends at 1. It has been open since the 1930s.

The wild one is 27

Most small numbers fall quickly. But 27 takes 111 steps and on the way climbs as high as 9,232, about 342 times its starting value, before dropping to 1.

Under 1,000, the longest journey belongs to 871, at 178 steps.

How far has it been checked?

Computers have tested every starting value up to 271[1], which is about 2.4 × 1021, as a 2025 paper reports: all of them end at 1. That is a great deal of evidence and no proof; in mathematics a billion billion cases is still a hunch.

The twist

The conjecture looks small and has defeated the best minds. One way to see the difficulty is that the rule mixes two operations that do not get on: halving (a division) and tripling-plus-one (a multiplication with an offset). There is no obvious reason they should never produce a number that goes up forever, or loops without reaching 1, and there is no obvious way to show they cannot.

Why it belongs here

Because it is a puzzle in the purest sense: a rule, a goal, and a feeling that you almost have it. Number puzzles of every kind, including Sum Spark, share that taste of arithmetic that wanders before it lands.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Two more journeys to try

Start at 12: 12, 6, 3, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, nine steps. Start at 19 and the path is longer: 19, 58, 29, 88, 44, 22, 11, 34, 17, 52, 26, 13, 40, 20, 10, 5, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1, which is 20 steps. Numbers that look alike can behave very differently: 9,780,657,630 and 9,780,657,631 both take exactly 1,132 steps, but 9,663 takes 184 steps and climbs to a peak of 27,114,424, about 27 million.

A hint of a theorem

In 2019 Terence Tao proved[2] a striking partial result. Using a measure called logarithmic density, he showed that almost all Collatz orbits come down below any function of the starting number that grows to infinity, however slowly. That is not the conjecture (which says every orbit reaches 1), but it is the strongest result of its kind: if the conjecture is false, the counterexamples are vanishingly rare.

Why a billion billion checks is not a proof

The conjecture holds for every start up to 271, about 2.36 × 1021. But the same article warns that computer evidence “is still not rigorous proof[2]”, because counterexamples may lie among very large numbers, as with two conjectures in number theory (the Pólya and Mertens conjectures) that held across huge ranges and were later disproved.

The twist

A conjecture can pass every test you can run and still be false; mathematics has been surprised before. The 271 check does not tell us the Collatz rule is true. It tells us that if it is false, the counterexample is bigger than a number with twenty-two digits, and that the people hunting for it have long since run out of small places to look.
