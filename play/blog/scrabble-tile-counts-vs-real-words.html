Scrabble’s 100 tiles versus the five-letter words we actually use

Scrabble has twelve Es and four Ss. In our list of 809 five-letter answers, S is the first letter of one word in six.

Word games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 11 November 2026

A Scrabble set has 100 tiles[1]: 98 letters and 2 blanks. The mix was designed by Alfred Butts, who by the popular account studied letter frequencies in newspapers (the story that he counted the front page of The New York Times is “a popular story”[1], not a documented fact). The result is the distribution everyone knows: twelve Es, nine As and Is, eight Os, six each of N, R and T, and so on down to single Js, Ks, Qs, Xs and Zs.

How a general set compares to one length of word

Scrabble tiles have to build words of every length. Letter Lock’s 809 answers are all exactly five letters. So how does the one mix compare with the other?

E leads both: 12.2% of Scrabble’s letters, 11.7% of ours. But look at R and S. They are far more common in five-letter words (8.9% and 5.8%) than in the tile bag (6.1% and 4.1%). Short words lean on a few workhorse letters, and some of those letters are ones that Scrabble deliberately rations, because S can pluralise nearly anything and is valuable in play.

The twist

The tile that is worth one point in Scrabble is the first letter of 140 of our 809 answers (17.3%): S begins more of them than any other letter, with C a distant second at 68. Meanwhile the letters Scrabble prices at 8 or 10 points (J, X, Q, Z) appear only 7, 7, 12 and 14 times in all 4,045 letters. The points are not rarity-pricing for five-letter words; they are for the long tail of words you can build.

Why this matters when you guess

If you know the first letter is more likely to be S than anything else, and that E is the likeliest last letter (it ends 22.5% of our answers), you can pick a first guess that uses those odds. The next post, the best first guess in Letter Lock, does exactly that.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

An architect’s game that nobody wanted

Alfred Mosher Butts was an architect, and he invented the game in 1931. He first called it Lexiko, later Criss Cross Words, and began to look for a buyer. In 1948 the game was trademarked[3], and Brunot and his wife turned an abandoned schoolhouse in Dodgingtown, Connecticut, into a Scrabble factory.

  1. 1931Butts invents the game, trying different letter distributions as he goes.
  2. Before 1948The game is called Lexiko, then Criss Cross Words, while Butts looks for a buyer.
  3. 1948The game is trademarked and the Brunots open a factory in a former schoolhouse.
  4. 1949The Brunots make 2,400 sets and lose $450.
  5. 1952Demand is more than they can handle; they ask the game maker Selchow and Righter to market and distribute it.

The distribution of tiles is Butts’s own. He gave the game only four S tiles[3], so that the ease of pluralising words would not make play too easy. That choice is why S is rationed in Scrabble while being the commonest first letter of five-letter words.

Every language gets its own bag

Most languages use 100 tiles, which is the original 98 letters plus two blanks, and every set is tuned to its own language. The same idea of a bag fitted to a language gives quite different mixes:

Italian is the odd one out: its commonest tile is O, not E, and the vowels A, E, I and O make up more than four tiles in ten. A set built for another language is, in effect, a one-line summary of how that language spells itself. The same article records that one distribution, in which K appears only in borrowed words, values the K at 20 points, the highest value it knows of for any letter in any set[4].

The twist

The popular story is that Butts built his distribution by counting letters on the front page of The New York Times. The Wikipedia article calls that “a popular story”, and so do we. What is documented is smaller and more human: he tried different distributions, and he kept the S tiles few on purpose.
