Type faster by slowing down

136 million keystrokes say the fastest typists aren’t the ones racing. They’re the ones who don’t have to go back.

Most of us learned to type by accident. A school computer lab, a lot of chatting, a bit of hunt-and-peck that slowly got faster. So how fast is normal, and what actually makes someone quick?

The biggest typing study ever

In 2018, researchers from Aalto University in Finland and the University of Cambridge collected 136 million keystrokes from about 168,000 volunteers in more than 200 countries, all taking the same online typing test.

  • The average typist managed 52 words a minute. The fastest hit 120.
  • For comparison, trained professional typists in the 1970s and 80s typically worked at 60 to 90.
  • Fast typists made fewer mistakes. The careless-but-quick typists spent so long correcting that they lost the race.
  • The fastest typists used rollover on 40–70% of their keystrokes, pressing the next key before releasing the last one.
  • Formal typing courses made no clear difference. Some self-taught people using fewer than ten fingers were just as fast as touch typists.

So you don’t need to have done a typing course at 14 to get quick. Plenty of fast typists never did.

Why accuracy comes first

Here’s the maths that surprises people. One wrong key isn’t one keystroke lost. It’s the wrong key, a backspace, the right key, and the moment it takes to notice and recover. Do that every twenty characters and your “fast” typing is quietly running at the speed of someone careful.

The study’s own advice was refreshingly unglamorous: slow down enough to avoid errors, don’t look at your fingers, practise with new sentences rather than the same text, and practise deliberately.

What “words per minute” means

By convention, a “word” is five characters, spaces included. So 50 WPM means 250 characters a minute, which is about four keys a second. Say it out loud and it sounds a lot less heroic. That’s good news: you’re only ever one key away from the next.

A week-one plan

  1. Take the placement test once, so the trainer knows where to start.
  2. Do two lesson runs a day. That’s about three minutes.
  3. Turn off the on-screen keyboard on day four, and stop looking down. It will feel awful for a few days. Then, all at once, it won’t.
  4. On day seven, take a 30-second speed test and compare.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Who was in the data

The headline number covers 168,000 people, but the paper is careful about who they were. Of about 406,000[1] volunteers who started the online test, only about 193,000 finished it, and the analysis used 168,000. The authors describe the sample as people interested in testing and developing their typing, and many had had some typing training. So the average of 51.56 words a minute[1] (standard deviation 20.2) describes keen typists, not the whole population.

  • 51.56average words a minute (SD 20.2)
  • 120+words a minute for the fastest typists
  • 239 msaverage gap between keys (SD 112)
  • 4.2keys a second on average

Training is worth about five words a minute

The study compared people who had taken typing training with those who had not. Trained typists were on average 5 words a minute faster[1], with similar spread, “a relatively small difference” (d = 0.27). A formal course explains very little of the variation between typists: the spread (about 20 words a minute either side) is four times bigger than the benefit of training.

Eight kinds of typist

Clustering each person’s pattern of gaps between keys, the authors found that most users fall into eight groups of typing style[1]. What the paper stresses is that rollover, pressing the next key before releasing the last, “is surprisingly prevalent”. That is the finding behind this post’s title: speed is more about rhythm and overlap than about how hard or how fast any one key press is.

Who typed, and what was counted

The paper’s own table of volunteers[2]: they came from 218 countries, 68% from the United States and 85% with English as a first language. The average age was 24.5, 72% said they had taken a typing course, the average typist spent 3.2 hours a day typing, and 98.1% used a QWERTY layout. Each person typed 15 sentences. The authors dropped anyone with an error rate over 25% or a gap of more than 50 seconds between keys, about 12% of participants.

The fastest tenth against the slowest tenth

The fast typists’ words-a-minute advantage is about 4.3 times, and almost all of it sits in the gaps between keys, which are about 4 times shorter. The time a key is held down differs by only a factor of 1.2. The paper’s verdict is that overlapping presses is a strong predictor of speed, with a correlation of 0.73 with words a minute, and that fast typists roll over on 40–70% of keystrokes. One of its eight groups of typists, the second largest, types about 68 words a minute with 38% rollover and the lowest error rates.

What a word a minute is in milliseconds

One claim in the post needs a source. “Some self-taught people using fewer than ten fingers were just as fast as touch typists” comes from earlier motion-capture research the paper cites, on a small sample. In the paper’s own data the number of fingers a person says they use does predict speed.

The twist

The average gap between keys is 239 milliseconds, about four keys a second, which is roughly 50 words a minute. Because the next key can go down before the last one comes up, the gap between keys understates how busy the fingers are: speed comes from overlapping movements, not from hitting each key faster.
