Sudoku was born in Indiana

The “Japanese” number puzzle started life in an American magazine as Number Place, and needed two more countries to become famous.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 23 January 2026

Sudoku sounds Japanese, and its name is. The puzzle itself was first published in the United States.

Number Place, 1979

In 1979, Dell Magazines in New York ran a puzzle called Number Place. It is generally credited to Howard Garns, a retired architect from Indiana. The rules were the ones you know: fill a 9 × 9 grid so every row, column and 3 × 3 box holds the digits 1 to 9 once each.

A name in Japan, 1984

In 1984 the Japanese publisher Nikoli introduced the puzzle to Japan under a long name that roughly means “the digits must be single”. It was shortened to sūdoku. Nikoli also championed hand-made puzzles with a pleasing, logical path to the answer.

A craze in Britain, 2004

The global boom came after Wayne Gould, a retired judge from New Zealand, wrote a computer program to generate the puzzles and persuaded The Times in London to print one in November 2004. Within a year sudoku was in newspapers around the world.

What we took from each

From Number Place: the rules, unchanged. From Nikoli: the belief that a puzzle should be solvable by reasoning, never by guessing, so every Ziggle Sudoku is checked to have exactly one solution. From the 2004 boom: the idea of a fresh puzzle every day.

And we added variety the classic grid doesn’t have: Addoku cages with sums, both long diagonals, a 6 × 6 mini, and even/odd cells, at four difficulties. On easy and medium, the number pad switches a digit off once all of it is in place, so you can see what is left.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Before Number Place: the French ancestors

The grid is older than the 1979 magazine. In the 1890s Paris newspapers were removing numbers from magic squares and asking readers to fill them back in. Le Siècle printed a half-filled 9 × 9 square with 3 × 3 sub-squares on 19 November 1892, though it needed arithmetic, not logic, and used two-digit numbers. On 6 July 1895 its rival La France got closer: a puzzle called carré magique diabolique (“diabolical magic square”) that used only 1 to 9. Its rows, columns and broken diagonals each held the digits once, and although the 3 × 3 boxes were not marked, they came out right too. Such puzzles ran weekly in French papers such as L’Écho de Paris for about a decade and disappeared around the First World War. Whether Garns ever saw one is unclear.

The architect in the back office

Howard Garns was born in Connersville, Indiana, on 2 March 1905, studied architectural engineering at the University of Illinois, served as a captain in the US Army Corps of Engineers in the Second World War, then joined the Daggett architecture firm in Indianapolis. A draftsman there, George Wiley, remembered him bent over a spare drawing board: “It looked like a crossword puzzle but it had numbers.[3]” When Wiley walked round to look, Garns covered it up. It was a secret.

Dell Pencil Puzzles and Word Games printed Number Place in May 1979 with no byline. The credit came out only in 2005, when Will Shortz noticed that Garns’s name appeared in the contributors’ list in every issue that carried Number Place and in none that did not. Garns died on 6 October 1989, fifteen years before The Times ran the first British sudoku.

How big is the space of sudoku?

  • 6.67 × 10²¹ways to fill the 9 × 9 grid
  • 5,472,730,538[2]essentially different ones
  • 17the fewest clues a proper puzzle can have

A finished grid is a Latin square with the extra rule about boxes. There are exactly 6,670,903,752,021,072,936,960[2] of them. Counting grids as the same if you can turn one into another by rotating, reflecting, shuffling rows and columns in the allowed ways, or renaming the digits leaves 5,472,730,538. Dividing one by the other (computed), each essentially different grid stands for about 1.2 trillion others. Nobody knows exactly how many minimal puzzles exist (ones where deleting any clue would give more than one answer), but a statistical estimate is about 3.1 × 10³⁷.

The “17” in the figures is a proven result: the paper in this post’s references showed by computer search that no 16-clue puzzle has a single solution. The article notes that tens of thousands of puzzles with 17 clues exist. In general, solving a sudoku on an n² × n² grid is NP-complete, so while backtracking is fast on the 9 × 9 grid, there is no known shortcut as the grid grows. The biggest puzzle the article mentions is a 100 × 100 “Sudoku-zilla”, published in 2010.

The variants behind Ziggle’s extras

Many of our extras are old friends. Adding sums to cages is the killer sudoku, a mix of sudoku and kakuro, where the numbers in a cage cannot repeat. Making both long diagonals unique is the common X variant. A 6 × 6 grid with 3 × 2 regions is the “Mini Sudoku” printed in USA Today. Our even/odd cells are the same kind of idea: an extra rule on top of row, column and box.

The twist

In June 2008 an Australian drugs trial that had already cost more than A$1 million was aborted when it turned out that four or five of the twelve jurors had been playing sudoku instead of listening to the evidence.
