The birthday paradox, and why daily puzzles are never picked at random

In a room of 23 people, two probably share a birthday. The same sums explain why a “random” daily puzzle would repeat within weeks.

Behind Ziggle · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 20 June 2026

How many people do you need in a room before it is more likely than not that two share a birthday? Not 183, half of 365. The answer is 23: with 23 people the chance of at least one shared birthday is 50.7%. With 57 people it is 99.0%, and with 70 it is 99.92%.

Why it feels wrong

We imagine a match with us, which needs about 253 people for even odds. The paradox counts every pair: 23 people form 253 pairs, and any pair could match. The number of chances grows with the square of the group, so a match turns up far sooner than your gut expects.

What it means for a daily puzzle

Suppose a game picked each day’s puzzle at random from a list of 400. The same arithmetic says the first repeat arrives, on average, after about 25 days (the square root of π × 400 ÷ 2), not after 400. Players would see a repeat within a month or so, and would be right to be annoyed.

The twist

This is why daily puzzles are not picked at random. Word Builder’s word of the day, for instance, takes the whole list in one fixed shuffled order and goes through it, one word a day, so nothing repeats until the list has been used up. A shuffle is a plan; a random pick is a coin toss that remembers nothing.

Not just birthdays

The same sums sit behind hash collisions in computing, shared lottery numbers and the date you share with a stranger at a conference. “It couldn’t be a coincidence” is a feeling, not a measurement: in a big enough crowd coincidences are the norm.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

An answer worth checking by hand

The calculation is short enough to do on paper. Imagine the people walking into the room one at a time. The first can have any birthday. The second must avoid the first’s day, which has a probability of 364/365. The third must avoid two days (363/365), and so on until the twenty-third, who must avoid twenty-two (343/365). Multiply these together and you have the chance that nobody shares a birthday; subtract it from one for the chance that somebody does. For 23 people the exact figure is 50.7297%[3]: the chance of no match is 49.2703% and of at least one match 50.7297%.

A shortcut you can do in your head

Each factor 1 − a/365 is almost exactly e−a/365, and multiplying those adds the exponents. The sum 1 + 2 + … + (n − 1) is n(n − 1)/2, which is the number of pairs, so the chance of at least one shared birthday is about 1 − e−n(n−1)/730. The exponent counts pairs: every pair is another roll of a 1-in-365 die. Set the exponent to ln 2 (the point where the chance of no match is one half) and you get n ≈ 23 straight away.

What the model leaves out

The standard calculation assumes 365 equally likely birthdays and ignores leap years, twins, selection bias and the seasonal and weekly variation in real birth rates[3]. Those are the choices that keep the sum clean, and any real room will differ a little from the model. At the other end the pigeonhole principle settles it: with 366 people a match is certain, however the birthdays fall.

The twist

The problem is generally credited to Harold Davenport, in about 1927[3], although he never published it. A question that now opens every probability course began life as an unpublished remark. And “23 is less than 1/15th of 365” is the whole surprise in one comparison: we compare ourselves to the whole calendar, when the calendar is being compared with all 253 pairs.
