Every four-digit number ends up at 6174

Pick almost any four digits, do one simple trick again and again, and you always land on the same number within seven steps.

Logic games · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 17 August 2026

In this article 6 sections

Try this. Pick a four-digit number with at least two different digits, say 3524. Arrange its digits from biggest to smallest (5432) and from smallest to biggest (2345). Subtract: 5432 − 2345 = 3087. Do it again with 3087: 8730 − 0378 = 8352. Again: 8532 − 2358 = 6174. Three steps. Now try 1001, 9998 or your birthday. You will always end at 6174, and once there you stay: 7641 − 1467 = 6174.

The Indian mathematician D. R. Kaprekar published the observation in 1955. The remarkable part is how fast it works: every four-digit number with at least two different digits (leading zeros allowed) reaches 6174 within seven steps[1].

All 9,990 of them, counted

We ran the routine on every four-digit start. Ten of the 10,000 are repeated digits such as 3333 (which collapse to zero), leaving 9,990. Here is how many steps they need:

It is not a trick of the number 10

Do the same with three digits and every start lands on 495. With five digits, however, there is no single destination: we found 3 separate loops that the numbers fall into, and none of them is a fixed point. Four digits (and three) are special, which is exactly why 6174 is famous.

The twist

The 5-digit routine splits the 99,990 possible starts into three groups of about 48,000, 3,000 and 48,000, each ending in its own cycle. So three and four digits giving one clean answer is a feature of those lengths, not of the routine in general.

Something to try on a train

Ask a friend for a four-digit number (not all the same digit), do the routine in your head, and tell them how many steps it will take before they have finished. The most you will ever need is seven. Sum Spark is a gentler cousin of the same game: small arithmetic, chained together to hit a target.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Why every step lands on a multiple of 9

Look at the sequence in the example: 3087, 8352, 6174. Each is a multiple of 9, and that is not a coincidence. The two numbers being subtracted have exactly the same digits, so they have the same digit sum, and two numbers with the same digit sum leave the same remainder when divided by 9. Their difference therefore has remainder zero. The same argument works in any base b, where every step after the first is a multiple of b − 1[3]. We checked it on the whole range: all 54 values that the first step can possibly produce, from the 9,990 valid starts, are multiples of 9 (confirmed; computed).

That is a big shrinkage. Ten thousand starting numbers collapse after one step into about 54 possible values, and each of them is a multiple of 9 with a limited set of digit patterns. The fast convergence is partly just this bottleneck.

Only two destinations in the whole decimal system

6174 is not the only fixed point of a Kaprekar-style routine, but it is one of only two in base 10. Prichett and colleagues showed that the constants are limited to 495[3] (for three digits) and 6174 (for four). There are none at all for 1, 2, 5 or 7 digits, because no solution fits the equations that describe them. For other lengths there are longer fixed points built from patterns: 6174, 631764, 63317664 and so on, a 6, a run of 3s, a 1, 7, a run of 6s and a 4 where the two runs are equal in length. Eleven digits give a loop of five numbers, and thirteen a loop of two, rather than a single fixed point.

The routine works in other bases too. The article gives base 4 as a worked example, with fixed points 3021, 310221, 31102221 and so on, a pattern of runs of 1s and 2s that grows exactly as the base-10 one does.

The twist

Only 384 of the 9,990 valid starts reach 6174 in a single step (computed). The magic is not in the last step but in the first: one subtraction squeezes ten thousand numbers into a short list of multiples of 9, and the rest of the journey happens inside that narrow gate.
