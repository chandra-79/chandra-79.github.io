The 1888 contest that made touch typing famous

A typist who never looked at the keys beat a rival who used eight fingers. Touch typing has been taught one key at a time ever since.

Training design · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 21 August 2026

In 1888, in Cincinnati, a court stenographer named Frank McGurrin took on Louis Traub in a typing contest. McGurrin typed without looking at the keyboard and is reported to have invented home-row touch typing; Traub used an eight-finger method. McGurrin won convincingly, and the publicity helped make touch typing the standard way to learn.

Why you learn it in small pieces

Touch typing means each finger owns a few keys, and you find them by position rather than sight. That is a lot of new habits at once, so courses have long introduced keys gradually, starting from the home row where your fingers rest.

Two things matter more than raw speed while you learn: accuracy, because errors repeated become habits, and not looking, because glancing down breaks the position sense you are building.

In Ziggle’s typing trainer

  • Level 1 is the home row. Each later level unlocks a few more keys.
  • To pass a level you need to hit an accuracy goal as well as a speed goal.
  • Drills mix in the keys you get wrong most often, so practice goes where it is needed.
  • The key map shows how each key is going, so you can see which fingers need work without looking down while you type.

McGurrin had no app. He just practised without looking, one key at a time. It still works.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The contest, and the doubt about it

The match took place on 25 July 1888[1]. According to the Wikipedia article McGurrin won $500 (about $18,000 in 2025 money), and the result was on the front pages of many newspapers. It adds that McGurrin was reportedly the only person touch typing at the time, but that whether he was truly the first, or only the first to be noticed, is disputed: the speeds reached by other typists in contemporary contests suggest that they must have been using similar systems. Traub, meanwhile, was using a Caligraph with an eight-finger method. (An earlier version of this post called him a two-finger champion. That was wrong: the article says eight fingers.)

What “home row” means

Touch typing in the narrow sense is a method in which the eight fingers rest on the middle row, called the home row, and reach for the other keys. On a QWERTY keyboard those home keys are A S D F for the left hand and J K L ; for the right. The thumbs rest on the space bar. Most keyboards have a small bump on F and J so that your index fingers can find home again without looking. The article’s example is the word “poll”: the right little finger reaches up to P and returns to the semicolon, the ring finger goes up to O and back, and the same finger then presses L twice. Typists who do not look but do not use the home row are called hybrid typists.

The number row has its own traditional method. Since the 1960s the left little finger has been taught to take 1 and 2, the ring finger 3, the middle finger 4 and the left index 5 and 6, the right index finger 7 and 8, the middle finger 9 and the ring finger 0. A mixed version uses the left-hand rule with the usual right-hand fingering, which the article says some people consider more ergonomic.

How Ziggle’s ten levels are laid out

The trainer follows the usual order, but it adds a gate that the article says matters: weak keys. Typing speed, the article notes, is typically determined by how slowly the weak keys are typed, not by how fast the rest are. So on the first three levels the letters unlock gradually: a key counts as ready only after at least 15 presses at 95% accuracy and the level’s target speed, and the next key opens only when every key already open is ready. The three levels add 9, 10 and 7 letters (computed from the key map), 26 in all.

For comparison, the article gives typical speeds: most people type about 30 to 40 words a minute, 60 to 80 is roughly the speed needed to keep up with your thoughts, and professional typists can sustain more than 100. It also notes that a Microsoft survey suggested many managers expect at least 50, and cites a 2014 report that fewer than half of Britons reach it[1]. On that scale, Level 8 is the “50 words a minute” line.

The placement test lets people skip lessons they do not need. A typing sample below 95% accuracy starts you at Level 1. Otherwise it places you by speed: 25 words a minute or more starts at Level 4, 35 at Level 5, 40 at Level 6 and 50 or more at Level 8 (computed from the placement rule).

Does the ten-finger method win?

Not always, and the article is candid about it. A 2016 study from Aalto University[1], with 30 typists of varied styles, found little difference in speed between touch typists and self-taught hybrid typists, who used six fingers on average. What the fastest typists shared was keeping their hands in one position instead of moving them across the keyboard, and consistently using the same finger for each letter. The study has been criticised because it only included people typing up to 75 words a minute, so it tells us little about the fastest typists.

Read together with the home-row method, that is encouraging: what Ziggle’s lessons train, with fingers that stay near home and a fixed finger for every key, is the part of the method the study found separated fast typists from the rest.

Why it feels worse before it feels better

The article, citing a 2003 paper in Human Factors on the move from touch-typing training to everyday use, says that for people who already hunt and peck, the first performance as a touch typist is far lower, so the change does not seem worth it at first. Speed commonly plummets at the start, it adds, but with consistent practice the dip does not last more than a few months. That is the case for taking the levels in order and not skipping the first lessons to get back to your old speed.

The twist

The article notes that many manufacturers sell blank mechanical keyboards, with no letters printed on the keys. A trained touch typist should not mind, and a hunt-and-peck typist is forced to type without looking, much like on the Braille keyboards used by people who are blind.
