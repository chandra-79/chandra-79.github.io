Mix it up: why eight different puzzles beat eighty of one

Shuffled maths homework did “vastly” better a week later. Your puzzle habit can use the same trick.

Science of practice · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 9 June 2026

Most homework is built in blocks. Twenty problems on area of a circle, then twenty on volume of a cone. It’s tidy. It also turns out to be one of the less effective ways to practise.

The shuffled homework study

In 2007, Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor tested this with college students learning to solve maths problems. Some practised one problem type at a time, in blocks. Others got the same problems, randomly mixed together.

A week later, the students who had practised the shuffled set did, in the researchers’ words, “vastly superior” on the test. A second experiment found that spreading practice over several sessions also beat cramming it into one, by a “much greater” margin.

Blocked practice feels better while you do it. By the fifth circle problem, you’re flying. But you’re flying partly because you already know which method to use: it’s the same as last time. A real test, like real life, doesn’t tell you which method comes next. Mixed practice makes you choose, every time, and the choosing is a big part of the skill.

The name for it

Researchers call this interleaving. It’s one of the “desirable difficulties” described by the psychologist Robert Bjork: ways of practising that feel harder and slower in the moment but stick better over time. (Desirable difficulties will get their own post soon, because we’re like that.)

Why Ziggle’s daily set is a mixed bag

We could have built one puzzle and made you play it eight times. Instead the daily set jumps from finding words to recalling them, from deduction to planning, from logic to memory. You have to switch gears each time. Some days that switching is the hardest part of the puzzle.

The typing trainer does a small version of the same thing. Drills mix the keys you’re strong on with the one you’re working on, instead of hammering a single key forty times.

Try it on anything

  • Learning an instrument? Rotate between three pieces in a session instead of drilling one for an hour.
  • Revising? Shuffle your flashcard decks together.
  • Practising a sport? Mix shots or moves instead of repeating one until it’s boring.

It will feel messier. Your session-by-session scores might even dip. Check again in a week. That’s where interleaving shows its hand.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The two experiments, in the order they were run

The post describes the shuffled-homework study as one experiment followed by another. The 2007 paper in[3] Instructional Science reports them the other way round. Experiment 1 tested spacing, and Experiment 2 tested mixing. The two changes are the two differences between the usual textbook format and the shuffled one: spreading problems of one kind over several sessions, and mixing the kinds within a session.

Experiment 1: the same four problems, one week apart or together

Students learned to count the distinct orderings of a letter sequence with repeated letters, for example that abbccc can be arranged in 60 ways. A base-rate group of 50 students from the same pool was tested first, and none got any such problem right. Sixty-six students completed all three weekly sessions (an extra 14 started and did not return), and six were set aside because they got none of the first two practice problems right, leaving 60.

Two findings came out. Spacing helped: 74% against 49%. And doubling the massed practice did nothing: 49% for the Massers against 46% for the Light Massers, which the authors say shows no detectable gain from the “overlearning” strategy of working more problems right after you can do one. Practice scores did not differ between the groups (83% against 82% on the second pair of problems), so the benefit of spacing was invisible during practice.

Experiment 2: four formulas, mixed or blocked

Students learned to find the volume of four unfamiliar solids: a wedge, a spherical cone, a spheroid and a half cone, each with a formula that depends on a radius and a height. Both groups saw exactly the same tutorials and 16 practice problems in each of two weekly sessions, a week apart. The Blockers saw four problems about one solid after its tutorial and then moved on. The Mixers read all four tutorials first, and then got problems in an order that included each solid in every set of four. A test of eight new problems came a week later. Before the study, 25 students from the same pool had tried the eight test problems and none got one right.

The mix flipped the order. During practice the Blockers were far ahead, and a week later the Mixers were far ahead. The paper checked where the Blockers went wrong: in a count across all students and problems, the right formula but the wrong answer happened twice, once for a Mixer and once for a Blocker. So almost every failure was choosing, or remembering, the wrong formula. As the authors put it, blocked practice never made students decide which formula a problem needed.

The caveats the authors list

  • College students, and the effects might be weaker with much younger ones (a later classroom study with seventh-graders, covered in the post on mixed practice, found large effects).
  • A test made of problems like those practised, so it is not known whether the results hold for transfer.
  • Laboratory, not classroom.
  • Procedural tasks, not conceptual ones.
  • Small groups: Experiment 2 had 18 students who finished, and 15 more who started did not come back (computed: 15 of 33, or 45%). The authors say power was not a concern because the effects were large.

What the authors suggest changing

The shuffled format can be adopted without changing the lessons or their order, they say, though if a lesson is skipped the matching problems must be skipped too. They suggest textbooks include an index of the lesson behind every problem, so that a student stuck on a mixed problem can find the lesson. The best-known example of the shuffled format is the Saxon series of mathematics textbooks, according to the paper.

The twist

The Mixers’ practice scores were worst in the first session, 43% against 87% for the Blockers, and closer in the second, 78% against 91%. A week later they had passed the Blockers by 43 points, 63% to 20% (computed). A low score while practising was not a sign the method was failing.
