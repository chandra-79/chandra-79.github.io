A wandering mind: what 250,000 phone check-ins revealed

People’s minds wandered almost half the time they were awake, and they were less happy when they did.

Quick question: what are you thinking about right now? If it’s not this sentence, you’re in good company.

The study

In 2010, Harvard psychologists Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert built an iPhone app that pinged people at random moments during the day. Each time, it asked three things: How are you feeling? What are you doing? And are you thinking about what you’re doing, or about something else?

2,250 volunteers aged 18 to 88 took part, and the study collected around 250,000 answers.

What they found

  • People’s minds were wandering 46.9%[1] of their waking hours.
  • Minds wandered at least 30% of the time during every activity measured, with exactly one exception. (It was making love. The researchers had to ask.)
  • People were happiest while making love, exercising or talking with others, and least happy while resting, working or using a home computer.
  • Whether someone’s mind was wandering predicted how happy they were better than what they were doing: about 10.8%[1] of their momentary happiness, compared with 4.6% for the activity.
  • Looking at the timing, mind wandering seemed to come before unhappiness, not the other way round.

The headline the researchers chose was blunt: a wandering mind is an unhappy mind.

The nuance

Mind wandering isn’t all bad. Daydreaming is where plenty of ideas come from, and the study measured happiness in the moment, not creativity or planning. But it’s striking how often we’re not really where we are.

Why absorbing things feel good

Some activities hold attention without any effort to “focus”. A conversation you care about. A run. And, for a lot of people, a good puzzle. When you’re hunting for the one place a 7 can go, there isn’t much room left for replaying an awkward meeting.

We won’t claim Ziggle makes anyone happier. That would be a big claim from a small app. But we design the dailies to be the kind of thing your attention can rest on for five minutes: one clear goal, quick feedback, a challenge that fits.

Try this

Next time you notice your mind has wandered, don’t scold it. Just notice where it went, and come back. Noticing is the skill. The rest is practice, which you already know is our favourite word.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

How the study worked

The Harvard Gazette’s report describes the method[2]. The iPhone web app contacted 2,250 volunteers at random moments and asked three things: how happy they were, what they were doing, and whether they were thinking about what they were doing or about something else that was pleasant, neutral or unpleasant. They could choose from 22 general activities, such as walking, eating, shopping and watching television. The volunteers ranged in age from 18 to 88, from a range of backgrounds and occupations, and 74% were American. The authors gathered 250,000 data points. By 2010 more than 5,000 people were using the app. That is an average of about 111 answers a volunteer (computed).

  • 2,250volunteers, aged 18 to 88
  • 22everyday activities to choose from
  • 46.9%of the time the mind was elsewhere

The method is called experience sampling, and the Wikipedia article on mind-wandering explains why it matters: before it, much of the evidence came from lab tasks and questionnaires. The article records that the first documented studies date from the 1960s[3], with John Antrobus and Jerome Singer’s questionnaire, and that Wallas, in 1926, had already called mind-wandering part of the “incubation” stage of creative thought. The Harvard team’s point was that, in daily life, it is far more common than in a lab.

The numbers, worked through

The 10.8% and 4.6% are the surprising pair. They say that where the mind is, whether on the task or off it, accounted for more than twice as much of a person’s momentary happiness as what the person was doing. It does not say that either explains most of it: the large majority is elsewhere. The researchers also said that the time-lag analysis suggested mind-wandering was generally the cause, not the result, of unhappiness; the Wikipedia article is more careful, noting that unhappy moods can also cause mind-wandering, and that the time-lags suggest it can lead to negative moods too.

What the researchers took care to say

People’s thoughts did not wander to bad places by default. The Wikipedia article says the effect was somewhat offset by people’s tendency to wander to pleasant topics, but that unpleasant wandering was rated as more unpleasant than other activities. The Gazette quotes the authors’ summary: the ability to think about what is not happening is “a cognitive achievement that comes at an emotional cost”. Humans, unlike other animals, spend a great deal of time on events past, future or imaginary.

What else is known about a wandering mind

  • It is common and varies. The article says people typically spend 25–50% of their time on thoughts irrelevant to their situation, so 46.9% sits at the upper end of that range.
  • It slows what you take in. Studies of brain responses show that when thoughts are unrelated to the task the brain processes both task and other information in less detail, and the article gives driving as a place where it often happens, because driving becomes almost automatic.
  • It points forward. People tend to have more future-oriented than past-oriented thoughts when their minds wander.
  • It depends on the task. Reports of task-unrelated thoughts are less frequent when a task does not demand continuous use of working memory, and mind-wandering increases in harder readings, when readers have difficulty building a picture of the story.
  • It is not always a fault. It can be deliberate, and deliberate wandering is distinct from unwanted wandering. The article notes that studies of people with attention difficulties find the unplanned kind characteristic, but not the deliberate kind.

Where puzzles fit, and where they do not

The study measured 22 activities and none was a puzzle, so it says nothing about puzzles directly. What it does show is that people were happiest in conversation and exercise, and least happy resting, working or using a home computer, and that only one activity fell below 30% mind-wandering. The reading we draw is a modest one: a task with a clear goal, quick feedback and no spare room for a second thought is a plausible place for attention to rest, and the daily set is built to be that kind of task, finishable in a few minutes. We do not claim that puzzles make anyone happier. The study found a link between where the mind is and how people feel, in the moment, and nothing more.

The twist

The article that sets out the study’s headline, “a wandering mind is an unhappy mind”, also records the oldest link in the field running the other way: in 1926 Wallas treated mind-wandering as part of the second stage of creative thought, incubation. The same behaviour that felt worse at the moment may have been doing something useful. The study measured the first and could not measure the second.
