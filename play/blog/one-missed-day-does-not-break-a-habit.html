One missed day does not break a habit

The best study of real habit formation found that skipping a single day made almost no difference. That is why Ziggle’s streaks forgive.

Habits · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 16 September 2026

In this article 6 sections

The streak is the most powerful and the most brittle thing in an app. Seven days feels like momentum, and then one missed Tuesday erases it and makes you feel you have failed. So what does the research on real habits say about the missed Tuesday?

In the best-known study of habit formation, 96 volunteers[1] each picked one everyday behaviour (a piece of fruit with lunch, a 15-minute run before dinner) and did it in the same situation for 12 weeks, rating each day how automatic it felt. 82 gave enough days of data to analyse; a habit curve could be fitted for 62, and fitted well for 39.

What the numbers say

  • 96volunteers started
  • 82gave enough data
  • 39had a clean habit curve
  • 18–254days to reach 95% of their own plateau

The headline is the range: the time to reach 95% of a person’s own automaticity plateau ran from 18 to 254 days[1]. The study lasted only 84, so the long times are predictions. There is no “21 days”. And the sentence at the end of the abstract is the one worth framing: missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process[1].

The twist

What mattered was the overall consistency, not the unbroken chain. People who performed the behaviour more consistently had curves that fitted better, but a single slip left the curve where it was. A streak counter measures the chain; the habit lives in the average.

So Ziggle’s streak forgives

That is why a streak here can be rescued, why missing a day does not wipe your history, and why the 12-week calendar fills in with every day you played rather than only the consecutive ones. We are not claiming Ziggle forms any habit, or that it improves anything. We are saying a game should not punish you for a Tuesday.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

How much of life is habit?

A daily-experience study by the habit researcher Wendy Wood and colleagues in 2002 found that about 43% of daily behaviours[2] are performed out of habit. Habits form because, when you repeat an action in a consistent context, the link between the context and the action strengthens a little each time. The 12-week study in this post is the best look we have at how long that takes: an average of 66 days[2] to reach the plateau of automaticity, with a range of 18 to 254 days (9.4 weeks on average, and the slowest took 36; computed). Since the study ran for 84 days, the average person reached the plateau only about four-fifths of the way through it (66 ÷ 84 = 79%).

Rewards matter in a specific way: intermittent or uncertain rewards have been found to be particularly effective in promoting habit learning. That is also why we do not claim a streak is magic. It is one kind of reward, and the study says that missing one day does not break the machinery.

Why “keystone” habits matter

Some habits are called keystone habits because they trigger the formation of others. A short daily routine that you reliably keep may be worth more than the thing it contains, because it is the one that is anchored to a cue. A habit can also work against a goal: when a habit prevails over a conscious goal, the result is called a capture error. William James’s line sums it up: living creatures are bundles of habits.

The twist

A popular rule of thumb says a habit takes 21 days. The study that is usually cited in support of habit formation found an average of 66, with some people at 18 and one fitted at 254. The honest number is a range, and the most helpful thing a streak can do is not to punish you for sitting at the long end of it.
