Level 3 A deeper dive into Hailstone numbers: a simple rule nobody can prove

THE ZIGGLE BLOG

The 44 numbers below a million that set a Collatz record

Most starting numbers fall to 1 in a few dozen steps. Forty-four of the first million each took longer than every number before it, up to 524 steps.

In the main article we saw that 27 takes 111 steps to reach 1 and that 871 is the longest journey below 1,000. A natural next question is how the longest journey grows. We ran the Collatz rule on every start below one million and noted each number that took more steps than everything before it: a record holder.

44 records in a million numbers

There are 43 of them after the trivial start at 1 (which takes no steps). The list matches the published sequence OEIS A006877[1] exactly.

What the records show

The champion is 837,799, at 524 steps. That is about 4.7 times the 111 steps of 27, for a number 31,000 times larger. Journeys grow far more slowly than the numbers themselves: a number 31,000 times bigger takes under five times as many steps.

Gaps between records also widen. Between 2,919 and 3,711 there are 792 starts; between 626,331 and 837,799 there are over 200,000. Each new record is rarer than the last, which is why the search for a record has to cover enormous ranges.

The twist

The record holders are the exceptions. Across all 999,999 starts below a million, the average journey is 131.4 steps and the median is 125; 87.4% finish in under 200 steps, and only 110 starts take more than 400. The champion, 837,799, takes four times the average and, on the way, climbs to 2,974,984,576. The hailstones that stay in the cloud longest are rare.

Checking the claim

Everything above was computed here and compared with the encyclopedia of integer sequences. If you want to reproduce it: for each n, apply the rule until you reach 1, counting steps, and keep the n that beat the earlier maximum. A few lines of any language will do, and a million starts takes a second or so.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What is known about the rule

Wikipedia’s article on the conjecture says[3] Lothar Collatz introduced the idea in 1937, two years after his doctorate, and lists the other names it goes by: the 3n + 1 problem, the Ulam conjecture, Kakutani’s problem, the Thwaites conjecture, Hasse’s algorithm and the Syracuse problem. Computers have checked every start up to 271, about 2.36 × 1021, and all of them reach 1. The article is careful to add that this is not a proof, because counterexamples can hide among very large numbers, as with the disproven Pólya and Mertens conjectures.

Two real results sit behind the evidence. Riho Terras proved that almost every positive integer has a finite stopping time, meaning its sequence drops below its start, and in 2019 Terence Tao strengthened that to say almost all orbits descend below any function of the start that grows without bound, however slowly. A heuristic explains the pull downward: counting only the odd numbers in a sequence, each is on average three quarters of the one before. The only cycle anyone knows is the trivial one, written (1, 2) in the shortcut form of the rule that the article uses. Paul Erdős said of the problem: “Mathematics may not be ready for such problems.”

The records at every power of ten

Our arithmetic from that table: each extra digit adds 37 to 264 steps, with no steady rhythm, and the leap from 108 to 109 adds only 37 while 106 to 107 adds 161. A tenfold bigger search range does not promise a proportionally longer record.

Where all the other starts land

The crowd is thickest between 50 and 150 steps (the mean is 131.4 and the median 125, computed) and the right-hand tail is thin. The 110 starts that take more than 400 steps are one in every 9,091 (computed), and the champion, at 524, is alone at the very end.

Longer journeys for bigger starts, but slowly

That is the shape the record table hides. The records grow unevenly, but the average climbs by a fairly steady amount for every extra digit, which is in keeping with the heuristic of a long random walk with a gentle downward tilt (our reading).

How high do the hailstones climb?

The peak champion below a million is not the step champion. 704,511 reaches 56,991,483,520, about 80,895 times its own size, in just 242 steps, against 524 for 837,799. By contrast 837,799 itself peaks at 2,974,984,576, about 3,551 times its start. Elsewhere, 143,290 of the starts from 2 to 999,999 (14.3%) never climb above where they began, and 1,529 climb to more than a thousand times their start.

Two halvings for every tripling

The article notes that for almost all 2-adic starting values (a way of extending the rule to infinite binary expansions) there are two division steps for every multiplication step. Our count for the champion is in that region: of its 524 steps, 195 are 3n + 1 moves and 329 are halvings, so the 3n + 1 moves are 37.2% of the steps. Across all starts the average share is 31.9% (computed), a little under a third.

The twist

The list of record holders is found by running the rule on every start in turn, and the step counts do not grow in a pattern anyone can see in the table: 524 at 837,799, then 685 at 8,400,511 and 949 at 63,728,127. A problem a child can state hides a list of numbers that is found by looking.
