The magical number was probably four, not seven

George Miller said an integer had been persecuting him. A later review suggests he had the number a little high, and that he knew it.

Memory · The Ziggle team · 5-minute read · 27 July 2026

In 1956 the psychologist George Miller opened a paper with one of the best first lines in science: “My problem is that I have been persecuted by an integer.”[1] The integer was seven. People seemed to hold about seven things in mind at once, give or take two, whether they were digits, letters or musical notes. “Seven plus or minus two” went on to become one of the most quoted numbers in psychology.

A rough estimate, not a law

In 2001 Nelson Cowan went back through the evidence in Behavioral and Brain Sciences. His abstract points out that Miller’s seven was “meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit”[2], and that when you stop people from grouping items into chunks, the limit looks much smaller: about four chunks[2] (other researchers had suggested three to five).

  • 7 ± 2Miller, 1956: a rough, rhetorical estimate
  • ≈ 4Cowan, 2001: the limit when chunking is controlled
  • 3–5the range other researchers had suggested

So why did seven feel right?

Because we are masters of chunking. Try to remember 2 0 2 6 1 1 2 3. That is eight digits, but if you see “2026” and “1123” it is two chunks, and nobody struggles with two. The seven in Miller’s data was partly a measure of how cleverly people regroup things, not how many raw items fit. A chess master looks at a board and sees a handful of familiar patterns instead of twenty-five pieces; we wrote about that.

The twist

The practical lesson is nearly the opposite of the popular one. “You can hold seven things” invites you to cram seven items into a screen or a menu. “You can hold about four chunks, and chunks are something you build” tells you to group things, name groups, and give people patterns to recognise. It is why a phone number comes in blocks and why Sudoku boxes are outlined.

Where this shows up in Ziggle

Memory Mischief starts with a few friends and adds more as you go, so the early rounds sit comfortably inside a four-chunk limit. Letter Lock’s colours let you carry a handful of constraints instead of a list of letters. In the memory palace trainer you learn to turn a sequence into a few vivid rooms, which is chunking on purpose.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What Miller actually measured

Miller’s paper joined two lines of research that had looked unrelated. In an absolute-judgement task, a person hears (say) ten tones that differ only in pitch and has to name each one. Performance tops out at a surprisingly low channel capacity of about two to three bits of information[3], which is the ability to tell four to eight alternatives apart. Memory span, the longest list someone can repeat back in order on half of the trials, came out at about seven. The two limits were close, and the coincidence is what the paper is about.

The detail that made the paper famous is the one that shows its limits. A span of about seven items held, he noticed, whether the items carried very different amounts of information: binary digits carry 1 bit each, decimal digits 3.32 bits (computed: log2 10), words about 10 bits. If the limit were counted in bits, a span of seven binary digits (7 bits) and seven words (70 bits) would be wildly different. What was being counted looked like items, which later work called chunks, not bits.

Why even four is not a constant

Later work found that memory span is not fixed even when it is counted in chunks. It depends on the type of material: span depends on whether the items are words a person knows[3], for instance. And a chunk is only a chunk if the information stored in it can be taken apart again into the data it stands for. Several other factors also affect a person’s measured span, which makes it hard to pin the capacity of short-term memory to a single number of chunks. This is why we say the magical number was probably four.

And a number you can see

There is a related limit that you do not need to memorise at all. Subitizing is the ability to know how many items there are at a glance, without counting; it is how you know three cups from four without counting. The film Rain Man showed a savant[3] apparently subitizing the toothpicks from a spilled box, and neuropsychologist Oliver Sacks reported a similar feat in his 1985 book. The cases are famous because they apparently go far beyond four objects.

The twist

The paper is often read as saying that human short-term memory holds seven plus or minus two things. Its own argument was about something stranger: a coincidence between two different limits, one of judgement and one of memory. The famous number was a clue in a puzzle, and later research showed that the puzzle did not have a single answer.
