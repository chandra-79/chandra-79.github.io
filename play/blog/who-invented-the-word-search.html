Who invented the word search?

A small-town Oklahoma newsletter, a Spanish puzzle writer, and a game that looks simpler than it is.

Origins · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 9 January 2026

The word search feels as if it has always existed. In fact it has a fairly recent and slightly disputed birthday.

The usual story credits Norman E. Gibat, who published a word search in the Selenby Digest, a free advertising newsletter in Norman, Oklahoma, on 1 March 1968[1]. Teachers reportedly asked for copies for their classes, and the format spread from there into newspapers and puzzle books.

There is a rival claim. In Spain, the puzzle writer Pedro Ocón de Oro is credited with publishing similar “sopa de letras” (letter soup) puzzles in the 1960s. Puzzles are like that: good ideas get found more than once.

Why it is harder than it looks

A word search is a test of scanning. Your eyes sweep the grid looking for a first letter, then check its neighbours for the second. Easy puzzles keep words running across and down, so the sweep is predictable. Once words can run backwards and on the diagonal, there are eight directions to check from every letter, and the job gets much bigger.

There is also a familiarity trap. If the same words keep coming back, you stop searching and start remembering where they usually hide.

How Ziggle’s word search grows

Easy uses a small grid and runs across and down only. Medium and hard draw from much bigger lists of longer, less familiar words, so a theme rarely repeats a word from one puzzle to the next. Hard adds decoys too: the opening letters of a real answer planted somewhere else, so a promising start is not proof you have found it.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

A birthday with more than one candle

The Wikipedia article gives the first published word search as Norman E. Gibat’s, in the Selenby Digest of Norman, Oklahoma, on 1 March 1968, and notes that the Spanish puzzle writer Pedro Ocón de Oro was publishing “sopas de letras” (letter soups) before that date. It also records a third claim, from Illinois, that a James Patrick Carr published similar puzzles under the title “Slate R Straight” earlier still. That one comes with no source we could check, so we leave it as a rumour. The article does say how the format spread: teachers in the Norman schools asked for reprints, one teacher mailed copies to friends in other schools around the country, and one of those scattered copies probably led someone to sell the idea to a syndicator.

The puzzle has been sold as WordSeek, WordFind, WonderWord, word sleuth and mystery word puzzle, and it was good enough for television: Now You See It, a CBS game show of the mid-1970s, was a made-for-TV version of a word search.

How the strategies work

The advice in the article is what most solvers work out by themselves, and each tip leans on a fact about how eyes search:

  • Start from the first letter. Sweep the grid for it, then check the eight letters around each hit for the second letter.
  • Hunt for the outsiders. Most puzzles use capitals, so Q, J, X and Z stand out. Words containing one are quick to find.
  • Look for doubles. Two identical letters side by side are easier to spot than two different ones, so a word like “ball” or “coffee” is a better lead than its other letters.
  • With no word list, go row by row, reading each row both ways, then each column, and so on.
  • Use the generator’s habits. Computer-made puzzles tend to place words in patterns, so after one word turns up, the neighbouring rows, columns or diagonals often hold another.

Twists on the grid

Publishers have long hidden extras in the puzzle. In one version, the words written backwards spell a secret message; in another, the letters that are not used in any word do. Kappa Publishing is known for this, and its magazines were sold as “the magazine with the last message”. Others hide a trivia question’s answer as an extra word not on the list. The hardest family is the snaking puzzle, where a word may bend at 90° at any letter, or even at 45°, and may reuse a letter. Some are laid out to trace a shape such as a square or a donut.

Word searches are also a classroom staple, especially for teaching English as a second language, and the article notes they are a good way to demonstrate searching algorithms.

The twist

The article credits app versions with keeping the word search among the most popular kinds of phone game. The same computers that make the puzzles also leave fingerprints: generated grids tend to put words in patterns, which is why one found word can point you to the next.
