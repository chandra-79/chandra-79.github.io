Show me first: the case for worked examples

For beginners, studying a solved example can beat solving problems cold. For experts, it can get in the way. Ziggle tries to do both.

Training design · The Ziggle team · 4-minute read · 18 September 2026

In the 1980s, the educational psychologist John Sweller and colleagues studied how people learn algebra and similar skills. They found that beginners often learned more from studying fully worked examples than from solving the same number of problems on their own. This became known as the worked-example effect.

The reason, in Sweller’s cognitive load theory, is that a novice solving a problem cold spends most of their mental effort searching for any route at all, leaving little capacity to notice why a route works. A worked example frees that capacity.

The twist: experts are different

Later research found the expertise reversal effect: once you know a skill well, worked examples can become redundant and even slow you down. Experts learn more from problems.

How Ziggle uses both

  • “Show me” in the cube trainer plays an algorithm on the cube while you watch, then lets you try. As you get better, you use it less.
  • Hints that teach: a Sum Spark hint builds on the chain you have started rather than giving the answer, and a Word Cloud reveals one clue cloud at a time.
  • After a miss, games show one way to the answer, so a loss becomes an example to study.

The help is there when you are new and quietly out of the way when you are not.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

The experiment behind the idea

The worked-example effect is one of the effects predicted by cognitive load theory, and Sweller calls it the best known and most widely studied of them all. A worked example is a step-by-step demonstration of how to do a task or solve a problem: it states the problem, shows the steps and gives the answer. The Wikipedia article says Sweller and Cooper were not the first to teach this way, but they were the first to describe it from a cognitive-load point of view. While studying problem-solving tactics, they used worked examples as a substitute for ordinary problem-solving practice with students learning algebra, and found that the students who studied examples did significantly better than those who solved problems themselves[2].

The explanation is about where mental effort goes. Cognitive load theory splits it into three parts: an intrinsic load that comes with the material and cannot be removed, an extraneous load caused by poor presentation or aimless searching, and a germane load that goes into building understanding. For a beginner with no stored solution patterns, solving cold is mostly search, which is extraneous. A worked example takes the search away, and prompts for self-explanation can turn the saved effort into germane load.

What makes an example work

The article collects several findings about how to build one:

  • Pairs and series beat single examples. Sweller and Cooper used example-and-problem pairs, and Renkl suggests that a series of examples is more effective than one.
  • Small steps between stages. Pillay found that examples showing three intermediate problem stages worked better than showing one[2], provided the distance between stages was small enough for students to connect them without inventing their own links.
  • Self-explanation, with a nudge. Successful learners studied examples for longer and explained them more actively to themselves, but most learners are passive and superficial self-explainers, so they should be guided.
  • Keep text and diagram together. Chandler and Sweller found that integrating text into the diagram reduces extraneous load, which they called the split-attention effect. Tabbers and colleagues suggested presenting the text as audio instead.
  • Faded examples. Renkl recommends sequences of examples in which worked steps are removed one at a time, so the learner moves gradually from studying to solving.

Where it stops working

The effect is not universal, and the article is open about that. Ward and Sweller found conditions where worked examples were no more effective, and possibly less effective, than solving problems. Quilici and Mayer found that giving three examples of each problem type did no better than one at teaching students to sort later problems into types. Paas found that students given a half-worked problem, a “completion” task, did as well on later problems as those given fully worked ones. And because an example shows steps, it only suits domains where steps can be written down, such as mathematics, physics and programming. For interpreting a poem, or for resolving a conflict, the article says worked examples may not be the best method.

The reversal

The expertise reversal effect says that a technique that helps novices can stop helping, or even hurt, as they learn. Kalyuga and colleagues showed it in three studies of on-screen diagrams in mechanical engineering, relay circuits and programming logic: the advantage of worked examples disappeared and often turned negative for more knowledgeable learners[3]. The explanation is the same cognitive load story turned round. An expert already holds the solution patterns, so an example that spells out the steps is redundant, and fitting it together with what they know adds load instead of removing it.

The article lists other pairs that reverse in the same way. Learners with little knowledge do better studying material than imagining it, and experts do better imagining it. Novices benefit from integrating text and diagram, and experts do better when one of the two is simply dropped. Novices gain from animations broken into segments, and in one study experts did not. It is an example of an “aptitude by treatment interaction”: the same teaching has different effects on different people.

Two caveats, both from the article. Many of the studies measure cognitive load by asking people to rate how difficult a task felt, on a scale such as 1 (extremely easy) to 7 (extremely difficult), and some researchers doubt that such ratings measure load accurately. And the effect has also been found in studies outside the cognitive-load tradition, so other explanations, including motivation, remain possible. There is a counter-example too: Nievelstein and colleagues (2013) used law cases, a less structured task, and found worked examples helped advanced law students as much as novices.

Fading, on a person’s own schedule

The remedy the articles describe is fading: removing worked steps as the learner improves. Fixed fading, with the points chosen in advance, already did better than plain problem-solving. Adaptive fading, which removes steps in response to what a learner has shown they understand, did better still, and the article notes that it needs software that keeps assessing the learner.

Ziggle does a simpler version, because it leaves the fading to you. The cube trainer’s “Show me” plays a sequence of turns on the cube and then lets you try, and you use it less as you get better. A Sum Spark hint builds on the chain you have started and does not hand over the answer, which is close to the article’s completion problem. A Word Cloud reveals one clue cloud at a time. After a miss the game shows one way to the answer, so the loss becomes an example to study. None of this measures your expertise for you, and we would rather it did not: you are the best judge of when the help has become redundant.

The twist

The article’s completion finding is the quiet hero here. Half-worked problems did as well as fully worked ones, so a hint that stops partway, and leaves the last step to you, may be as good a teacher as a full answer and keeps you in the game.
