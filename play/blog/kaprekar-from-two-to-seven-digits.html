Kaprekar’s routine from two to seven digits: what happens to each length

Four digits give one constant. We ran the routine on every number from two to seven digits long, 10 million starts in all, and the endings are far stranger.

The four-digit routine always lands on 6174. Does a similar thing happen with other lengths? We ran the routine (sort the digits down and up, subtract, repeat) on every start with at least two different digits, for lengths from 2 to 7, and recorded where each ends up.

Reading the table

Two digits end in a loop of five numbers (9 → 81 → 63 → 27 → 45 → 9 and round again), never at a single number. Three digits all reach 495. Four digits all reach 6174. After that the pattern breaks: five digits split into three loops of different sizes, six digits have two fixed points (549945, reached from only 1,950 starts, and 631764) plus a loop of seven numbers that most starts fall into, and seven digits all end in a single loop of eight.

The twist

The two famous numbers are special in a precise way. In 1981 G. D. Prichett and colleagues showed that in base 10 the Kaprekar constants are limited to two numbers, 495 (three digits) and 6174 (four)[1]. In our table those are the two lengths where every start reaches the same single number; six digits have fixed points too, but only a small share of starts find them. The charm of 6174 is that it is one of exactly two.

Try it yourself

Pick any five-digit number, say 12345. The routine goes 54321 − 12345 = 41976, then 97641 − 14679 = 82962, which belongs to the loop 61974 → 63954 → 75933 → 82962. You will meet one of three loops and then circle forever.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

What the routine is and who it is named for

Wikipedia’s article on Kaprekar’s routine names it[3] after the Indian mathematician D. R. Kaprekar and gives the standard example: start with 8991 in base 10 and take 9981 − 1899 = 8082, then 8820 − 288 = 8532, then 8532 − 2358 = 6174. The routine works in any number base, and the article states that any four-digit number with at least two distinct digits reaches 6174 within seven iterations.

One structural fact explains a lot: the two numbers subtracted always have the same digit sum, so they have the same remainder on division by 9, and after the first step every number in a base-10 sequence is a multiple of 9. 6174, for instance, is 9 × 686.

The four-digit journey, step by step

The article says seven is the longest, and we reproduce that. For three digits the longest journey to 495 is six steps (54 of the 990 starts take that long).

Every length from 2 to 13 digits

The earlier table in the post is confirmed for two to seven digits. Beyond that the picture gets richer. Eight digits split four ways, into a loop of 7 (48.8%), a loop of 3 (48.2%) and two fixed points. Nine digits fall almost entirely, 94.9%, into a loop of 14. By ten digits there are eight different endings, and by twelve there are sixteen.

Fixed points, a family and a check

The fixed points our search found, by number of digits, are 495 (3); 6174 (4); 631764 and 549945 (6); 97508421 and 63317664 (8); 864197532 and 554999445 (9). From ten to thirteen digits the numbers of fixed points among the endings are 3 for 10, 1 for 11, 5 for 12, 1 for 13. 6174, 631764 and 63317664 belong to the family Wikipedia describes: 6174, 631764, 63317664 and so on, a 6, a block of 3s, 17, a block of 6s and a 4, with the two blocks the same length.

Wikipedia also records Iwasaki’s 2024 classification of all the fixed points, which implies there are none for one, two, five or seven digits. Our table agrees: no fixed point appears for 2, 5 or 7 digits. The nine-digit 864197532 is what the routine does to 123456789 (987654321 − 123456789).

Who proved what

  1. 1981Prichett and colleagues show that, in base 10, the constants are limited to 495 and 6174.
  2. 2005Y. Hirata calculates all fixed points up to 31 decimal digits.
  3. 2024Haruo Iwasaki classifies all the decimal fixed points into five disjoint sets.
The twist

The result everyone remembers is the rare tidy one. In our table only four of the twelve lengths, 2, 3, 4 and 7 digits, send every start to a single ending, and only two of those, 3 and 4, end at a single number. The tidy cases are the exception: the other eight lengths split between 3 and 16 different endings.
