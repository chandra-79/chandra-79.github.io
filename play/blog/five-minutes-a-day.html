Five minutes a day: how small habits actually stick

The “21 days to a habit” rule is made up. Here’s what the studies found, and why your streak has freezes.

Somewhere, somebody once said it takes 21 days to form a habit, and the internet has repeated it ever since. It sounds like science. It has the confident look of a number. It is not, as far as anyone can find, based on any study at all.

Here’s one that is.

The 66-day study

In a study published in 2010, Phillippa Lally and her team at University College London asked 96 volunteers[1] to pick one new daily habit and stick to it for 12 weeks. People chose things like eating a piece of fruit with lunch, going for a 15-minute run before dinner, or doing 50 sit-ups after their morning coffee. Every day they rated how automatic it felt. 82 of them gave enough days of data to analyse.

The study’s typical figure was 66 days[1] for a habit to feel as automatic as it was ever going to get. The range was enormous: one person got there in 18 days, and for the slowest the researchers predicted 254 (that’s a long time to be doing sit-ups on purpose). The study lasted 84 days, so the longer times are predictions from each person’s curve, and the curve fitted well for only 39 of the 82 people. The honest summary is “somewhere from a few weeks to most of a year”.

And here’s our favourite finding: missing a single day made no real difference. People who skipped once carried on building the habit as if nothing had happened. What hurt was missing lots of days, which slowly lowered how automatic the habit ever became.

That’s exactly why Ziggle’s streak comes with freezes. Every seven-day run earns one, you can bank two, and a missed day quietly uses one up. A habit that falls apart because you had the flu isn’t much of a habit. Guilt doesn’t build habits. Repetition does.

The one trick that has real evidence

Psychologists call it an implementation intention, which is a fancy name for an if-then plan. Instead of “I’ll do the puzzle every day”, you decide: “If I pour my first coffee, then I open Ziggle.”

It sounds almost too simple. But when Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran pooled 94 tests of the idea in 2006, the average effect on actually reaching goals was d = 0.65[2], which researchers count as a medium-to-large effect. That’s a strong result for something you can set up in ten seconds.

The trick is the anchor. Pick something you already do every day without thinking (coffee, the train, brushing your teeth) and hang the new habit on it. Your old habit does the remembering for you.

Why Mondays feel like a fresh start

Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis noticed that people start things at “temporal landmarks”. Google searches for “diet” jump at the start of a week, a month and a year. Gym visits rise at the start of a week, a new semester and just after a birthday.

In one experiment, people were asked when they’d like a reminder to start a goal. When March 20 was described as “the first day of spring”, 26% chose it[3]. When the same date was just an ordinary Thursday, only 7% did. Same day, new label, more than three times the takers.

Ziggle uses this on purpose. Grand Slam weeks open each quarter with double points, Cup weeks land mid-month, and Mondays start a fresh league week. If your streak broke, the next landmark is never far away.

Our five-minute recipe

  1. Shrink it. One daily is enough. All eight is a perfect day, not a requirement.
  2. Anchor it. Write your if-then plan somewhere you’ll see it. “If I sit down on the train, then I play Letter Lock.”
  3. Never miss twice. One missed day doesn’t matter, which is what the freezes are for. Two in a row is where habits start to leak.
  4. Make it visible. The 12-week calendar on the home page fills in as you go. A row of squares is oddly motivating.
  5. Use the landmarks. Fell off? Restart on a Monday, the first of the month, or the next Grand Slam week. Your brain will help.

And if it takes you 254 days? That’s fine too. Nobody is timing you except you.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Inside the “first day of spring” experiment

The 26% versus 7% figure comes from one small, neat experiment in Dai, Milkman and Riis’s 2015 paper. They invited 400 people[3] to take a survey and offered a goal reminder; 165 signed up, and were randomly given one of two lists of seven dates, from 18 to 24 March 2014, to choose when to be reminded. The lists were identical except for one label. In the new-beginning condition, 20 March was called “The First Day of Spring 2014”; in the control condition it was “The Third Thursday in March 2014”.

A separate group of 141 people[3] rated how much each label felt like a new beginning on a 1 to 7 scale. “First day of spring” scored 3.40[3] and “Third Thursday” 1.55, so the label did what it was meant to do.

The fresh-start effect in three data sets

The experiment in the post is the small one.[4] The same authors’ larger paper, in Management Science in 2014, uses three archival data sets. For Google searches it takes the daily US volume of searches for “diet” over 3,104 days, from January 2004 to June 2012, scaled against all searches. For exercise it takes a university’s gym attendance records. For commitments it uses goals set on the website stickK. Searching and visiting both rise after temporal landmarks, and the authors propose the cause is mental accounting: a landmark starts a new period, which relegates past lapses to the previous one.

The largest single figure is the first-of-January jump in dieting searches, 82.1%, and the largest for actual behaviour is the start of a new semester, 47.1%. The paper also checks the alternative that people simply eat more on weekends and holidays, and finds that does not account for the pattern.

What an if-then plan is measured against

The comparison in the 94-study review is with a goal intention alone[5], “I intend to do X”, and the added benefit of specifying when and where is the 0.65. A later chapter by Parks-Stamm and Gollwitzer repeats the figure as covering over 8,000 participants in 94 independent studies. In other words the 0.65 is not “plans against nothing” but “plans against good intentions”, which is the harder comparison.

What the freezes do, simulated

The post says Ziggle’s streak earns a freeze for every seven-day run, banks at most two, and spends one on a missed day. We replayed that rule (the same logic as the streak code) for 20,000 imaginary players who each have the same chance of playing on any given day over 100 days, first with the freezes and then without them.

Our reading: freezes matter most for the reliable-but-human player. At a 95% chance a day, a freeze saves the streak for 87% of players, and the average streak is 77.4 days instead of 19.9. At 70% the figure is 9%. This fits the habit research: one miss is forgiven and a pattern of misses is not.

The twist

The calendar date was the same day in both conditions; only the story changed. And in both groups the most popular choice of all was the last date on the list (24 March), so people also like to wait. A fresh start can pull a goal forward, but procrastination pulls the other way, and the experiment shows both in one table.

If-then plans, in numbers

The other pillar of this post, the “if-then” plan, rests on Gollwitzer and Sheeran’s 2006 meta-analysis of 94 independent tests[2], which found a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65). The pattern of the plan matters: you specify the situation in advance (“when I finish my coffee, I do the daily puzzle”) so the cue triggers the action without a fresh decision each time.
