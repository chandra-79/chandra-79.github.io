Why the last stamp feels urgent

Coffee drinkers bought faster as they got closer to a free cup, and a card with two “free” stamps got finished sooner.

The idea is old. Early psychologists proposed that animals speed up as they get closer to a reward, and called it the goal gradient. In 2006, three researchers at Columbia wondered whether people with loyalty cards were any different. (Spoiler: not very.)

The coffee card study

Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky and Yuhuang Zheng studied a real café reward scheme: buy ten coffees, get one free. As customers got closer to their free coffee, they bought coffee more often. The finish line pulled them in.

Then came the clever part. Some customers got a normal 10-stamp card. Others got a 12-stamp card with two stamps already filled in. Both groups needed to buy exactly ten coffees. But the people who started with “progress” on their card finished their ten purchases faster.

The researchers found the same pattern online. People rating songs to earn rewards visited more often, rated more songs per visit and stuck around longer as they got closer to the goal.

Why it works

Effort seems to depend on how much of the distance is left, not how far you’ve come. Ten out of ten to go feels like a long way. Ten out of twelve to go feels like you’re already on your way. The finish line looks closer, so you walk faster.

How Ziggle uses this (and how it doesn’t)

We show progress clearly because it genuinely helps: a tick for each of the eight dailies, “4 of 8 done” on the home page, levels in the trainers, trophies that fill in. When you’re one daily away from a perfect day, you’ll feel the pull. That’s the goal gradient, and it’s a nice feeling.

What we won’t do is fake progress or punish you for stopping. There are no pre-filled stamps to trick you into starting, no red warnings that your streak is about to “die”, no countdown timers designed to make you anxious. Freezes cover a missed day automatically. A habit built on worry tends to snap the moment life gets busy.

Use it on yourself

  • Break big goals into visible steps. “Read 12 books” becomes 12 boxes to tick.
  • Start the card. Do the first tiny step right now, so you’re never starting from zero.
  • Put the finish line where you can see it. A paper calendar on the fridge works brilliantly.

The deeper read

That was the short version. What follows goes further, with each fact read in its source.

Older than loyalty cards: rats in a runway

The full text of the 2006 paper in the[2] Journal of Marketing Research credits the idea to the behaviourist Clark Hull, who formulated it in 1932, with a refinement by Miller in 1944. In a classic experiment Hull measured how long rats took to cross each of several six-foot sections of a straight runway, using electrical contacts, and found they ran faster the closer they were to the food. In another, rats held back near or far from food pulled harder when they were close to it. The paper notes that most goal gradients had been found in rats and in physical responses such as running speed. Kivetz and Urminsky were at Columbia Business School and Zheng at Fordham.

The café, in numbers

  • 949completed ten-stamp cards
  • ~10,000coffee purchases tracked
  • 24.6 daysaverage time to fill a card
  • 29.4 daystime at the first rate (computed by the authors)

The study used the real reward scheme of a café on a university campus: ten purchases earned a free coffee, one stamp per visit. The stamps were numbered in sequence, unknown to customers, so the researchers could track each card. Because only completed cards were handed in, they also bought back 73 incomplete cards for $4 each and a 1% chance of $100, and ran a control group of 42 customers with unredeemable cards, paid $5 to join and $15 after six weeks regardless of how much coffee they bought.

Between the first and last purchase on a card, the time to the next coffee fell by 0.7 days, an acceleration of 20%. The average card took 24.6 days to complete, against 29.4 at the speed of the first purchase, a difference of nearly five days (16%). The authors then fitted a hazard model on 29,076 day-by-day rows and found the effect held after controls for time trends and for differences between customers.

After the reward the gradient resets

People sped up towards the first reward, dropped back to their old pace right after it and then sped up again. A plain upward trend in coffee drinking could not do that.

The 12-stamp card, with the numbers

For the experiment with “illusory progress”, 108 customers were randomly given either a ten-stamp card or a twelve-stamp card with two stamps already filled in, described as an offer to everyone who joined. Both needed ten purchases. The first group finished in 15.6 days on average and the second in 12.7, nearly three days faster, which the paper rounds to 20% (our arithmetic: 2.9 days is 19% of 15.6). The medians were 15 days and 10 days.

Both cards need the same ten purchases, so the absolute distance is identical. Only the share of the distance already travelled differs, and that is the only thing that could explain the faster finish.

Ruling out the obvious explanations

The authors tested the rivals with questionnaires of travellers in a train station. A first group of 65 imagined a pizza-chain card needing eight purchases, with or without two free bonus stamps on a ten-stamp card; those with the bonus stamps guessed they would finish in 11 weeks on average against 16 (a difference the paper reports as marginal, p < .1). The likelihood of joining did not differ (5.9 against 5.8 on an 11-point scale), so the effect was not about liking the programme more. A second group of 118 compared the bonus card, the plain card and a card where the two stamps had been earned by real purchases, and how bad people felt about losing it. Losing the earned-stamp card felt worse (2.1) than losing the bonus-stamp card or the control (1.7 for both). The bonus-stamp card was no harder to lose than the plain one, which the paper takes as evidence against a sunk-cost account.

A second setting: rating songs

The paper also analysed a music-rating website. Members who rated 51 songs earned a $25 gift certificate. Each song took about four minutes to rate on roughly 50 scales (our arithmetic: 51 songs at four minutes each is 204 minutes, about 3.4 hours of rating per certificate). The data covered 148 members who rated 14,866 songs in 472 website visits. Members rated more songs per visit when closer to the reward, with a diminishing rate of speed-up.

Faster to the first reward, more likely to come back

The authors also report that members who accelerated more strongly towards their first reward were more likely to earn a second one and to return sooner. They suggest a steeper gradient reflects stronger motivation, so the card tells the shop something about its customers.

The twist

The bonus stamps were a gift with nothing behind them: ten purchases were needed either way. The people who got them finished nearly three days sooner, and in the questionnaire version the bonus stamps did not make people more likely to join. The paper’s own title calls the effect “illusionary goal progress”, and Ziggle’s promise not to use it is a design choice the research makes you notice, not one it makes for you.
